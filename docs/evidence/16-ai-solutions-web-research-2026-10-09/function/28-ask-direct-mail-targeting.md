There is credible evidence for machine-learning and data-driven household selection, but the public results are uneven: several vendors report useful performance figures, while few publish enough detail to confirm a rigorous, randomized mail-versus-no-mail holdout.

| Vendor / example | Publicly reported result | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|
| **Epsilon** | Says clients see an average **6%–8% lift in direct-mail performance** from machine-learning modeling. It also reports AI-powered segments delivering **1.5×–3.9× higher incremental lift than campaign average** when assessed with holdouts. A nonprofit case reports **31% higher campaign ROI** after suppressing lower-performing names.[4] | The clearest published claims that combine predictive targeting with holdout-based incrementality, though the cited material does not identify the mid-market verticals in the question. |
| **Path2Response — home improvement** | A home-improvement client using website behavior and audience targeting reported **4% overall response lift from August to September**.[6] | Relevant to home services, but the result is a month-to-month comparison—not clearly a randomized holdout. |
| **Path2Response — recent site visitors** | A client’s recent site visitors generated a **15% response rate versus a historical 5% campaign average**, or **3× the prior rate**.[9] | Strong response-rate evidence for scoring and targeting; the public case does not show a control group, so it should not be presented as incremental lift. |
| **Path2Response — collectibles** | A custom response model was used to identify likely new coin-collector responders; the vendor says the test exceeded benchmarks, but the public summary gives no quantified lift.[1] | Shows the modeling approach, but not enough public numbers to estimate impact. |
| **PostPilot — retail** | Mizzen+Main reports **6.35× incremental ROAS** for automated winbacks validated with holdout testing, plus six figures in additional annual revenue. Other campaign ROAS figures are also reported, but are not all identified as holdout-tested.[2] | A useful example of holdout-tested direct-mail economics in retail; it is not evidence of response-model performance specifically. |

**Bottom line:** The strongest quantified public case for *incremental* results here is Epsilon’s holdout-based reporting; the clearest relevant home-services figures are Path2Response’s 4% month-over-month response lift and 3× response rate among recent site visitors, but neither public summary establishes a randomized holdout. PostPilot publishes a holdout-validated retail ROAS result, though not a direct comparison of ML-selected households against a control.[2][4][6][9]

For vendor evaluation, ask for the test design and raw denominators: randomized holdout size, response and conversion definitions, incremental revenue or profit per mailed household, postage/production costs, campaign dates, and results by vertical. Treat “response lift,” modeled-audience performance, and incremental lift versus an unmailed control as different metrics.
