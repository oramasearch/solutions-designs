The strongest documented examples are concentrated in discharge prediction, length of stay (LOS), and prior-authorization-related discharge planning. The available sources provide few named vendor deployments or financial ROI figures; several report operational outcomes rather than a measured return on investment.

| Use case | Health system / vendor | Measured outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Discharge prediction and LOS** | A U.S. hospital network operating seven hospitals; the source does not name the system or vendor | After gradual deployment to more than 200 doctors, nurses, and case managers, average LOS fell by **0.67 days per patient**. Combining 48-hour discharge predictions with clinicians’ predictions was associated with **10%–28.7% more discharges** and fewer 7- and 30-day readmissions. The authors estimated **$55–$72 million in annual financial benefits**; this is an estimate, not reported realized ROI.[7] |
| **Discharge prediction and LOS** | Community hospital in Columbia, Maryland; vendor not identified | Adding predictions to multidisciplinary rounds reduced LOS by **more than 12 hours** on one medicine unit and one telemetry unit. The study found no significant change on the surgery unit or a second medicine unit, so results varied by unit.[3] |
| **Post-acute discharge planning / authorization timing** | Hospital and vendor not identified in the cited report | A model predicting post-acute discharge disposition from initial nursing assessments reportedly enabled authorization to start on admission day rather than near discharge. The study reports an **average 22.22% reduction in inpatient LOS** and model accuracy of **84.16%**; it does not give a health-system name or dollar ROI.[10] |
| **Long-stay risk** | Gloucestershire Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust; internally developed prototype | The proof of concept identified **about two-thirds of patients who became long-stayers** (stays over 21 days). The NHS case study describes a prototype and potential for earlier intervention, not a demonstrated reduction in LOS or measured ROI.[6] |
| **Surgical LOS and discharge disposition prediction** | Duke Health; research team developed the models, with no external vendor named | Using data from more than 63,000 elective inpatient surgeries, models predicted postoperative LOS with **81% accuracy** and discharge disposition with **88% accuracy**. The source reports predictive performance, not an operational or financial impact from deployment.[4] |
| **Prior authorization for prescriptions** | Mount Sinai Health System; enterprise pharmacy-intelligence platform, vendor not named in the source | Mount Sinai reported handling **15%–20% year-over-year prescription growth without adding staff**, cutting authorization work from **25–30 minutes to under five**, and getting medications to patients in **two rather than four days**. These are reported operational outcomes, not a dollar ROI calculation.[1] |

**What the evidence does—and doesn’t—show:** The clearest quantified operational impact is the seven-hospital network’s reported LOS reduction, but its system and vendor are unnamed and the financial figure is an anticipated benefit. The other examples mostly measure time, LOS, or model performance; they should not be presented as proven financial ROI. For OR scheduling, nurse staffing forecasting, readmission-risk deployment, sepsis alerts, and bed-capacity optimization, the available results here do not establish a named health system, vendor, and measured production ROI together.
