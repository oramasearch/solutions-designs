## Executive orientation

**What a solution is.** In this compendium's vocabulary, a *use case* is the business problem (e.g. churn), a *methodology* is a reusable arrangement of AI methods (e.g. extract tabular data → feature engineering → XGBoost → SHAP → report), and a **solution** is the methodology **applied in a correct real-world setup and connected to the business action that realizes the value**. Every solution here therefore has three parts: (a) the methodology it composes from, (b) the **correctness adaptations** its real context demands — the disciplines (point-in-time features, temporal validation, entity resolution, imbalance handling, drift monitoring, train/serve consistency) that separate a correct application from a naive one, and (c) the **business wiring** — decision policies, systems of action, owners, cadences, and the measurement loop that proves incremental value. The cardinality is the structural insight: **many use cases ≈ many solutions ≫ few methodologies**. Roughly ten methodology families generate the thirty-four solutions cataloged here, and one of them — the tabular supervised methodology — underpins by itself more than a third of the catalog, because tabular data is the majority of business data.

**The anatomy.** Every deployed, value-producing solution in this catalog instantiates the same seven-stage template: `methodology → correctness adaptations → decision policy → action integration → measurement loop → governance → adoption`. Model output never touches the business directly; it passes through an explicit **decision policy** (thresholds, budgets, contact limits, approval gates) into one of four **action-integration patterns** — report→human decision, task-in-system-of-work, policy engine, or closed-loop automation with guardrails — and its impact is proven by a **measurement loop** (holdouts, uplift measurement, phased rollout, guardrail metrics) that feeds back into both the model and the action playbook. Solutions that skip a stage do not degrade gracefully; they stall (no action integration), silently self-congratulate (no holdout), or quietly rot (no drift monitoring).

**The headline on success and failure.** The gap between pilot and production is the dominant fact of enterprise AI. Gartner forecast that at least 30% of generative-AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by end-2025 (Gartner press release, 2024) and that over 40% of agentic-AI projects will be canceled by end-2027 (Gartner, 2025). The MIT NANDA "GenAI Divide" report found ~95% of enterprise GenAI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact (MIT, 2025). McKinsey's State of AI survey found 88% of organizations using AI but only ~39% able to attribute any EBIT impact, and just ~6% attributing more than 5% of EBIT (McKinsey, 2025). The widely repeated "87% of data-science projects never reach production" figure circulates without a clean primary source and should be treated as citation-chain folklore, but the verified statistics tell the same story. Critically, the documented post-mortems (Zillow Offers' >$500M write-down in 2021; the Epic sepsis model's AUC 0.63 in external validation, JAMA Internal Medicine 2021; IBM Watson for Oncology's $62M abandonment at MD Anderson, 2017) show that solutions fail overwhelmingly at the **applied layer** — leakage and drift the correctness adaptations should have caught, actions nobody was wired to take, measurement nobody designed, governance nobody consulted — and far more rarely because the methodology itself was wrong. That is why this compendium's center of gravity is field 4 (correctness adaptations) and fields 5–9 (business wiring and measurement) of every dossier.

## Family A — Customer & revenue

### 1. Churn prediction & retention program (anchor)

1. **Name** — Churn prediction & retention program (the anchoring solution of this compendium).
2. **Use case solved** — Which customers will leave, why, and what to do about it, so that revenue lost to churn is reduced.
3. **Methodology composed from** — The tabular supervised methodology: extract tabular data → feature engineering → train XGBoost (gradient-boosted trees) → SHAP explanation → report. The single most common enterprise methodology, because customer data is tabular.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — The methodology is naive until it is made **timeseries-appropriate**; churn data is time-ordered and almost every naive churn model leaks the future into the features. The full discipline:
   - **Point-in-time feature snapshots.** Every feature must be computed *as of* a prediction date T, using only data available at T. A feature like "support tickets in last 90 days" computed from a live table silently includes post-T tickets — often tickets *caused by the decision to leave* — and produces a model that "predicts" churn by observing its symptoms after the fact. Implementation: snapshot tables or a feature store with time-travel semantics; every feature keyed by (entity_id, as_of_date); joins are `as-of` joins, never latest-value joins.
   - **Label windows that don't peek.** Define churn as an event inside an explicit outcome window (T, T+H] — e.g. "cancels within 90 days of the snapshot date". The label window must start strictly after the feature window ends; a gap ("blackout") period between them is often needed because the operational churn signal (non-renewal notice, downgrade request) precedes the recorded churn date. Choose H to match the business's ability to act: a 90-day horizon gives a retention team time to intervene; a 7-day horizon produces accurate but unactionable predictions.
   - **Temporal validation, not random splits.** Random k-fold cross-validation on time-ordered data leaks: the model trains on the future of its own test set, and offline AUC overstates production performance. Use out-of-time splits (train on snapshots up to month M, validate on M+1..M+k), and evaluate on multiple rolling windows to see performance stability, not one lucky split.
   - **Multiple snapshots per customer, handled carefully.** Stacking monthly snapshots multiplies training data but correlates rows; group-aware splitting (a customer's rows never straddle train/test) prevents identity leakage.
   - **Censoring awareness.** Customers still active at the end of the observation window are right-censored — "didn't churn *yet*" is not "won't churn". Either restrict labels to fully observed windows or use survival methods (Cox, gradient-boosted survival) when tenure modeling matters.
   - **Class imbalance handled at the decision layer, not by mangling the data.** Churn base rates of 1–5%/month are normal. Prefer proper scoring (log-loss), class weights, and threshold-setting against business costs over naive oversampling; if sampling is used, calibrate probabilities afterward (Platt/isotonic) because retention economics need calibrated probabilities, not just rank order.
   - **Entity resolution.** "Customer" must be resolved across billing, CRM, product telemetry, and support systems; households vs accounts vs users must match the unit the business can act on. Mis-resolved entities produce duplicate rows (leakage) and actions aimed at the wrong unit.
   - **Leaky feature exclusion by construction.** Features that proxy the label — "days since cancellation call", account-manager churn-risk flags, win-back campaign membership — must be excluded. A too-good-to-be-true AUC (>0.95 on a hard business problem) is the standard symptom; several published churn "case studies" reporting 0.99+ accuracy (e.g. a SaaS Random Forest study reporting 0.998 accuracy / 0.997 AUC, 2022) are best read as leakage warnings, not benchmarks.
   - **Concept drift discipline.** Churn drivers shift with pricing changes, competitor moves, and macro shocks. Monitor input distributions (PSI), prediction distributions, and realized performance per cohort; retrain on a schedule (typically monthly/quarterly) *and* on drift triggers.
   - **Train/serve consistency.** The features computed in the batch scoring job must be bit-identical in definition to the training features — the same code path or a shared feature store, not a re-implementation in the scoring pipeline.
   - **Correlation vs causation in the SHAP layer.** SHAP explains the *model*, not the *world*: "contacted support 3× → high churn risk" does not mean suppressing support contact reduces churn. Drivers are hypotheses for intervention design, to be validated by the measurement loop — this is precisely the boundary between the report and the action.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — The report (scored list + SHAP driver analysis) is used to **change the business** on two levels:
   - **Retail level (per-customer actions):** the scored list, segmented by risk × value × dominant driver, drives a treatment playbook — proactive outreach call for high-value/high-risk, targeted discount or plan-fit review where the driver is price, onboarding/success intervention where the driver is low product usage, service-recovery escalation where the driver is support friction. Decision rules sit between score and action: risk threshold per treatment tier, per-quarter retention budget, contact-frequency caps (do not message the same customer twice a month), eligibility exclusions (in collections, in an open complaint), and — the mature form — **uplift/persuadability targeting** rather than raw propensity, because contacting predicted churners can *cause* churn: causal-uplift work at the Portuguese insurer Fidelidade found a "sleeping dogs" segment where withholding contact reduced churn by ~7 percentage points vs. the untargeted control (DML uplift thesis on Fidelidade data, 2020s), and Devriendt et al. showed uplift models beat propensity models on retention-campaign profitability (Applied Soft Computing / financial-industry study, 2021).
   - **Wholesale level (change the business itself):** the SHAP driver report is routed to product, pricing, and service owners as a decision document — recurring drivers become roadmap items (fix the onboarding cliff at day 30, restructure the tier that over-indexes in churn, staff the support queue that drives escalations). This is the report→decision variant elaborated in dossier 34; the churn program is deliberately *both* an action pipeline and a decision-intelligence loop.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — CRM (Salesforce/HubSpot/Dynamics: risk score + driver fields on the account record, tasks created for owners), marketing automation (Braze/Salesforce Marketing Cloud/Iterable: suppression lists and triggered retention journeys), customer-success platforms (Gainsight/ChurnZero: health-score ingestion, playbook triggering), billing (discount/entitlement application), and the BI layer (the driver report). Standard integration: batch score to warehouse → reverse-ETL to CRM/CS tool → playbook automation in the system where the acting team already lives. The score must appear where the owner works, not in a separate dashboard.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Consumers: customer-success managers / retention agents (act on accounts), retention-marketing owner (runs campaigns), product & pricing leadership (consumes the driver report), data science (owns model + measurement). Cadence: scores refreshed weekly/monthly to match the action cycle; a monthly retention council reviews driver trends, treatment performance, and playbook changes. Replaces: reactive save-desks that only engage after a cancellation request, and gut-feel account triage.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — High-value B2B accounts: human CSM decides and personalizes the intervention (assisted decisioning). High-volume B2C: automated journeys within policy caps, with human review only of the playbook, not each contact. Escalation: accounts that trip both high risk and high value route to a named owner with an SLA; discount offers above a cap require manager approval. Full automation of *contact* is appropriate; automation of *concessions* is gated.
9. **Measurement loop** — The non-negotiable core: a **randomized holdout** of at-risk customers who receive no intervention, maintained permanently (typically 5–10% of each risk tier), so incremental retention = treated-cohort retention − holdout retention, not raw campaign response. Mature programs run uplift-designed A/B tests per treatment (offer A vs B vs none) and report saved revenue = incremental retention × CLV − offer cost. Documented scale of the prize: a major UK insurer's uplift-modeled retention program preserved ~£16M/year via a 2-percentage-point churn reduction over two years (InoGen case study, 2020s; vendor-reported); telecom literature synthesis associates CRM-integrated churn ML with churn reductions up to ~15% (Scientific Reports, 2025). Guardrails: contact-fatigue complaints, discount-cost budget, margin erosion, NPS in treated vs holdout. Feedback: treatment outcomes label the next uplift model; driver report changes the playbook; playbook changes are themselves A/B tested. The most common measurement failure — attributing natural retention to the campaign because no holdout exists — is the single most documented way churn programs overstate value.
10. **Operational architecture** — Batch scoring (weekly/monthly) is the norm; near-real-time only for event-triggered saves (cancellation-page intervention). Latency requirements are hours, not milliseconds. Retraining monthly/quarterly plus drift triggers; monitoring covers feature drift (PSI), score distribution, calibration decay, per-segment AUC on realized labels (available after each label window closes), and action-execution rates (are the tasks actually being worked?).
11. **Governance & safety** — Access control on the scored list (it is commercially sensitive and, under GDPR, profiling of identifiable persons); audit trail of score → action → outcome; explainability to internal stakeholders via SHAP (also the trust mechanism for CSMs); fairness review where treatments confer economic benefit (who systematically gets retention discounts?); suppression of vulnerable-customer segments per sector rules (e.g. UK FCA Consumer Duty for financial services).
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Thin first version: one snapshot pipeline, ~30–60 leakage-checked features, XGBoost, SHAP, a scored list in the CRM, and a holdout — feasible in 6–10 weeks where the warehouse is in order. Long poles: entity resolution across systems, historical label reconstruction (point-in-time data often doesn't exist retroactively), CRM integration, and change management with the retention team. The model itself is days of the total effort.
13. **Adoption & change management** — CSMs distrust opaque scores; SHAP reason codes on each account ("risk driven by: usage −40% QoQ; 2 open P1 tickets") are the adoption mechanism, not a nicety. Run 1–2 cycles in shadow/side-by-side mode (model list vs CSM intuition, compare hit rates) before the score drives quotas or account routing. Documented failure mode: lists exported to spreadsheets nobody works; the fix is embedding tasks in the CRM with owners and SLAs.
14. **Documented deployments** — (a) Fidelidade (Portuguese insurer): causal-uplift retention targeting; persuadable-targeting reduced churn ~6.8 pp vs. untargeted treatment; sleeping-dog protection ~7.0 pp vs. control (uplift/DML thesis on production data, 2020s). (b) Major UK insurer: uplift-modeled retention preserved ~£16M/year, churn −2 pp over two years (InoGen case study; vendor-reported). (c) Financial-services retention: uplift models outperformed propensity churn models on campaign profitability (Devriendt et al., 2021). (d) Telecom sector: CRM-integrated AI churn programs associated with up to ~15% churn reduction (Scientific Reports synthesis, 2025). (e) Netflix frames its retention economics around personalization rather than a churn-score program, valuing the combined recommendation/personalization effect at >$1B/year in retention (Gomez-Uribe & Hunt, ACM, 2015) — the boundary case where the "retention action" is the product experience itself.
15. **Failure modes** — (i) **Leakage-inflated pilots**: offline AUC 0.95+, production usefulness near zero; the pilot's credibility is spent before wiring begins. (ii) **Score without action**: the list is produced, no team owns treatments — the most common stall. (iii) **Propensity backfire**: contacting sure-things wastes budget; contacting sleeping dogs increases churn (Fidelidade evidence above; Radcliffe & Simpson's uplift literature, 2008). (iv) **No holdout**: program "succeeds" by claiming natural retention; later scrutiny kills funding. (v) **Incentive misalignment**: retention teams comped on save-rate learn to target easy saves, gaming the metric while incremental value stays flat. (vi) **Drift rot**: a pricing change shifts drivers; unmonitored, the model quietly targets last year's churners.
16. **Variants** — B2C high-volume (telecom/streaming/retail banking): automated journeys, uplift targeting, weekly batch. B2B SaaS: account-level health scores in CS platforms, human CSM actions, monthly cadence, heavier driver reporting. Non-contractual retail: churn is silence, not cancellation — label design shifts to inter-purchase-gap / BTYD-style definitions. Data-maturity ladder: batch report → embedded CRM tasks → uplift-optimized automated journeys.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Frequently built in-house (the tabular methodology is commodity; the value is in the org-specific wiring); bought/configured inside CS platforms (Gainsight, ChurnZero, Totango health scores), CDPs and cloud AutoML; telecom/insurance often buy vendor retention suites with uplift capability. The wiring — playbooks, holdouts, driver councils — cannot be bought.
18. **Key references** — Gomez-Uribe & Hunt, "The Netflix Recommender System" (ACM, 2015); Devriendt et al. on uplift vs. predictive retention (2021); Radcliffe & Simpson uplift papers (2008); Fidelidade causal-uplift thesis (Técnico Lisboa); Scientific Reports telecom churn synthesis (2025); practitioner literature on point-in-time feature stores (Feast/Tecton docs) and temporal validation.

### 2. Next-best-action / personalization engine

1. **Name** — Next-best-action (NBA) / personalization decisioning engine.
2. **Use case solved** — Which message/offer/action to present to each customer at each touchpoint to maximize long-term customer value (not per-touch clicks).
3. **Methodology composed from** — Tabular supervised propensity models per action + ranking/decision layer (often contextual bandits) + uplift modeling; components shared with churn (features, gradient boosting, explanation).
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Position/selection bias**: training on logged interactions biases toward previously shown actions; requires propensity logging (record what *could* have been shown and with what probability) and off-policy evaluation (IPS/doubly-robust) before any offline claim is trusted. (ii) **Incrementality over propensity**: click-propensity targeting rediscovers customers who would convert anyway; uplift objectives or bandit exploration measure lift. (iii) **Feedback loops**: the model shapes its own future training data; without exploration traffic (epsilon or Thompson sampling) the system collapses onto a self-confirming policy. (iv) Point-in-time features and entity resolution exactly as in churn. (v) **Arbitration correctness**: when many models bid for one slot, a value-based arbitration layer with eligibility rules must prevent contradictory actions (retention discount and upsell in the same session). (vi) Cold start for new offers via hierarchical priors/bandits.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Per-touchpoint action selection: which banner, offer, email, agent script, or "no action" (a first-class arm). Decision rules: eligibility (regulatory, inventory, margin floors), contact-frequency caps across channels, budget constraints per offer pool, and business-priority overrides. Executed automatically by the delivery channel; humans design the action catalog and constraints.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Web/app personalization layer, marketing automation, contact-center scripting (agent sees the NBA), CDP as the feature/identity backbone. Pattern: central decision service (API) called by every channel, so decisions and logging are consistent — the "decisioning hub" (e.g. Pega Customer Decision Hub, Salesforce Interaction Studio, Adobe Target) rather than per-channel models.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Marketing/CX owns the action catalog and strategy; data science owns models and off-policy evaluation; a weekly trading meeting reviews arm performance and retires/launches actions. Replaces channel-siloed campaign calendars ("everyone gets the May email") with per-customer arbitration.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Fully automated at decision time (volume makes per-decision review impossible); human control moves to the catalog, constraints, and exploration budget. High-stakes offers (credit line increases, retention concessions) gate through approval workflows.
9. **Measurement loop** — Global holdout (customers who get rule-based/random policy) is the gold standard for program-level incrementality; per-arm bandit statistics for local optimization; guardrails on unsubscribe rates, margin, and complaint volume. Long-horizon value measured on cohorts, not per-touch CTR — the classic failure is optimizing clicks while eroding annual value.
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time decision API (tens of ms) over precomputed features + session context; batch feature refresh; continuous or daily model updates for bandit layers; monitoring of arm distributions, exploration rate, and reward drift.
11. **Governance & safety** — Offer-fairness review (who systematically gets the good prices?), regulatory eligibility (financial promotions rules), consent/preference enforcement, full decision logging for audit ("why did this customer see this offer?").
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Thin version: one channel, 3–5 actions, a bandit with propensity logging, a holdout — a quarter of work. Full omnichannel arbitration hubs are multi-year programs; the long pole is identity + channel integration, not modeling.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Marketing teams must surrender per-campaign control to a policy — the hardest sell in the family; side-by-side periods ("your segment plan vs the engine, same budget") and transparent arm-level reporting build trust. Documented failures cluster on catalog neglect: engines optimizing over stale, near-identical offers.
14. **Documented deployments** — Netflix personalization/recommendation stack valued at >$1B/year in retention effect (Gomez-Uribe & Hunt, 2015). Large banks and telcos run decisioning hubs at production scale (Pega/Adobe/Salesforce customer bases; vendor-documented, magnitudes vendor-reported). Streaming platforms document uplift-aware messaging with "do-not-disturb" segments to avoid churn-inducing contact (practitioner accounts, 2020s). Sector evidence is strong; audited per-company numbers are scarcer than for churn/fraud.
15. **Failure modes** — Optimizing short-term clicks against long-term value; self-reinforcing policies without exploration; offer-catalog rot; channel teams bypassing the hub (two systems contacting the same customer); measurement theater (per-campaign response reported, program holdout absent).
16. **Variants** — Batch NBA lists for outbound teams (low maturity) → real-time single-channel → omnichannel arbitration (high maturity). Retail media / commerce variants blur into recommendations (dossier 6).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Predominantly **bought/configured**: Pega CDH, Salesforce Marketing Cloud Personalization, Adobe Target/AJO, Braze; in-house builds at digital-native scale (feature store + bandit service). The action catalog and constraint design are always in-house work.
18. **Key references** — Off-policy evaluation literature (IPS/DR estimators); contextual-bandit deployment papers (e.g. Microsoft Decision Service, 2016); Netflix ACM paper (2015); vendor architecture docs for decisioning hubs.

### 3. Lead & opportunity scoring in CRM

1. **Name** — Lead & opportunity scoring embedded in CRM.
2. **Use case solved** — Which leads/opportunities deserve sales attention now, so pipeline effort concentrates where it converts.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Tabular supervised methodology (extract CRM + firmographic + product-signal data → features → gradient boosting/logistic → SHAP reason codes → scores in CRM); the churn methodology re-aimed at conversion labels.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Outcome leakage from CRM hygiene**: fields edited *after* the outcome (deal size updated on close, stage backfilled) leak; features must come from point-in-time CRM history (field-history tables), not current records — the single most common leak in this solution. (ii) **Selection bias**: history only shows outcomes for leads sales *chose* to work; a model trained on it learns "what sales already likes", not "what converts" — mitigate with exploration quotas (a slice of low-scored leads worked anyway) and careful claims. (iii) Label design: conversion window per funnel stage; recycled leads need dedup and re-entry logic (entity resolution across marketing systems). (iv) Class imbalance and calibration (routing capacity decisions need calibrated probabilities). (v) Drift with ICP changes, pricing changes, and market cycles; retrain quarterly. (vi) Train/serve consistency between the warehouse model and the CRM-visible score.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Routing (hot leads to senior reps within SLA), prioritized work queues, SLA tiers (respond in 5 min vs nurture), automated nurture assignment for low scores, and opportunity-stage risk flags driving manager coaching. Decision rules: score bands × territory × capacity; caps prevent starving new segments.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — CRM natively (Salesforce Einstein-style fields, HubSpot score properties), marketing-automation (Marketo/HubSpot) for nurture, sales-engagement tools (Outreach/Salesloft) for sequences, routing engines (LeanData). Integration: score + top-3 SHAP reasons written to the lead/opportunity record; queue views sort by score.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — SDR/AE teams consume; sales ops owns routing rules; marketing owns nurture; RevOps runs the weekly funnel review where score bands vs conversion are inspected. Replaces alphabetical/first-in-first-out lead lists and BANT gut checks.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Assisted decisioning: reps decide; the score orders the queue. Full automation only for suppression (never auto-reject a lead solely on score without an exploration slice). Escalation: high-score untouched leads alert managers.
9. **Measurement loop** — Randomized routing experiments (score-ordered vs status-quo queues, matched capacity) prove lift in conversion per rep-hour; holdout slices of unscored routing guard against selection-bias self-confirmation; guardrails: lead-response SLA, coverage of new segments, rep gaming (cherry-picking). Feedback: closed-won/lost labels retrain; reason-code accuracy reviewed with reps.
10. **Operational architecture** — Batch (hourly/daily) scoring is standard; near-real-time for inbound speed-to-lead. Monitoring: score distribution drift, per-segment precision, CRM field-latency issues.
11. **Governance & safety** — Modest relative to family D; care with firmographic proxies that encode demographic bias in SMB selling; documentation for sales-comp disputes ("the model sent me bad leads").
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Among the fastest in the catalog: 4–8 weeks thin version if CRM history is usable; long pole is CRM data hygiene and rep trust, not modeling.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Reps ignore scores that contradict experience unless reason codes are visible and early wins are socialized; side-by-side ("work your list vs the model's list") pilots are standard; adoption failures are the norm where scores appear without queue/SLA changes (score-as-decoration).
14. **Documented deployments** — Ubiquitous as a product capability (Salesforce Einstein Lead Scoring, HubSpot predictive scoring — vendor-documented at large customer scale); public, audited lift numbers are rare because outcomes are competitively sensitive; sector case studies typically report conversion-rate lifts in the tens of percent (vendor-reported; treat as directional). This dossier's evidence base is thinner on named numbers than churn/fraud — an honest gap.
15. **Failure modes** — CRM hygiene garbage-in; selection-bias self-fulfilment (model canonizes existing bias about "good leads"); score-as-decoration with unchanged process; gaming when comp attaches to scored leads; silent breakage when marketing changes form fields feeding features.
16. **Variants** — PLG product-qualified-lead scoring (product telemetry features dominate); ABM account scoring (aggregation to account level); opportunity risk scoring (in-flight deals, forecast hygiene) — same methodology, different label and cadence.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Overwhelmingly **configured inside CRM/MAP platforms**; built in-house where product telemetry is the signal (PLG SaaS); specialist vendors (6sense, MadKudu) for intent-data enrichment.
18. **Key references** — Vendor documentation (Salesforce Einstein, HubSpot); RevOps practitioner literature on lead-scoring experiments; selection-bias treatments in the off-policy literature.

### 4. Dynamic pricing & discount governance

1. **Name** — Dynamic pricing & discount governance.
2. **Use case solved** — Setting prices/discounts that track demand, inventory, and competition to lift revenue/margin without eroding trust or breaking rules.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Demand forecasting + price-elasticity estimation (tabular/causal ML) + constrained optimization; guardrail/policy layer; for airlines/hotels, classical revenue management augmented with ML.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Price endogeneity**: historical price-demand data is confounded (prices were set in response to demand); naive supervised elasticity is wrong by construction — requires causal identification: pricing experiments, natural experiments, instrumental variables, or at minimum train/serve awareness that the model's own policy generated the data. (ii) **Exploration under constraint**: deliberate price tests within legal/brand bands to keep elasticity estimates identified as conditions shift. (iii) **Competitive reaction & feedback loops**: competitor-matching models can spiral (documented degenerate case: two algorithmic booksellers repricing a used book to $23M on Amazon, 2011); damping and sanity bounds are correctness features. (iv) **Inventory/censoring**: observed sales are demand *censored by availability*; stockouts must be treated as censored demand or elasticity is biased down. (v) Temporal validation across seasons; holiday/event regime features. (vi) **Fairness/perception constraints as hard bounds** (no personalized prices on protected proxies; regulated sectors prohibit some price discrimination outright).
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Price updates pushed to the pricing engine at defined cadences; markdown schedules; discount-approval thresholds for sales teams (deal-desk governance: model recommends floor/target, discounts beyond band require approval); promo depth recommendations. Decision rules: min/max bands, margin floors, MAP compliance, change-frequency caps, category-level strategy overrides.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — E-commerce pricing engines, ERP price lists, POS, CPQ systems (deal-desk variant), channel-manager systems (hotels), GDS/RMS (airlines: PROS, Amadeus; hotels: IDeaS, Duetto).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Pricing/revenue-management team owns strategy and bands; merchandisers/category managers review exceptions; finance audits margin. Cadence: intraday (e-commerce, airlines) to weekly (B2B lists). Replaces cost-plus spreadsheets and static seasonal calendars.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Closed-loop automation *within bands*; humans set bands, review exceptions and step-changes; deal-desk discounts are assisted decisioning with approval gates. New-product pricing stays human-led (cold start).
9. **Measurement loop** — Price experiments (region/store/SKU-randomized where feasible), switchback tests where markets interfere, margin and revenue vs forecast counterfactuals; guardrails: conversion, price-perception/NPS, complaint volume, competitor-position drift, legal band compliance. Airlines/hotels measure RASK/RevPAR against control routes/properties.
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time or intraday scoring for e-commerce/travel; batch for lists. Elasticity retraining seasonal; heavy monitoring of price-change volume, band-hit rates, and demand-model error.
11. **Governance & safety** — Price-discrimination law, MAP/antitrust (algorithmic collusion is an active regulatory topic — DOJ/EU scrutiny of RealPage-style shared pricing algorithms, 2023–2025), brand-trust rules (surge caps after the reputational lessons of ride-hailing), and audit logs of every price decision.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Markdown optimization and deal-desk floors are quick wins (a quarter); full dynamic pricing is a multi-quarter program; long poles: price-execution plumbing across channels and the experimentation program.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Merchant/revenue teams keep override rights initially; transparency reports (what changed, why, what it earned) each week; documented adoption failure: silent overrides re-imposing old prices, so the "AI pricing program" runs while humans nullify it.
14. **Documented deployments** — Lufthansa Group attributed ~€300M incremental revenue over three years to dynamic-pricing deployment (earnings-presentation figure via PROS case material, 2019–2020; vendor-linked) with a reported ~7% revenue-per-passenger uplift (PROS case study, 2019). Simulation studies of airline dynamic pricing show 1–7% revenue gains over traditional RM (Wittman et al., Journal of Revenue and Pricing Management, 2018). An airline-ancillary ML pricing A/B test showed +36% conversion and +10% revenue per offer vs. rule-based pricing (Deepair, arXiv, 2019). Hotel chains adopting AI-driven RMS report ~5–10% RevPAR lifts (industry syntheses, 2020s; secondary), with small-property automated pricing studies reporting ~22% average revenue increase moving from manual pricing (RoomPriceGenie study of European hotels, 2019; vendor-reported).
15. **Failure modes** — Endogeneity-corrupted elasticities (prices chase noise); algorithmic price spirals; customer-trust damage from visible volatility or perceived unfairness (surge-pricing backlash); override culture nullifying the system; legal exposure where algorithms share competitor data.
16. **Variants** — Airline/hotel RM (mature, decades old, ML-augmented); e-commerce repricing; markdown optimization (fashion/grocery end-of-life); B2B deal-desk floors (CPQ); subscription price-testing. Wiring differs mainly in cadence and approval structure.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Travel: buy (PROS, Amadeus, Sabre; IDeaS, Duetto). Retail: buy (Revionics, Blue Yonder pricing) or build at scale (Amazon-style in-house). B2B: CPQ-embedded (Salesforce, Pros Smart CPQ). Elasticity science is often in-house even when execution is bought.
18. **Key references** — Wittman et al. (2018); Deepair ancillary-pricing A/B (arXiv, 2019); Phillips, *Pricing and Revenue Optimization*; regulatory commentary on algorithmic pricing (2023–2025).

### 5. CLV-driven audience management & promotion optimization

1. **Name** — Customer-lifetime-value–driven audience management & promotion optimization.
2. **Use case solved** — Allocating acquisition/retention/promo spend by long-term customer value rather than short-term response, and setting promo depth/targeting that is incrementally profitable.
3. **Methodology composed from** — CLV modeling (BTYD probabilistic models or gradient-boosted regression on point-in-time features) + uplift modeling for promo response + audience segmentation; the tabular methodology with a value-regression head.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Censoring is the core problem**: most customers' lifetimes are unfinished; naive "average revenue of past customers" biases low/high depending on cohort mix — use BTYD-class models or explicit horizon-limited value (e.g. 24-month CLV) with out-of-time validation. (ii) Cohort drift: acquisition-channel mix shifts change value distributions; validate on recent cohorts. (iii) **Promo incrementality**: redemption ≠ lift; uplift design or holdouts required because coupons cannibalize full-price purchases (the classic measured result of promo holdouts). (iv) Point-in-time features (spend history as-of date); identity resolution across channels/devices (audience quality depends on it). (v) Selection bias in paid-media audiences (platforms optimize toward converters; incrementality needs geo/PSA experiments).
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Value-tiered audiences pushed to ad platforms (lookalikes seeded on high-CLV), budget allocation across channels by predicted payback, promo depth per segment, suppression of discount-addicted segments, and value-based bidding (CLV as the conversion value sent to ad platforms). Decision rules: CAC caps per value tier, promo budget constraints, frequency caps.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — CDP → ad platforms (Meta/Google value-based audiences), ESP/marketing automation, promo engines/POS for offer execution, MMM/attribution stack for measurement.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Growth/performance-marketing team consumes daily; finance validates CLV assumptions quarterly; merchandising owns promo calendar integration. Replaces last-click ROAS budgeting.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Automated audience sync; human control at budget-strategy level; deep promo discounts gated by margin approvals.
9. **Measurement loop** — Geo-experiments and platform conversion-lift studies for media; permanent promo holdout groups for offer incrementality; CLV model backtests against realized 12/24-month value; guardrails: margin, discount dependency (share of revenue on promo), new-vs-returning mix.
10. **Operational architecture** — Batch (daily/weekly) everywhere; the only latency-sensitive piece is bid-value sync. Retrain CLV quarterly; monitor cohort-level calibration (predicted vs realized value curves).
11. **Governance & safety** — Privacy/consent for audience sharing (GDPR/CCPA); fairness in who receives discounts; financial-reporting discipline if CLV enters investor metrics.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Thin: horizon-limited CLV on warehouse data + value-tiered audiences in one platform, ~6 weeks. Long poles: identity resolution and experiment infrastructure.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Finance must bless the CLV definition or the program dies in metric disputes; marketing teams need side-by-side ROAS-vs-CLV budget reporting through a transition period.
14. **Documented deployments** — Widely deployed in e-commerce/retail (value-based bidding is a first-class Google/Meta product capability, 2020s); public audited numbers are scarce; promo-holdout literature and retail case studies consistently show large shares of redeemed promos are non-incremental (measured cannibalization; magnitudes vary by study — stated qualitatively). Danone's promotion-forecasting program improved net promotion uplift by 36%→55% and net ROI by 6→8 pp across 2011–2012 (ToolsGroup case study; vendor-reported).
15. **Failure modes** — CLV overfitting to acquisition-era covariates; promo programs that report redemption as success (no holdout) while margin erodes; audience sync privacy incidents; finance/marketing metric wars stalling adoption.
16. **Variants** — Subscription (CLV ≈ retention modeling, ties to dossier 1); retail/CPG (promo optimization dominates); marketplaces (two-sided value). Batch-report variant = quarterly value-segmentation strategy decks; embedded variant = live value-based bidding.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — CLV models usually built (or via CDP add-ons: Segment/Bloomreach/Lifesight-class); promo optimization bought in retail (Eversight-class, now inside larger suites); measurement via platform lift studies + in-house geo tests.
18. **Key references** — Fader & Hardie BTYD literature; platform docs on value-based bidding; ToolsGroup/Danone case (2012); promo-incrementality practitioner literature.

### 6. Recommendation systems in product & commerce

1. **Name** — Product/content recommendation system.
2. **Use case solved** — Surfacing the right item to the right user in-product, driving engagement, conversion, and retention.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Ranking/recommendation methodology: candidate generation (collaborative filtering / embeddings / two-tower retrieval) → feature-rich ranking model (gradient boosting or neural) → business-rule re-ranking; online experimentation as a first-class component.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Logged-feedback bias**: users can only click what was shown; train with exposure-aware objectives, propensity weighting, and exploration traffic. (ii) **Popularity feedback loops**: recommenders amplify their own past choices; diversity/novelty constraints and periodic exploration prevent catalog collapse. (iii) **Cold start** (new items/users): content-based features, embeddings from item metadata, bandit boosts for new inventory. (iv) **Train/serve skew** is acute (feature computed differently in offline pipeline vs online service is the classic recsys production bug); shared feature/embedding services required. (v) Offline metric ≠ online value: offline NDCG gains routinely fail online — the A/B platform *is* the correctness mechanism. (vi) Real-time session features must be point-in-time consistent with training replay.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Automatic content of shelves/rows/emails/PDPs ("customers also bought"); merchandising rules layered on model output (margin boosts, stock-aware suppression, sponsored slots); the action *is* the product surface — the tightest possible business wiring, which is exactly why this solution family monetizes so reliably.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — The product itself (web/app), email/push systems, search platform (blended search-and-rec), ad/retail-media stack. Integration pattern: recommendation service API + event stream of impressions/clicks/purchases closing the loop.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Product managers own surfaces and guardrails; merchandisers set business rules; ML engineers own models and experiments. Weekly experiment reviews decide ship/kill. Replaces manual merchandising of every slot.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Fully automated serving; human control via rules, blocklists, and editorial pinning; escalation paths for content-safety incidents (recommending inappropriate items) with kill-switches per surface.
9. **Measurement loop** — Continuous A/B testing with holdback cells (a permanent small "no-personalization" or "previous-model" cell measures cumulative value); north-star metrics: conversion, watch time, long-term retention (measured via long-running holdouts); guardrails: diversity, return rate, complaint/report rate. Netflix quantified the combined personalization/recommendation effect at >$1B/year in retention value and reported ~75–80% of viewing driven by recommendation rows (Gomez-Uribe & Hunt, ACM, 2015). The oft-quoted "35% of Amazon purchases driven by recommendations" traces to a McKinsey 2013 estimate (via New America's case study) — indirect sourcing; treat as an estimate.
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time serving (tens of ms) with precomputed candidates + online ranking; streaming feature updates; frequent (daily→continuous) retraining; monitoring of CTR drift, coverage, staleness, and feedback-loop health.
11. **Governance & safety** — Content-safety filters; filter-bubble/diversity obligations (DSA transparency requirements for large platforms, EU, 2023+); explainability lite ("because you watched"); sponsored-content disclosure.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Thin: co-occurrence "also-bought" on one surface in weeks; competitive neural stacks are permanent programs. Long pole: event instrumentation and experimentation infrastructure — not the model.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Internal adoption issue is merchandiser displacement: resolve by giving merch teams the rule layer and experiment visibility. User trust: explanation strings and controls ("not interested").
14. **Documented deployments** — Netflix (>$1B/yr retention value; ~80% of viewing; ACM 2015). Amazon (~35% of purchases; McKinsey 2013 via New America — indirect). Otto (Germany) applied deep-learning demand prediction with ~90% 30-day accuracy in the adjacent replenishment domain (Economist/Harvard case, 2016–2017), illustrating the same methodology family monetized two ways. Platform-scale recsys (YouTube, Spotify) documented in engineering papers (RecSys/KDD, 2016–2020s) with watch-time/engagement gains reported qualitatively.
15. **Failure modes** — Offline-online metric divergence; feedback-loop catalog collapse; clickbait optimization damaging long-term retention (documented platform corrections toward "responsible" watch-time metrics); stale embeddings after catalog shifts; reputational incidents from unsafe adjacency.
16. **Variants** — Commerce (conversion/margin-aware), media (engagement/retention-aware), B2B catalogs (sparse data → content-based), email/push (batch), retail media (revenue-aware ranking with disclosure obligations).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Digital natives build; mid-market buys (Algolia Recommend, AWS Personalize, Google Retail API, Dynamic Yield, Bloomreach); the experimentation discipline must exist in-house either way.
18. **Key references** — Gomez-Uribe & Hunt (ACM, 2015); Covington et al., YouTube DNN recommendations (RecSys, 2016); industrial recsys bias/feedback-loop literature; New America Amazon case study (citing McKinsey 2013).

### 7. Automated ticket triage & routing

1. **Name** — Automated ticket triage & routing.
2. **Use case solved** — Getting every inbound ticket to the right queue/priority/team instantly, cutting misroutes and time-to-resolution.
3. **Methodology composed from** — NLP classification methodology (text extraction → embedding/features → multi-class classifier or fine-tuned LLM → routing decision); increasingly LLM zero/few-shot classification with the same wiring.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Label noise from historical routing**: the training labels are where tickets *ended up*, including misroutes and org-chart artifacts — cleaning against resolution outcomes ("which team actually closed it") beats raw first-assignment labels. (ii) **Taxonomy drift**: queues reorganize; models must be retrained on taxonomy change and category mappings versioned. (iii) **Class imbalance** across hundreds of queues; hierarchical classification (department → team → queue). (iv) **Confidence-based abstention**: below-threshold tickets route to a human triage queue — the abstention threshold is the key correctness control, tuned to misroute cost. (v) Multilinguality and channel mixing (email vs chat vs form) require per-channel evaluation. (vi) Temporal validation (product launches shift topic distributions overnight). (vii) PII handling in ticket text before any external model call.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Auto-assignment of queue/team/priority/SLA tier; auto-tagging for reporting; urgent-flag escalation (outage clusters page on-call); suggested macros attached. Decision rules: confidence thresholds per action (auto-route at 0.9, suggest at 0.6, human below), VIP-account overrides, safety keywords (legal/complaint/self-harm) always to humans.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Ticketing/ITSM platforms (Zendesk, ServiceNow, Jira SM, Freshdesk, Salesforce Service Cloud) via native triggers or middleware; QA/analytics stack for tag-driven reporting.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Removes the human dispatcher role (the process it replaces); team leads own taxonomy and threshold governance; support ops reviews misroute dashboards weekly.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Assisted for low confidence, automated above threshold; every human re-route is captured as a correction label (the feedback flywheel); escalation keywords hard-coded around the model.
9. **Measurement loop** — Misroute rate (re-assignment count) before/after, first-response and resolution times by category, with a phased rollout by queue as the natural experiment; guardrails: SLA breaches on auto-routed tickets, escalation latency for urgent classes. Vendor case studies claim large effects (e.g. a routing vendor reporting misrouting cut from 26% to 3.4% and 78% faster resolution — vendor-reported, 2026; treat as directional); independent audited numbers are scarce, so programs should generate their own via the phased rollout.
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time (sub-second to seconds) at ticket creation; retraining monthly or on taxonomy change; monitoring of per-class precision, abstention rate, and correction volume.
11. **Governance & safety** — PII redaction pre-inference; audit of routing decisions for SLA disputes; guardrails ensuring regulated-complaint categories always reach compliance queues (a legal requirement in banking/insurance).
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — One of the fastest LLM-era wins: weeks for a thin version (LLM classification into the existing taxonomy + abstention). Long pole: taxonomy cleanup — most orgs discover their queue structure is the real problem.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Dispatcher-role redeployment must be handled openly; team leads get threshold and mapping control; shadow mode for 2–4 weeks comparing model routes to human routes is the standard trust builder.
14. **Documented deployments** — Ubiquitous as platform capability (Zendesk intelligent triage, ServiceNow predictive intelligence — vendor-documented at scale, 2020s); the NBER agent-productivity study context (5,179 agents; Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, 2023) documents the adjacent workflows; named audited triage-specific numbers remain thin — an honest evidence gap; sector practice is nonetheless routine.
15. **Failure modes** — Taxonomy rot making the model confidently wrong; correction labels not harvested (flywheel never spins); over-automation of ambiguous categories breaching SLAs; "urgent" classes under-detected because rarity was never addressed.
16. **Variants** — ITSM incident categorization; email-room routing in insurance/banking (document-heavy, blends with dossier 31); social/chat triage with intent detection; multilingual hubs.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Overwhelmingly configured in-platform now; custom builds persist for complex taxonomies and on-prem constraints; LLM APIs have collapsed the build cost.
18. **Key references** — Platform ML docs (Zendesk, ServiceNow); text-classification production literature; NBER w31161 (2023) for the surrounding evidence base.

### 8. AI resolution / deflection with escalation

1. **Name** — AI resolution / deflection with escalation (customer-facing service automation).
2. **Use case solved** — Resolving routine customer inquiries end-to-end without an agent, while escalating cleanly, so cost-to-serve falls without satisfaction falling.
3. **Methodology composed from** — LLM/RAG generation methodology (retrieval over help content + policies → grounded generation → action tools for account operations) + intent classification + escalation policy; formerly intent-tree chatbots, now retrieval-grounded LLMs.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Grounding discipline**: answers must be constrained to retrieved, current policy content — ungrounded generation is a liability, not a UX flaw: in *Moffatt v. Air Canada* (BC Civil Resolution Tribunal, 2024) the airline was held liable for its chatbot inventing a bereavement-fare policy. (ii) **Permission-aware retrieval**: the bot must not read or reveal content the customer couldn't access. (iii) **Action-tool safety**: account actions (refunds, cancellations) need idempotency, amount caps, and verification gates. (iv) **Escalation-intent detection** as a first-class classifier (frustration, vulnerability, legal threats) with generous thresholds. (v) **Evaluation harness**: golden-set QA + resolution-quality sampling, run pre-deploy and continuously — "resolution rate" must mean *correctly* resolved, verified by sampling, not "conversation ended". (vi) Content freshness pipelines (stale KB = confidently wrong bot). (vii) Prompt-injection resistance where the bot browses user-supplied content.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Direct resolution (answer + account action), guided self-service, or warm handoff with full context to an agent. Decision rules: intent allowlist for automation (start narrow), monetary caps on automated refunds/credits, mandatory human review for regulated topics, always-available human path (Klarna's 2025 course correction — recruiting humans back so customers can always reach a person — is the canonical governance lesson; reported 2025).
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Chat/messaging platforms, help centers, IVR/voice bots; CRM/order systems via tool APIs; ticketing for escalations (context-carrying handoff is the integration make-or-break).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Support leadership owns the automation-scope policy; content/knowledge team becomes the bot's supply chain (KB quality = bot quality); agents shift toward escalations and QA sampling. Replaces tier-1 routine handling.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Automated within the intent allowlist; human escalation on request, on detection, or on failure; QA humans sample transcripts continuously. Full automation is appropriate for verifiable, low-stakes, policy-bounded intents; assisted or human-only for emotional, complex, or regulated contacts.
9. **Measurement loop** — Deflection/resolution rate *with quality sampling*; CSAT of bot-resolved vs agent-resolved; repeat-contact rate within 7 days (the honest metric — a "resolved" chat that returns is a failure); escalation friction; A/B rollouts by intent. Klarna's published first-month figures: 2.3M conversations, ~2/3 of service chats, work of ~700 FTE agents, resolution time 11 min → <2 min, −25% repeat inquiries, projected $40M profit improvement (Klarna press release / OpenAI case, Feb 2024) — followed by the 2025 acknowledgment that automation went too far and humans were re-hired, which is itself measurement-loop evidence: satisfaction and quality guardrails were under-weighted.
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time conversational latency (sub-2s per turn); retrieval index refreshed continuously; model/prompt versions release-managed with regression evals; monitoring of resolution, escalation, sentiment, and hallucination-flag rates.
11. **Governance & safety** — Legal accountability for bot statements (Air Canada precedent, 2024); disclosure that the customer is talking to AI (EU AI Act transparency obligations, phasing 2025–2026); data-privacy handling of conversation logs; accessibility and vulnerable-customer routes.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Thin: RAG over the existing help center for informational intents only, no account actions — weeks. Account-action automation is the long pole (API integrations, verification, caps). Content remediation is chronically underestimated.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Agent workforce fear must be addressed with explicit role evolution (escalation specialists, bot trainers); customer adoption depends on the bot *earning* the first turn — forced containment (hiding the human) measurably damages satisfaction and drove the Klarna reversal (2025).
14. **Documented deployments** — Klarna (figures above; 2024, with 2025 rebalancing). Intercom Fin and comparable products report resolution rates in the ~50% range on suitable volumes (vendor-reported, 2024–2025; not independently audited). The NBER agent-assist RCT (2023) sits adjacent (dossier 9). Sector adoption is broad; honest deployments distinguish *containment* from *resolution*.
15. **Failure modes** — Hallucinated policy (legal liability); deflection theater (containment counted as success while repeat contacts rise); escalation dead-ends destroying CSAT; stale knowledge; over-broad launch scope burning trust in week one; brand damage from viral bot failures.
16. **Variants** — Informational-only FAQ RAG (lowest risk) → account-action automation → proactive conversational care; voice vs chat; B2B (lower volume, higher complexity — assisted beats automated).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Dominantly bought (Intercom Fin, Zendesk AI agents, Salesforce Agentforce, Sierra, Decagon); in-house builds where deep proprietary actions matter; either way the intent-scope policy, content pipeline, and QA sampling are in-house.
18. **Key references** — Klarna press release (2024) and 2025 follow-up coverage; Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024); vendor resolution-rate methodology docs; NBER w31161 (2023).

### 9. Agent-assist

1. **Name** — Agent-assist (copilot for customer-service agents).
2. **Use case solved** — Raising agent productivity and consistency by generating suggested responses, surfacing knowledge, and automating wrap-up — while the human stays on the interaction.
3. **Methodology composed from** — LLM/RAG generation (retrieval over KB + conversation context → suggested replies/next steps) + summarization + real-time transcription (voice); the copilot pattern (dossier 33) specialized to contact centers.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Suggestion, never auto-send**: the correctness boundary is that the agent commits every message; UI must make editing frictionless or agents rubber-stamp. (ii) Grounding on current policy with source display so agents can verify in one glance. (iii) **Skill-distribution awareness**: the documented productivity gain concentrates in novice agents (+34% for the least experienced vs +14% average — NBER, 2023); rollout and training should target accordingly, and expert agents must be able to ignore suggestions costlessly. (iv) Real-time latency (suggestions after the agent has already typed are worthless). (v) Auto-summary accuracy audited by sampling (wrap-up notes feed CRM records and downstream analytics — silent corruption risk). (vi) PII minimization in prompts/logs.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Suggested responses, retrieved policy snippets, guided workflows for procedures, auto-generated wrap-up/disposition codes, real-time compliance nudges (required disclosures). Decision rules: which intents get suggestions, compliance-critical scripts locked verbatim, QA sampling quotas.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Agent desktop (embedded panel in Zendesk/Salesforce/Genesys/Five9/NICE), knowledge platform, QA tooling, WFM (handle-time changes feed staffing models).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Agents consume continuously; team leads coach with the same suggestions; knowledge managers curate the corpus; QA measures adherence. Augments rather than replaces the agent workforce — politically the easiest family-B solution to land.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Human always in the loop by construction; escalation unchanged; the design question is nudge intensity (passive panel vs interruptive prompts) — over-nudging measurably degrades expert performance.
9. **Measurement loop** — The reference RCT: staggered rollout across 5,179 agents at a Fortune 500 software firm found +14% issues resolved per hour on average, +34% for novice/low-skill agents, improved customer sentiment, fewer supervisor escalations, and 8.6% lower agent attrition (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, NBER w31161, 2023) — the cleanest causal evidence for any LLM-era solution. Programs should replicate the design: staggered rollout = built-in experiment. Guardrails: quality scores, compliance adherence, suggestion-acceptance rate (too high = rubber-stamping, too low = uselessness).
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time (sub-second suggestion latency); retrieval index near-live; conversation transcripts streamed; monitoring of acceptance, edit distance, latency, and per-cohort productivity.
11. **Governance & safety** — Recording/transcription consent; agent-performance data governance (works councils in EU — assist data must not silently become surveillance); compliance-script integrity.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Weeks-to-months: vendor panels deploy fast; the long pole is knowledge-base quality and coaching workflow changes.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Frame as agent tooling, not monitoring; involve top agents in tuning (their playbook becomes the model's); the NBER evidence that novices gain most (2023) implies pairing assist rollout with revised onboarding — firms captured the value partly by compressing ramp time.
14. **Documented deployments** — The NBER Fortune-500 deployment (2023; figures above). Contact-center suites (NICE, Genesys, Salesforce, Google CCAI) document large-scale rollouts (vendor-reported gains typically in the 10–30% handle-time range; treat as directional). Klarna's post-2025 model pairs assist for humans with bounded automation (2025 coverage).
15. **Failure modes** — Rubber-stamping wrong suggestions (quality collapse at high acceptance rates); knowledge rot propagating at scale; expert-agent slowdown from interruptive UX; surveillance backlash; wrap-up auto-notes corrupting CRM data unnoticed.
16. **Variants** — Voice (real-time transcription + assist) vs chat; compliance-heavy variants (collections, insurance — scripts locked); multilingual assist; blended with deflection (bot resolves, assist handles escalations).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Dominantly bought (contact-center platform add-ons); differentiation is knowledge quality, not model quality; in-house only at very large scale or unusual privacy constraints.
18. **Key references** — Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, "Generative AI at Work" (NBER w31161, 2023; NBER Digest 2023 for the 8.6% attrition figure); contact-center vendor architecture docs.

## Family C — Forecast-to-action

### 10. Demand forecasting → replenishment & inventory

1. **Name** — Demand forecasting → replenishment/inventory (includes hierarchical forecast reconciliation for S&OP as its planning variant).
2. **Use case solved** — Buying/positioning the right stock at the right node at the right time — fewer stockouts *and* less surplus.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Forecasting methodology (feature-based global models — gradient boosting/deep nets across all SKU×location series — or classical statistical baselines) + inventory optimization (safety stock, order policy) + hierarchical reconciliation; the forecast is an input to an *ordering policy*, which is the actual solution.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Censored demand**: sales ≠ demand when stocked out; uncensoring (imputing lost demand) or availability-aware training is mandatory or the model learns to under-order exactly where it failed before. (ii) **Probabilistic, not point, forecasts**: replenishment needs quantiles (service-level targets consume P90s, not means); point-forecast MAPE optimization is a category error. (iii) **Hierarchical coherence**: SKU×store forecasts must reconcile with category/region/network totals (MinT-style reconciliation) or planning meetings relitigate numbers — the S&OP variant lives here. (iv) Intermittent-demand handling (Croston-class or global models) for long-tail SKUs. (v) Promotion/price/cannibalization features with point-in-time discipline (the promo calendar as known-in-advance covariates). (vi) New-SKU cold start via attribute-based similarity. (vii) Temporal validation across seasons and regime shifts (COVID-era data taught everyone that stale regimes must be down-weighted, not averaged in). (viii) Lead-time and pack-size constraints inside the ordering policy, not bolted on.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Automated purchase/transfer orders within policy bounds; safety-stock updates; allocation decisions; markdown triggers on projected overstock; exception queues for planner review. Decision rules: service-level targets per segment, order-quantity caps, budget/open-to-buy constraints, human review above spend thresholds or on anomaly flags.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — ERP/replenishment modules (SAP, Oracle, Blue Yonder, RELEX, ToolsGroup, o9, Kinaxis), WMS for allocation, supplier EDI for orders, S&OP planning platforms.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Demand planners shift from generating numbers to managing exceptions and enriching with market intelligence (Danone reported a 50% reduction in planner workload; ToolsGroup case, 2012); S&OP monthly cadence consumes the reconciled hierarchy; buyers act daily.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Auto-ordering for stable/low-value SKUs (Otto let its system order ~200,000 items/month from third parties with no human touch — Economist/Harvard case, 2016–2017); planner review for new items, promotions, and high-spend orders; forecast overrides tracked and scored (forecast-value-added analysis routinely shows many human overrides subtract accuracy — the FVA discipline is part of the wiring).
9. **Measurement loop** — Forecast accuracy (quantile loss, not just MAPE) is a diagnostic, not the KPI; the business loop measures service level, stockout rate, inventory turns, waste/obsolescence against a phased rollout (by category/region) or against FVA baselines. Documented magnitudes: Danone — 20% forecast-error reduction (to 92% accuracy), 30% lost-sales reduction, 30% obsolescence reduction, service level 98.6% (ToolsGroup case study, 2011–2012; vendor-reported); Otto — ~90% 30-day forecast accuracy, ~20% surplus-stock reduction, ~2M fewer returns/year (Economist 2017 / Harvard case; ComputerWeekly 2013 reported ~40% forecasting improvement and ~€10M expected annual savings); Otto's later Google Cloud stack reported up to 30% additional accuracy improvement (Google Cloud case study, 2020s; vendor-reported); a large US mass-market retailer's multi-horizon RNN + auto-replenishment reported 16% stockout reduction, 10% turnover improvement, 10% logistics-cost reduction (Supply Chain Dive-cited case, 2025).
10. **Operational architecture** — Batch daily (intraday for perishables/fresh); horizon-appropriate cadences (operational daily, tactical weekly, strategic monthly); retraining weekly-to-monthly; monitoring: per-segment accuracy drift, bias (systematic over/under), stockout-feedback contamination.
11. **Governance & safety** — Financial controls on automated ordering (spend caps, segregation of duties); food-waste/ESG reporting where relevant; supplier fairness in allocation.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Thin: probabilistic forecasts + exception-based planner workflow on top of existing ordering, one category, a quarter. Auto-ordering integration is the long pole (ERP change management). Danone-scale programs run 1–2 years.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Planner trust via FVA transparency (show where overrides help vs hurt); phased category rollout; the recurring failure is planners re-overriding everything, reverting the system to expensive decoration.
14. **Documented deployments** — Danone (2011–2012, figures above); Otto (2013–2017 and 2020s, figures above); large US retailer (2025, above); McKinsey-attributed sector estimates of AI forecasting cutting lost sales and inventory costs substantially (e.g. "65% fewer revenue losses, up to 40% lower inventory costs" — quoted via secondary sources, 2020s; treat as consulting estimates).
15. **Failure modes** — Optimizing MAPE while service levels stagnate (wrong objective); censored-demand death spiral; override culture; promo-calendar disconnects (marketing surprises the forecast); regime shifts (COVID) breaking unmonitored models at maximum stakes.
16. **Variants** — Grocery/fresh (intraday, waste-focused), fashion (one-shot buys, markdown coupling), manufacturing (component MRP), pharma (regulatory stock rules); S&OP reconciliation variant where the "action" is the aligned plan.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Mid-market buys (RELEX, ToolsGroup, Blue Yonder, o9, Kinaxis); large retail hybrids (in-house global models + bought execution); pure in-house at digital-native scale. The ordering-policy design and FVA discipline are in-house either way.
18. **Key references** — Danone/ToolsGroup case (2012); Otto coverage (Economist 2017; Harvard D3 case; ComputerWeekly 2013; Google Cloud case); M5 competition findings on global models; Hyndman et al. on hierarchical reconciliation; FVA literature (Gilliland).

### 11. Sales & cash-flow forecasting → capacity, quota & treasury actions

1. **Name** — Sales & cash-flow forecasting driving capacity, quota, and treasury actions (two sibling wirings of one methodology, presented together because the correctness core is identical and the action layer distinguishes them).
2. **Use case solved** — (a) Revenue/pipeline forecasts that set hiring, capacity, and quotas; (b) cash-position forecasts that drive borrowing, investing, and FX/liquidity actions.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Forecasting methodology on tabular business data (pipeline features + gradient boosting for deal-level rollups; time-series/global models for cash flows) + hierarchical aggregation; SHAP-style driver attribution for the "why".
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **CRM-hygiene leakage** (as dossier 3): stage/amount fields mutate post-outcome; deal-level features must be point-in-time from field history. (ii) **Sandbagging/incentive distortion in labels**: rep-entered close dates and stages are gamed; models must lean on behavioral signals (activity, engagement) over self-reported fields. (iii) Small-n at the aggregate level: quarterly company-level series are short — hierarchical pooling across segments beats one thin top-level series. (iv) Cash forecasting: entity resolution across bank accounts/ERPs, calendar effects (paydays, tax dates) as first-class features, and fat-tail awareness (a single large receipt dominates — quantile forecasts, scenario bands). (v) Backtesting with strict as-of reconstruction (what did the pipeline look like on day N of last 12 quarters?). (vi) Regime honesty: macro shifts break sales models silently; ensemble with judgment bands rather than false precision.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Sales: hiring/ramp plans, quota setting, territory rebalancing, in-quarter interventions (deal-risk flags to managers, pipeline-generation sprints when coverage is short). Treasury: daily/weekly cash positioning, short-term borrowing/investing decisions, FX hedging quantities, covenant-headroom alerts. Decision rules: forecast bands trigger playbooks (coverage <3× → pipeline sprint; cash below buffer P10 → draw revolver), with CFO approval gates on financial actions.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — CRM + revenue-intelligence platforms (Clari, Gong, Salesforce forecasting) for sales; TMS (Kyriba, GTreasury), ERP, banking APIs for treasury; planning tools (Anaplan, Pigment) for capacity/quota.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Weekly forecast call re-anchored on model + exceptions (replacing anecdote rollups); RevOps owns the sales loop; treasury runs daily positioning with the forecast as baseline; FP&A reconciles both into planning.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Forecasts advise; humans commit (quota and borrowing decisions are human with model bands); automation appropriate only for mechanical sweeps/investments within pre-approved treasury policy limits.
9. **Measurement loop** — Forecast-accuracy tracking against a documented baseline (rep rollup / naive model) each quarter — the FVA discipline again; treasury measures realized vs forecast cash and the cost saved (reduced idle cash, avoided emergency borrowing — Danone North America reported 96% monthly receivables-forecast accuracy with AI, HighRadius case, 2021; vendor-reported). Guardrails: forecast-driven decisions logged and reviewed (did the pipeline sprint triggered in Q2 pay back?).
10. **Operational architecture** — Batch: daily (cash) / weekly (sales); no latency pressure; retraining quarterly; monitoring of bias drift (persistent over-forecasting is an organizational, not statistical, signal).
11. **Governance & safety** — Forecasts feeding guidance/disclosure enter SOX-adjacent controls; treasury actions bounded by board-approved policy; auditability of model-informed financial decisions.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Sales: a quarter to stand up deal-scoring + rollup with backtests (given CRM history). Treasury: 1–2 quarters (bank-data plumbing is the long pole). Thin version: model forecast presented alongside human forecast for two quarters — trust before wiring.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Sales leaders' judgment must be repositioned, not replaced: the model sets the baseline, leaders adjust with logged rationale — scoring both builds the case honestly. Documented pattern: forecast tools ignored where comp attaches to the human number.
14. **Documented deployments** — Revenue-intelligence category at scale (Clari/Gong customer bases; vendor-reported accuracy improvements); Danone NA receivables forecasting at 96% accuracy (HighRadius, 2021; vendor-reported); enterprise cash-forecasting ML adoption documented across treasury surveys (2020s; qualitative). Independent audited lift numbers remain scarce in this family — the honest gap; the measured evidence is strongest where firms publish FVA-style internal baselines.
15. **Failure modes** — Model laundering of sandbagged inputs; forecast theater (numbers presented, decisions unchanged); quota decisions blamed on the model, destroying trust; treasury false precision leading to under-buffering (fat tails ignored).
16. **Variants** — Workforce planning → scheduling is the same wiring aimed at labor: demand/volume forecast → shift schedules in WFM systems (contact centers, retail, warehouses) — covered here as the capacity-action variant; SaaS ARR forecasting; project-services capacity.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Sales: buy (Clari, Gong, Salesforce). Treasury: buy (Kyriba, HighRadius) with in-house models at large corporates. Capacity/WFM: buy (NICE, Verint, Legion, Quinyx — the latter two ML-native for retail scheduling).
18. **Key references** — Revenue-intelligence vendor methodology docs; HighRadius/Danone case (2021); FVA literature; treasury-forecasting practitioner surveys (AFP, 2020s).

### 12. Load forecasting → grid & energy operations

1. **Name** — Load/renewables forecasting → grid & energy operations.
2. **Use case solved** — Predicting electricity demand and renewable output so unit commitment, dispatch, trading, and balancing keep the grid stable and costs down.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Forecasting methodology (weather-driven ML: gradient boosting/deep nets on weather forecasts + calendars + telemetry) feeding optimization (unit commitment/dispatch) and market bidding.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Weather-forecast-as-input discipline**: train on *forecast* weather as it was known at decision time, not actuals — training on actual weather then serving on forecasts is this domain's signature leakage. (ii) Extreme-event fidelity: average MAPE hides the tails that matter (heat waves, cold snaps); tail-weighted evaluation and scenario ensembles. (iii) Structural drift: solar-behind-the-meter growth, EV adoption, and efficiency trends invalidate old load shapes — rolling retraining with trend terms. (iv) Hierarchies: node → zone → system coherence for dispatch. (v) Probabilistic outputs for reserve-margin decisions. (vi) Holiday/anomaly calendars as first-class features. (vii) Physical plausibility constraints on outputs.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Day-ahead unit commitment, intraday dispatch adjustments, reserve procurement, market bids/offers, demand-response activation, maintenance-window scheduling. Decision rules: reserve margins tied to forecast-uncertainty bands; operator confirmation for major commitments; automated re-dispatch within tight bounds.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — EMS/SCADA, market-bidding platforms, demand-response management systems, trading desks' ETRM.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Control-room operators consume hourly; trading desks act on price/volume forecasts; planning consumes long-horizon variants. The forecast desk cadence is 24/7.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Operators retain dispatch authority (grid-code requirement); automation limited to advisory ranking and small-bound adjustments; escalation to manual procedures on model anomaly.
9. **Measurement loop** — Continuous scoring against realized load (day-ahead MAPE typically ~2–4% for mature ISOs, with ML methods contributing fractional-point improvements that are economically large — ISO reporting, 2010s–2020s; estimate ranges, not source-verified per-ISO here); balancing-cost and imbalance-penalty tracking; DeepMind/Google reported ML wind-output forecasting (36-hour horizon) increased the market value of wind energy by ~20% vs no-forecast baseline (DeepMind blog, 2019; company-reported).
10. **Operational architecture** — Near-real-time streaming telemetry; rolling intraday re-forecasts (5–60 min cadence); day-ahead batch; automated retraining with strict champion/challenger promotion; monitoring on tail-day performance specifically.
11. **Governance & safety** — Grid codes and regulator oversight (NERC/ENTSO-E); model changes to operational forecasts go through formal change control; trading-compliance separation (forecast desk vs trading book).
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Utilities have decades of statistical baseline: ML programs deliver incremental accuracy in 1–2 quarters per zone; the long pole is operational certification, not modeling.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Control rooms adopt via long side-by-side (model vs incumbent forecast) with tail-day post-mortems; a single bad extreme-event miss can set adoption back years — stage the trust accordingly.
14. **Documented deployments** — DeepMind wind value +~20% (2019; company-reported); Google data-center cooling — the adjacent load-optimization case — cut cooling energy ~40% in initial deployment (DeepMind/Google blog, 2016) settling to ~30% average in autonomous operation (DeepMind, 2018); ISO/TSO ML adoption is documented across NYISO/National Grid ESO programs (2020s; qualitative).
15. **Failure modes** — Tail-event misses with outsized cost (the only failures that matter); weather-input leakage inflating offline results; structural drift (EV/solar) rotting seasonal models; operator distrust after unexplained anomalies (explainability is operationally required).
16. **Variants** — System-operator load forecasting; renewables-generation forecasting (wind/solar); retailer/aggregator portfolio forecasting for trading; large-consumer (data-center, industrial) load management; microgrid/EV-charging orchestration.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — ISOs/utilities blend in-house forecast desks with vendor systems (Itron, AWS/Google forecasting stacks, specialist weather-analytics vendors); renewables forecasting has a dedicated vendor market (DNV, Meteologica-class).
18. **Key references** — DeepMind wind blog (2019) and cooling posts (2016, 2018); GEFCom forecasting-competition literature (Hong et al.); ISO forecast-performance reports.


## Family D — Risk decisioning

### 13. Real-time transaction fraud decisioning

1. **Name** — Real-time transaction fraud decisioning (approve / challenge / block).
2. **Use case solved** — Stopping fraudulent payments in-flight while approving good customers — the two-sided cost problem (fraud loss vs false declines).
3. **Methodology composed from** — Tabular supervised methodology at streaming speed: real-time feature aggregation (velocity counters, device/graph signals) → gradient-boosted/deep classifier → calibrated score → **policy engine** (rules + thresholds) → decision in-line. The most mature closed-loop solution in enterprise AI.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Label maturity/censoring**: fraud labels arrive as chargebacks weeks-to-months later; training must respect label-maturity windows (score-time features, outcome known only after maturation) and interim proxy labels handled explicitly. (ii) **Selection bias from own decisions**: blocked transactions have no outcome label — the system never learns what it declined; mitigations: small randomized approve-throughs on gray-zone traffic (exploration), reject inference, and monitoring the gray zone. (iii) **Point-in-time velocity features** computed in the streaming layer exactly as in training replay (train/serve consistency is *the* engineering problem; feature stores with online/offline parity exist largely because of this solution). (iv) **Extreme class imbalance** (bp-level fraud rates): PR-AUC/recall-at-fixed-FPR as the metric, cost-weighted thresholds. (v) **Adversarial drift**: fraudsters probe and adapt in days — champion/challenger retraining weekly or faster, drift alarms on score distributions and attack-pattern features. (vi) **Entity/graph resolution** across cards, devices, merchants (fraud rings). (vii) **Latency budget as a correctness constraint**: the full feature+model+policy path must fit the authorization window (Stripe evaluates >1,000 signals in <100 ms — Stripe engineering material, 2020s). (viii) **Two-sided cost calibration**: false declines are projected to cost merchants more than fraud itself (Visa projects global false-decline losses exceeding $264B by 2027 — Visa insight, 2023–2024; industry research puts total merchant cost at ~$4.61 per $1 of fraud — 2026 summary of LexisNexis-style studies), so threshold-setting is an economic optimization, not a recall maximization.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — In-line approve / step-up challenge (3DS, OTP) / block; post-auth queue for manual review; merchant/account-level actions (velocity limits, holds); case creation for confirmed fraud. Decision rules live in a rules+model policy layer: score bands × amount × segment; challenge budgets (friction caps); allow/deny lists; regulatory rules (SCA under PSD2) as hard constraints.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Payment gateway/authorization stream (the decision executes inside it), case-management for analysts, chargeback/dispute systems closing the label loop, customer-notification rails (fraud alerts), SIEM-adjacent monitoring.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Fraud-operations analysts work the review queue and tune rules; fraud strategy owns thresholds vs loss/approval targets; data science owns models; a weekly loss-review reconciles fraud losses, false-decline estimates, and friction metrics. Replaces static rules-only stacks (which still run *inside* the policy layer as guardrails).
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Full automation for clear approve/block (volume and latency make review impossible); human review for mid-score high-value cases; analysts feed confirmed labels back same-day. Escalation: attack-in-progress alarms page fraud ops to deploy emergency rules — the humans manage the policy, not the transaction.
9. **Measurement loop** — Continuous champion/challenger on live traffic; the exploration slice quantifies missed-good/missed-bad; KPIs: fraud bps, approval rate, challenge rate, manual-review rate, chargeback rate — reported *jointly* (any one alone is gameable). Documented magnitudes: Stripe Radar reports 32–38% average fraud reduction with a claimed ~0.1% false-positive rate, and blocked 20.9M fraudulent transactions worth $917M over Black Friday/Cyber Monday 2024 (Stripe/Chargebacks911 statistics, 2024–2026; company-reported); Stripe's ML-driven Adaptive Acceptance recovered ~$6B in would-be false declines in 2024 (+60% YoY; company-reported); Visa's Decision Manager prevented an estimated $22B in potential fraud in 2021 (Visa, 2021); PayPal's deep-learning models improved fraud AUC from 0.89 to 0.947 over a decade of engineered features (H2O.ai case study, ~2020).
10. **Operational architecture** — Streaming, real-time (<100 ms budget); online feature store + model service in the authorization path; shadow scoring for challengers; retraining weekly or faster; monitoring: score drift, feature-pipeline health (a broken velocity counter is a silent catastrophe), attack-signature dashboards, label-maturation tracking.
11. **Governance & safety** — Model risk management (SR 11-7-class validation in banks); adverse-action/dispute processes; PSD2/SCA compliance; fairness monitoring on decline rates across demographics; full decision audit trail (every score, rule hit, and action logged).
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Buying (Stripe Radar-class, embedded in the PSP) is instant-on; building in-house is a multi-quarter program dominated by streaming-feature infrastructure and case-management integration, not modeling. Thin in-house version: batch-scored post-auth review prioritization before attempting in-line decisioning.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Fraud analysts embrace models that demonstrably cut queue noise; the friction fight is with *growth* teams over decline rates — resolved only by the joint-KPI reporting above. Rules-team → model-policy-team evolution needs explicit reskilling.
14. **Documented deployments** — Stripe Radar (figures above; 2024–2026). Visa Decision Manager ($22B potential fraud prevented, 2021). PayPal deep-learning fraud stack (AUC gains above; ~2020; scale: 400M+ accounts). Mastercard Decision Intelligence operates at network scale (public quantified outcomes not verified here). These are the canonical closed-loop deployments of the tabular methodology.
15. **Failure modes** — False-decline blindness (fraud KPI looks great while good customers are silently lost — the measured $264B problem); feedback starvation (no exploration → model calcifies against yesterday's fraud); feature-pipeline breakage mid-attack; rules sprawl re-accreting around the model until nobody can explain a decision; adversarial adaptation outpacing retraining cadence.
16. **Variants** — Card-not-present e-commerce; issuer-side authorization scoring; ACH/instant-payment fraud (faster rails = harder labels; Stripe reported 20% ACH / 42% SEPA fraud reductions on expansion — Stripe, 2023–2024); ATO (account-takeover) detection; first-party/friendly-fraud (label ambiguity dominates); marketplace seller fraud.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — PSP-embedded (Stripe Radar, Adyen), network products (Visa/Mastercard), specialist vendors (Forter, Sift, Featurespace, Feedzai), bank in-house builds atop vendor cores. Mid-market buys; top-tier issuers/PSPs build. The policy layer, review ops, and loss governance are always in-house.
18. **Key references** — Stripe Radar engineering posts (2020s); Visa fraud-detection insights (2021–2024); H2O/PayPal case (2020); false-decline research (Visa $264B projection; LexisNexis cost-of-fraud series); Featurespace/Feedzai technical whitepapers.

### 14. AML alert triage & investigation

1. **Name** — AML alert triage & investigation augmentation.
2. **Use case solved** — Drowning compliance teams: rules-based transaction monitoring generates alerts that are overwhelmingly false positives (~90–95%+ by industry consensus; regulatory and vendor commentary, 2010s–2020s); ML re-ranks, enriches, and suppresses so investigators spend time where SARs actually live.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Tabular supervised methodology (historical alert dispositions as labels → gradient boosting → risk-ranked queue) + network/graph analytics (entity linking) + increasingly LLM summarization for case narratives; the *rules stay* — the model sits on top of them (regulatory reality).
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Labels are dispositions, not truth**: "closed, no SAR" reflects investigator behavior and staffing, not ground truth; label quality review and disposition-consistency analysis come first. (ii) **Regulator-facing conservatism**: suppression (auto-closing alerts) demands *below-the-line testing* — periodically investigating samples of suppressed/low-score alerts to prove nothing material is lost; this is the domain's signature correctness mechanism and is expected by examiners. (iii) **Entity resolution across accounts/parties** is half the value (structuring only appears when the network is assembled). (iv) Class imbalance (SAR rates of 1–5% of alerts). (v) Typology drift (new laundering patterns) monitored via unsupervised anomaly overlays. (vi) Point-in-time features (customer risk ratings as-of alert date). (vii) Model governance documentation to bank-examiner standard from day one — an undocumented model is an unusable model in this domain.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Alert risk-ranking (investigator queue order), auto-enrichment (case pre-population: linked entities, prior alerts, KYC context), hibernation/suppression of ultra-low scores (with below-the-line testing), escalation to enhanced-due-diligence, SAR filing recommendations. Decision rules: score bands map to queue tiers and SLA clocks; suppression thresholds ratified by compliance officers, never by data science.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Transaction-monitoring platforms (Actimize, Oracle FCCM, SAS AML, Napier, ComplyAdvantage), case-management, KYC/CDD systems, SAR e-filing rails (FinCEN/goAML).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — L1 investigators consume the ranked queue; L2/compliance officers approve SARs; the MLRO owns policy; model risk management validates annually. Cadence: daily queues, monthly threshold reviews, annual validation. Augments (never replaces) the regulated investigation process.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Humans make every SAR decision (regulatory requirement); automation limited to ranking, enrichment, and governed suppression; escalation paths mirror existing compliance hierarchy.
9. **Measurement loop** — SAR yield per investigator-hour, alert-to-SAR conversion by score band, below-the-line test results (the guardrail), backlog/SLA metrics; phased rollout by segment with before/after productivity. Documented magnitude: HSBC's work with Ayasdi reported ~20% reduction in alerts requiring investigation without detection loss (case-study evidence, mid-2010s; vendor-linked); industry programs commonly report double-digit false-positive reductions (vendor-reported range; treat directionally).
10. **Operational architecture** — Batch (daily) scoring of the alert stream; no latency pressure; retraining quarterly under change control; monitoring of disposition drift, score-band conversion stability, and below-the-line findings.
11. **Governance & safety** — The heaviest governance in this compendium: model risk management (OCC/Fed expectations), examiner review, full auditability, documented threshold rationale, four-eyes on suppression changes; explainability required per alert (reason codes in the case file).
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — 6–12 months to production (validation and examiner comfort dominate); thin version: enrichment + ranking with *no* suppression — value from day one, minimal regulatory novelty; suppression follows after two below-the-line cycles.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Investigators adopt readily (the queue gets less miserable); compliance leadership needs the below-the-line evidence and peer-bank precedent; regulator engagement early (innovation-office dialogues) de-risks the program.
14. **Documented deployments** — HSBC/Ayasdi (~20% alert reduction; mid-2010s; case study). Large-bank ML alert-scoring programs are now standard practice per regulator innovation statements (FinCEN/OCC joint statement encouraging innovation, 2018) and vendor deployment bases (Actimize/SAS; 2020s; vendor-documented). Public audited numbers are rare because AML performance is examination-confidential — the honest cap on this dossier's evidence.
15. **Failure modes** — Disposition-label garbage canonizing past under-investigation; suppression without below-the-line testing (examiner findings, consent orders); model documented poorly → validation failure → shelfware; typology drift unmonitored (the model ranks yesterday's risk); productivity gains absorbed as headcount cuts leaving no capacity for the testing obligations.
16. **Variants** — Sanctions-screening hit triage (name-matching false positives; same wiring); fraud-AML convergence units; crypto-transaction monitoring (graph-heavy); LLM case-narrative drafting (2023+; human-signed).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Monitoring platforms bought; the triage/scoring layer both bought (vendor add-ons) and built (large banks) — in-house wins where entity graphs are proprietary; case management bought. Model validation capacity is the binding in-house constraint.
18. **Key references** — HSBC/Ayasdi case (mid-2010s); US regulators' joint statement on AML innovation (2018); Wolfsberg/FATF publications on ML in AML; vendor whitepapers (Actimize, Featurespace) for factual capability claims.

### 15. Credit underwriting & limit management

1. **Name** — ML credit underwriting & limit management.
2. **Use case solved** — Approving more good borrowers at better prices with equal-or-lower losses, and managing limits over the customer lifecycle.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Tabular supervised methodology under regulatory constraint: alternative + bureau data → engineered features → gradient boosting (often constrained/monotonic) → calibrated PD/LGD → decision policy (approve/price/limit) → adverse-action reason codes (SHAP-derived, mapped to compliant reason taxonomies).
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Reject inference**: outcomes exist only for approved loans; ignoring it trains the model on a selectively censored population — handled via reject-inference techniques, exploration lending (small controlled slices), or explicit population-stability caveats. (ii) **Outcome windows & vintage effects**: default is a slow label (12–36 months); vintage-based temporal validation, macro-cycle awareness (a model trained in benign years must be stress-checked), and through-the-cycle vs point-in-time calibration choices. (iii) **Monotonicity constraints** (risk must not decrease as debt-to-income rises) both for sanity and examiner defensibility. (iv) **Fair-lending correctness as a hard constraint**: disparate-impact testing, adverse-action reason validity (ECOA/Reg B), and searches for less-discriminatory alternatives — fairness here is a legal correctness property, not an ethical garnish; the Apple Card episode (NYDFS investigation into gender disparity in algorithmic credit limits, 2019) shows perception + opacity alone create regulatory events. (v) Calibration to economic capital (PDs feed pricing and provisioning — rank order is not enough). (vi) Data-source stability governance (alternative data vendors change schemas; bureau attribute drift). (vii) Train/serve consistency with the loan-origination system's real-time data pulls.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Approve/decline, risk-based pricing, credit-limit assignment, and lifecycle actions (limit increases/decreases, pre-approvals); counteroffer logic. Decision rules: cutoff scores by product/segment tied to loss appetite; pricing grids; policy overlays (knockout rules) that pre-empt the model; manual-review bands for near-cutoff or thin-file cases; ~70% of Upstart-platform loans were reported fully automated with no manual touch (Upstart via Federal Reserve comment letter, 2022).
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Loan-origination systems, decision engines (FICO Blaze, Experian PowerCurve, in-house), core banking for limits, adverse-action letter generation, collections systems downstream (dossier 17).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Credit risk committee owns cutoffs and appetite; underwriters handle review bands and exceptions; model risk management validates; fair-lending compliance tests quarterly. Replaces scorecard-only underwriting and manual file review at volume.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — High-volume consumer credit: automated within policy, human review for exceptions/appeals (a legally required path); commercial credit: model-assisted human underwriting. Second-look processes for declined applicants near the boundary.
9. **Measurement loop** — Vintage curves vs projections (the ground-truth loop, 12–36 months); champion/challenger with swap-set analysis (who does the new model approve that the old declined, and how do they perform?); documented magnitudes: Upstart's ML model vs a traditional scorecard — 27% more applicants approved at 16% lower average APRs, with near-prime (FICO 620–660) applicants approved roughly twice as often (CFPB no-action-letter update, 2019, covering 2017–2019); Upstart internal studies reported to regulators claim ~75% lower loss rates at constant approval rates (Upstart via CFPB/Fed filings, 2019–2022; company-reported); Zest AI's Commonwealth Credit Union case: +15% funded auto loans at no added risk, automated decisioning 6%→30% (Zest case study, ~2021; vendor-reported). Guardrails: approval-rate and default-rate by protected-class proxy, override rates, population-stability index.
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time decisioning (seconds) at application; batch lifecycle scoring monthly; retraining infrequent (annual-ish) because every change triggers validation + fair-lending re-testing; monitoring: PSI on inputs, score drift, early-delinquency roll rates as fast proxies for slow labels.
11. **Governance & safety** — The regulated maximum: SR 11-7 model validation, ECOA/Reg B adverse-action reasons, FCRA permissible use, fair-lending exams, EU AI Act high-risk classification for credit scoring (obligations phasing 2025–2027); full decision audit; documented less-discriminatory-alternative searches.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — 9–18 months in a bank (validation + compliance dominate); fintechs move faster at regulatory risk; thin version: challenger model in shadow on current applications for two quarters, generating the swap-set evidence that funds the program.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Credit officers trust monotonic, reason-coded models validated on their own vintages; the cultural shift is from "the scorecard we've always used" to championed challengers — driven by the swap-set economics, not accuracy metrics.
14. **Documented deployments** — Upstart (CFPB-verified access/pricing figures, 2019; automation share, 2022). Zest AI credit-union deployments (vendor-reported, 2021–2023). Large-bank internal ML underwriting is widespread but examination-confidential (qualitative). The counter-case: Apple Card/Goldman NYDFS investigation (2019) — opacity cost, even with no finding of intentional discrimination (NYDFS report, 2021).
15. **Failure modes** — Benign-cycle models meeting a downturn (vintage risk); reject-inference neglect quietly narrowing the approved population; fair-lending findings (existential); alternative-data vendor drift; adverse-action reason codes that don't genuinely explain the decision (regulatory exposure); model-change paralysis (validation burden freezes improvement).
16. **Variants** — Consumer unsecured (most automated); credit cards + lifecycle limit management (behavioral scoring); SME lending (cash-flow/transaction data); BNPL (thin-file, real-time); mortgage (heaviest regulation, least automated); each shares the methodology, differs in label horizon and governance depth.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Banks build scorecards in-house on bought decision engines; fintech platforms (Upstart, Zest) sell model + platform to smaller lenders; bureaus (FICO/Experian) sell scores + attributes. Fair-lending testing capacity is the scarce in-house asset.
18. **Key references** — CFPB Upstart no-action-letter update (2019); Federal Reserve comment letters (2022); Zest AI case studies (2021–2023); NYDFS Apple Card report (2021); SR 11-7; EU AI Act Annex III (credit scoring).

### 16. Insurance claims triage & fraud referral

1. **Name** — Claims triage, straight-through processing & fraud referral (incl. claims-document adjudication support).
2. **Use case solved** — Routing every claim to the right path — instant pay, fast-track, standard adjudication, or SIU fraud investigation — cutting cycle time and leakage simultaneously.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Tabular supervised methodology (claim + policy + history features → severity and fraud-propensity models) + document/image extraction (photos → damage estimates; documents → structured fields, the IDP methodology of dossier 31) + decision policy for path assignment.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Label asymmetry on fraud**: confirmed fraud labels exist only where SIU investigated — the same selective-label problem as AML/credit; referral-outcome feedback and random-referral audit slices keep the model honest. (ii) **Severity develops over time** (bodily-injury claims mature for years): point-in-time snapshots of claim state, development-aware labels, and leakage discipline on fields populated late (reserve updates leak outcomes). (iii) Document/photo pipeline accuracy audited per field (an OCR'd policy number wrong 2% of the time corrupts entity joins silently). (iv) Entity resolution across claimants/providers/repair shops (fraud rings). (v) Straight-through-processing eligibility as conservative allowlists (clear coverage, low amount, verified identity, benign signals) — STP is a *policy* achievement, not a model achievement. (vi) Catastrophe-regime handling (hurricane claim surges shift every distribution; models need CAT modes/overrides).
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Path assignment (instant settle / fast-track / standard / complex), SIU referral with reason codes, reserve-setting suggestions, vendor dispatch (adjuster, repair network), document-request automation. Decision rules: STP caps by line and amount; fraud-referral thresholds balancing SIU capacity; mandatory human adjudication for injury/liability lines; CAT-event overrides.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Claims-management platforms (Guidewire ClaimCenter, Duck Creek), SIU case management, payment rails, repair-network platforms, document-intake (FNOL portals, email rooms).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Adjusters consume triage + pre-populated files; SIU consumes ranked referrals; claims leadership owns STP policy; actuarial monitors leakage. Replaces uniform manual adjudication queues.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — STP fully automated inside the allowlist (Lemonade publicizes instant AI settlement for a share of simple property claims — company communications, late 2010s–2020s; company-reported); everything else assisted; SIU referral always lands on human investigators; customer appeal path mandatory.
9. **Measurement loop** — Cycle time by path, leakage audits (re-adjudicating samples of STP claims — the below-the-line testing of this domain), fraud-referral hit rate vs SIU capacity, customer NPS by path; phased rollout by line of business. Guardrails: overpayment rate on STP, false-referral burden on honest customers, regulator complaint rates.
10. **Operational architecture** — Near-real-time at FNOL (first notice of loss) for path assignment; batch re-scoring as claims develop; retraining semi-annual + CAT overrides; monitoring of path-mix drift, STP leakage samples, referral conversion.
11. **Governance & safety** — Insurance regulators increasingly require algorithmic-claims governance (NAIC model bulletin on AI use, 2023; Colorado SB21-169 quantitative testing regime); unfair-claims-practices law constrains automated denials — the safe pattern automates *payment*, never denial; documented reason codes for referrals; fairness testing on referral rates.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Fraud-referral ranking on existing claims data: one-two quarters. STP requires policy, payment, and document plumbing: 2–4 quarters. Thin version: SIU queue re-ranking — no customer-facing change, immediate capacity value.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Adjusters fear dossier-quality erosion — pre-populated files that are *visibly right* win them; SIU teams co-design referral reasons; publicized instant-pay claims build customer acceptance (Lemonade made it a brand feature).
14. **Documented deployments** — Lemonade instant claims (share of simple claims settled in seconds; company-reported, 2010s–2020s). Tractable photo-based auto-damage estimating deployed at major carriers with cycle-time reductions (vendor-reported, magnitudes not independently verified here). Traditional carriers' fraud-analytics programs report double-digit hit-rate improvements (sector/vendor-reported; directional). Evidence caveat: this family's public numbers are largely company/vendor-reported.
15. **Failure modes** — Fraud model over-referring (SIU flooded, honest customers harassed, cycle time worsens — the guardrail metrics exist because this happened); STP leakage discovered late; automated-denial scandals and regulatory action (the industry's live third-rail); CAT events breaking path models when volume matters most; document-extraction silent corruption.
16. **Variants** — Auto physical damage (most automatable, photo-driven); property; travel/pet (highest STP shares); bodily injury/liability (assisted only); health claims (adjacent, heavier regulation); subrogation-opportunity detection as a sibling wiring.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Claims platforms bought (Guidewire ecosystem); fraud analytics both (FRISS, Shift Technology as vendors; large carriers build); photo-estimating bought (Tractable, CCC); STP policy design always in-house.
18. **Key references** — Lemonade transparency posts (2010s–2020s); Shift/FRISS factual product documentation; NAIC AI bulletin (2023); Colorado SB21-169 regime; claims-leakage practitioner literature.

### 17. Collections prioritization & treatment paths

1. **Name** — Collections prioritization & treatment optimization.
2. **Use case solved** — Recovering more delinquent debt at lower cost and lower customer harm by matching accounts to the right treatment (self-cure watch, digital nudge, call, hardship plan, agency placement).
3. **Methodology composed from** — Tabular supervised methodology (risk-of-roll, payment-propensity models) + uplift modeling for treatment assignment (the same propensity-vs-persuadability upgrade as churn) + contact-channel optimization.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Self-cure awareness**: many early-stage delinquents pay without contact; propensity targeting wastes spend and *annoys payers into complaints* — uplift modeling against treatment cost is the correct formulation (this is churn dossier 1's sleeping-dog logic with a regulator watching). (ii) Point-in-time balance/delinquency snapshots; label windows per bucket (roll from 30→60 within cycle). (iii) Contact-policy compliance as hard constraints in the decision layer (FDCPA/Reg F call-frequency caps in the US, vulnerable-customer rules in the UK) — the model may *never* be the thing enforcing law; the policy engine is. (iv) Hardship/vulnerability detection with generous recall (mis-treating a vulnerable customer is a conduct breach). (v) Selection bias from historical treatment assignment (past strategy shaped labels; randomized strategy tests are the clean source). (vi) Calibrated value predictions (expected recovery × cost) because placement decisions are economic.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Treatment assignment per account per cycle: no-contact watch, SMS/email nudge with self-serve payment link, agent call, restructure/hardship offer, settlement authority bands, agency/debt-sale placement. Decision rules: cost-tiered treatment ladders, contact caps (legal + policy), settlement floors, vulnerability overrides to human specialists.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Collections platforms/dialers (CGI, FICO Debt Manager, C&R), digital-collections layers (self-serve portals), core banking, hardship-workflow tools, agency-management systems.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Collections ops consumes daily queues; strategy team owns treatment ladders and champion/challenger tests; compliance reviews contact policies; agents handle call treatments and hardship conversations. Replaces bucket-based uniform dialing.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Digital treatments automated; calls human; hardship/vulnerability always human; settlement beyond bands needs approval. Escalation to specialist teams on distress signals.
9. **Measurement loop** — Champion/challenger strategy testing is native to this domain (collections ran randomized strategy tests before "A/B testing" was a phrase): recovery rate, cost-to-collect, roll rates, complaint rates per strategy cell; uplift-measured treatment effects; guardrails: complaints, hardship-detection recall, regulator metrics (FCA outcomes reporting).
10. **Operational architecture** — Daily batch scoring by delinquency cycle; dialer integration for queue ordering; retraining quarterly; monitoring of roll-rate drift (macro-sensitive) and strategy-cell contamination.
11. **Governance & safety** — Consumer-protection regulation is the frame (FDCPA/Reg F, FCA CONC + Consumer Duty); fairness testing on treatment assignment; auditability of every contact decision; vulnerable-customer policy supremacy.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Fast where a collections platform exists: risk-ranked queues in weeks; uplift-based treatment ladders in one-two quarters; digital self-serve build is the long pole and the biggest ROI (cheapest treatment).
13. **Adoption & change management** — Collections agents accept ranked queues readily; the strategy team's shift from bucket rules to test-and-learn cells is the real change; comp plans tied to recovery-per-call must be adjusted or agents fight digital deflection of easy accounts.
14. **Documented deployments** — Digital-first collections programs report large recovery and complaint improvements (fintech and vendor case studies, e.g. digital-collections vendors reporting double-digit recovery lifts — vendor-reported, 2020s; treat directionally); bank champion/challenger collections optimization is standard practice documented in credit-risk literature (qualitative); public audited numbers are scarce because collections performance is competitively and reputationally sensitive — honest gap.
15. **Failure modes** — Propensity targeting harassing self-payers (complaints, conduct risk); contact-cap violations when the policy engine and dialer disagree (regulatory fines); macro shifts breaking roll models (2020 forbearance waves); strategy cells contaminated by ops overrides, destroying the test readout; digital channels cannibalizing measurement (payment attributed to the last nudge).
16. **Variants** — Early-stage (digital, uplift-heavy) vs late-stage (placement/sale economics); utilities/telco (service-suspension levers); BNPL (high-volume micro-balances); debt purchasers (portfolio valuation variant).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Platforms bought; strategy/uplift models built in-house at banks, bought by mid-market (TrueAccord-class digital-collections vendors sell the whole loop); the treatment ladder and compliance wiring are in-house.
18. **Key references** — Reg F/FDCPA and FCA CONC texts (the binding design constraints); uplift literature (as dossier 1); digital-collections vendor case studies (2020s; factual claims only).


## Family E — Asset & operations

### 18. Predictive maintenance → work-order generation

1. **Name** — Predictive maintenance (PdM) → work-order generation & scheduling.
2. **Use case solved** — Fixing equipment just before it fails instead of after (reactive) or on a calendar (preventive) — less unplanned downtime, lower maintenance cost, longer asset life.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Anomaly detection + tabular supervised methodology on sensor/telemetry data (condition indicators → feature engineering over time windows → failure-probability or remaining-useful-life models → explanation → **work order**); the report of the tabular methodology becomes a CMMS work order — the defining wiring of this family.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Failure labels are scarce and dirty**: real failures are rare (well-maintained fleets fail rarely — the label paradox), and failure events live in free-text work-order logs that must be mined and aligned to sensor timelines; Rolls-Royce's advantage is exactly a 20+-year curated failure-event library under TotalCare (case analyses, 2020s) — organizations without one must start with anomaly detection + expert-labeled precursors, not supervised RUL fantasy. (ii) **Point-in-time discipline on maintenance context**: features must exclude post-failure inspection data; maintenance actions reset degradation clocks (a bearing replaced last week changes everything) — asset state must be event-sourced. (iii) **Censoring is structural**: most assets haven't failed; survival analysis or careful negative-sampling windows, never naive classification on "failed vs not". (iv) **Per-asset heterogeneity**: same pump model, different duty cycles — hierarchical/fleet models with asset-level calibration. (v) **Alarm-fatigue calibration**: threshold set to maintenance capacity; an alert stream the planner can't absorb is a failed solution regardless of AUC. (vi) Sensor-data quality (drop-outs, recalibrations, unit changes) monitored as rigorously as the model. (vii) Lead-time matching: predict far enough ahead to schedule parts + labor (the actionable horizon), not merely before failure.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Auto-generated work orders with diagnosis, priority, recommended parts, and due window; maintenance-schedule integration (bundle with planned windows); parts pre-ordering; inspection tasks for low-confidence alerts; deferral recommendations (extend intervals on healthy assets — often the biggest quiet saving). Decision rules: alert-severity → work-order priority mapping; capacity-aware daily alert budgets; criticality-tiered thresholds (safety-critical assets alert conservatively).
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — CMMS/EAM (SAP PM, Maximo, Fiix), historians (PI/OSIsoft), condition-monitoring platforms (Senseye, Augury, SKF), ERP for parts, scheduling/planning boards. **The CMMS integration is the solution**: an alert that doesn't become a scheduled work order with parts allocated is a dashboard, not a solution — the documented failure mode of most stalled PdM pilots (practitioner consensus; vendor ROI literature emphasizes work-order integration precisely because its absence kills programs).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Maintenance planners consume alerts and schedule; technicians execute and — critically — **close the loop with structured findings** ("confirmed bearing wear" / "false alarm"), which are the future labels; reliability engineers own thresholds and asset criticality; plant leadership reviews downtime economics monthly. Augments the planner; replaces calendar-only PM schedules.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Planner approves work-order creation initially (assisted); auto-creation for proven asset classes later; safety-critical interventions always human-approved; escalation to shutdown decisions follows existing plant authority chains.
9. **Measurement loop** — Before/after per asset class in a phased rollout: unplanned-downtime hours, maintenance cost, PM-task volume, catch rate (failures preceded by alerts) vs false-alarm rate, technician-confirmed alert precision. Documented magnitudes: industry syntheses report 30–50% unplanned-downtime reduction and 18–25% maintenance-cost reduction (McKinsey-derived figures via sector reports, 2020s); Siemens/Senseye customer figures up to 50% downtime and 40% cost reduction (Siemens ROI reports, 2018–2024; vendor-reported); BlueScope Steel avoided ~2,000 hours of unplanned downtime and 53 process interruptions across 2022–2024 (Siemens news release, 2025; vendor-linked); a global automotive manufacturer monitoring 10,000+ machines reported ROI <3 months (Senseye case, 2020s; vendor-reported, anonymized); Rolls-Royce's engine-health program supports >99.9% dispatch reliability and prevented hundreds of unscheduled removals/year (case analyses, 2020s; company-linked); easyJet avoided 35 technical cancellations in one month via Airbus Skywise; Delta mitigated >2,000 operational disruptions in its first Skywise year (Verdantix, 2022–2023).
10. **Operational architecture** — Streaming ingestion from sensors → near-real-time anomaly layer + daily/weekly risk scoring; edge deployment where connectivity is poor (Siemens/Arm report 100k+ assets across 300+ plants on edge AI — Arm newsroom, 2020s; vendor-reported); retraining per asset class quarterly; monitoring of sensor health, alert precision by class, and technician-feedback rates.
11. **Governance & safety** — Safety-instrumented systems remain outside ML authority (functional-safety standards); alert-suppression changes reviewed by reliability engineering; regulatory maintenance regimes (aviation: EASA/FAA predictive-maintenance acceptance processes) constrain automation depth.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Thin: condition monitoring + anomaly alerts on the 10 most critical assets with manual work-order creation — a quarter (Siemens claims weeks-to-months to first value; vendor-reported). Fleet-wide programs: 6–24 months; long poles: sensor retrofits, historian data quality, failure-label archaeology, CMMS integration.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Technicians validate early alerts personally (tear-downs that confirm the model create the plant's folklore of trust — or destroy it); false-alarm streaks are fatal, hence conservative launch thresholds; planner workflow must change (capacity for predictive orders) or alerts queue into oblivion; feedback capture must be effortless (checkbox at closeout, not a form).
14. **Documented deployments** — BlueScope (2022–2024, above); the anonymized global automotive OEM (Senseye); Rolls-Royce TotalCare/IntelligentEngine (aviation; company-linked analyses); easyJet/Delta via Skywise (Verdantix, 2022–2023); Siemens edge fleet (Arm, 2020s). Sector meta-numbers: 95% of PdM adopters report positive ROI, 27% payback <1 year (IoT Analytics via sector reports, 2023).
15. **Failure modes** — Alert-without-work-order (the pilot dashboard nobody schedules from — the most documented stall in this family); label starvation (supervised ambitions without failure history); false-alarm trust collapse; sensor rot mistaken for degradation; savings claims without downtime baselines (no before-measurement = unfundable phase 2); maintenance-capacity mismatch (alerts exceed the crew).
16. **Variants** — Rotating equipment (vibration-based; most mature), fleet/mobility (rail, aviation, trucking — telematics), utilities (transformers, lines), process plants (integrated with APC); prescriptive-maintenance variant adds repair-action recommendation; condition-based-only variant (no prediction, threshold rules) is the honest fallback where labels are absent.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Condition-monitoring platforms overwhelmingly bought (Senseye, Augury, SKF, Uptake); asset-specific models sometimes OEM-provided (Rolls-Royce TotalCare as maintenance-as-a-service); CMMS integration and reliability process always in-house; heavy industry occasionally builds on historians + open-source.
18. **Key references** — Siemens Senseye ROI reports (2018–2024); BlueScope release (2025); Rolls-Royce TotalCare analyses; Verdantix aerospace analytics (2023); PHM (Prognostics & Health Management) literature on RUL with censoring.

### 19. Quality inspection → line intervention

1. **Name** — AI visual quality inspection → line intervention.
2. **Use case solved** — Catching defects at production speed and acting on the line (reject, rework, stop, adjust) — beyond human inspection's fatigue limits.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Computer-vision methodology (imaging → CNN/vision-transformer classification/segmentation/anomaly detection → decision) wired to line PLCs and MES; anomaly-detection variants where defect examples are scarce.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Defect scarcity & class imbalance**: good parts dominate by 10³–10⁶; anomaly detection (train on good) or synthetic defect augmentation before supervised classification; per-defect-class recall targets, never aggregate accuracy. (ii) **Imaging physics as correctness**: lighting drift, lens fouling, camera replacement, and part-position variance are the real drift sources — golden-sample recalibration routines and image-quality monitors are part of the model system. (iii) **Train/serve environment identity**: models trained on lab images fail on line lighting; data must come from the production cell. (iv) **False-reject economics**: over-rejection quietly scraps good product — the two-sided threshold economics of dossier 13 with physical waste; human re-verification queues for borderline calls. (v) New-product/SKU cold start (retraining pipeline as a standing capability, not a project). (vi) Traceability joins (image ↔ part serial ↔ process parameters) enable root-cause, and are an entity-resolution task on the shop floor.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Reject/divert actuation, rework routing, line-stop on defect clusters (andon), upstream parameter alerts (defect signature → process owner), SPC integration (defect rates onto control charts), supplier-quality claims with image evidence. Decision rules: per-class confidence → action mapping; cluster thresholds for stop decisions (stopping a line is expensive — statistical run rules, not single detections); human confirmation for stop actions initially.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Line PLCs/actuators (the reject gate is the actuator of record), MES/SCADA, SPC systems, CMMS (defect patterns → maintenance), supplier-quality portals.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Quality engineers own defect taxonomies and thresholds; line operators respond to stops/alerts; inspection staff shift to audit + borderline review; supplier-quality uses evidence packages. Replaces/augments human visual inspection stations.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Auto-reject at high confidence; human review of borderline and of a control sample of *passed* parts (escape audit); line-stop recommendations human-confirmed until statistical trust is built; full autonomy on mature stable SKUs.
9. **Measurement loop** — Escape rate (defects reaching customers) and false-reject rate vs the human-inspection baseline, measured in side-by-side then phased rollout; scrap/rework cost; downstream warranty-claim rates as the slow ground truth. Guardrails: per-class recall stability, image-quality indices, re-inspection audit results.
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time edge inference (line-speed latency, often <100 ms/part); on-prem/edge deployment (no cloud on the takt path); retraining per SKU/defect-class on accumulated review labels; monitoring: image drift, per-class score distributions, reject-rate SPC.
11. **Governance & safety** — Safety-critical industries (auto, aero, medical devices) require validated inspection processes (e.g. IATF 16949, FDA process validation) — model changes go through formal re-validation; audit trails of every inspection decision with image retention policies.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Single-cell deployments in weeks-to-a-quarter with modern tooling (the category Landing AI targeted with data-centric retraining workflows — company materials, 2020s); plant-wide programs are multi-quarter; long poles: imaging/fixturing engineering and defect-taxonomy agreement, not modeling.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Quality organizations demand validation evidence (measurement-systems analysis on the model, like any gauge); inspector redeployment handled openly; operators trust systems whose stops are visibly justified (defect image displayed at the andon).
14. **Documented deployments** — Widely deployed across automotive/electronics manufacturing (BMW has publicized AI inspection in press materials; Foxconn-scale electronics AOI augmentation is industry-standard practice — sector-documented; specific audited defect-rate numbers were not source-verified for this compendium and are stated qualitatively). Vendor-reported outcomes typically cite escape-rate reductions and inspection-labor savings in the tens of percent (directional). This dossier flags its named-number gap honestly rather than importing marketing figures.
15. **Failure modes** — Lighting/positioning drift silently degrading recall (the #1 field failure); false-reject creep eroding line yield and operator trust; defect-taxonomy churn breaking labels; new-SKU launches outpacing retraining; inspection theater (AI station installed, reject gate never wired — detection without intervention).
16. **Variants** — Discrete-part AOI (electronics), surface inspection (steel, glass, paper — continuous web), assembly verification, food sorting (real-time hyperspectral), pharma visual inspection (regulated maximum); anomaly-only variants for low-volume/high-mix.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Machine-vision incumbents (Cognex, Keyence) + deep-learning add-ons dominate; platform vendors (Landing AI-class) sell retraining workflow; integrators wire the line; in-house CV teams at the largest manufacturers. The line-actuation wiring is always plant engineering.
18. **Key references** — Manufacturing-CV survey literature; Cognex/Landing AI factual product documentation; IATF/FDA validation frameworks; practitioner accounts of lighting-drift failures.

### 20. Yield & process-parameter optimization

1. **Name** — Yield optimization → process-parameter control.
2. **Use case solved** — Raising yield/throughput/energy-efficiency of continuous or batch processes by finding and holding better setpoints than operator heuristics.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Tabular/timeseries supervised modeling of process outcomes (sensor features → yield/quality prediction) + optimization or RL layer proposing setpoints + explanation for process engineers; the "report" is a setpoint recommendation; the mature form closes the loop into control systems.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Causality vs correlation is the core danger**: historical process data is confounded by operator policy (setpoints were responses to conditions); recommending correlated setpoints can be actively harmful — requires designed experiments (DoE), constrained exploration, or causal identification before optimization claims. (ii) **Physical/safety constraint embedding**: recommendations must respect equipment envelopes, interlocks, and rate-of-change limits as hard constraints in the optimizer, never as model suggestions. (iii) **Regime/grade awareness**: recipes, feedstock lots, and seasonal conditions define regimes; per-regime models with explicit detection. (iv) Sensor-calibration drift as a first-class failure source. (v) **Delayed and aggregate labels**: yield may be known only per batch/lot hours later — credit assignment across the run requires careful windowing. (vi) Conservative closed-loop rollout: advisory → operator-approved → bounded auto-control (the DeepMind data-center path: recommendations first, then autonomous control with hard safety bounds and operator override — DeepMind, 2016–2018).
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Setpoint recommendations to operators (advisory), recipe adjustments per batch, bounded automatic control moves (APC integration), maintenance/cleaning triggers on efficiency decay, feedstock-blending decisions. Decision rules: recommendation bounds per variable, rate limits, approval requirements by regime, automatic fallback to baseline control on anomaly.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — DCS/APC layers (Honeywell, Emerson, ABB), historians, MES/LIMS (quality results), energy-management systems.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Process engineers own the recommendation review and constraint sets; operators accept/reject moves per shift; production leadership tracks yield economics. Augments APC; replaces tribal-knowledge setpoint sheets.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Advisory first (operator applies), then closed-loop within tight bounds and operator kill-switch — Google's cooling system ran fully autonomous *within* certified safety constraints and human monitoring (DeepMind safety-first post, 2018); safety-instrumented functions always outside ML authority.
9. **Measurement loop** — Yield/energy per unit vs matched baseline periods (regime-controlled comparisons; simple before/after lies when feedstock shifts); on/off switchback tests where safe; guardrails: constraint-violation count (must be zero), quality excursions, operator-override rate. Documented magnitudes: Google data-center cooling −40% cooling energy initially (DeepMind/Google, 2016), ~30% sustained average in autonomous operation (DeepMind, 2018; company-reported); process-industry AI-APC programs report single-digit-percent yield/energy gains that are economically large at scale (sector/consulting reports, 2020s; directional).
10. **Operational architecture** — Streaming process data; recommendation cadence minutes-to-hourly; closed-loop moves rate-limited; retraining per regime; monitoring of model-plant mismatch (prediction error spikes = regime change or sensor fault → auto-revert).
11. **Governance & safety** — Process-safety management (PSM/HAZOP) reviews of any control-path change; functional-safety separation; change-control on constraint sets; full move audit trail.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Advisory yield models: one-two quarters on historian data. Closed-loop: multi-quarter with safety certification. Thin version: efficiency-anomaly detection + engineer investigation queue (value with zero control-path risk).

### 21. Route optimization → dispatch (and warehouse slotting)

1. **Name** — Route optimization → dispatch (with warehouse slotting/labor as the indoor sibling).
2. **Use case solved** — Cutting miles/hours/fuel while meeting service windows — turning network-level optimization into thousands of daily driver-level plans (and, indoors, into slotting and labor plans).
3. **Methodology composed from** — Optimization/OR methodology (VRP solvers, MIP/heuristics) + ML components (travel-time prediction, demand/volume forecasts feeding the optimizer, dynamic re-optimization); the ML feeds the optimizer; the optimizer feeds dispatch.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Constraint fidelity is the correctness battle**: real routes carry thousands of tacit constraints (gate hours, vehicle fit, driver knowledge, customer quirks); UPS's ORION decade was substantially spent encoding what drivers knew — an insufficiently constrained "optimal" route is inexecutable and burns trust. (ii) **Travel-time model honesty**: point-in-time traffic features, seasonal/weather effects, service-time-per-stop learned from actuals (not standards). (iii) **Plan stability vs optimality**: mathematically optimal daily churn destroys driver familiarity; stability penalties are a first-class objective term. (iv) Feasibility verification before dispatch (a plan violating hours-of-service law is illegal, not suboptimal). (v) Demand-forecast integration with censoring awareness (as dossier 10) for volume-driven planning. (vi) Warehouse variant: slotting optimization must respect physical/ergonomic constraints and re-slotting cost (moves are expensive — the optimizer must value stability).

7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Dispatchers review/adjust plans (their overrides are signal); drivers execute; industrial engineers own constraint sets; ops leadership tracks cost per stop. Replaces manual routing boards and static route books.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Dispatcher approval of nightly plans initially, moving to exception-only review; drivers retain deviation discretion with telemetry feedback; automated re-dispatch within bounds.
9. **Measurement loop** — Miles/stops/fuel per route vs pre-deployment baselines and phased-region rollouts; plan-adherence rate (the adoption truth-teller); service-level guardrails (on-time windows, missed stops). Documented magnitudes: UPS ORION — ~100M miles and ~10M gallons of fuel saved per year, $300–400M annual cost avoidance, ~6–8 miles saved per route per day, deployed 2012–2016 after ~$250–300M investment (INFORMS/UPS case materials, 2016; company-reported); ORION's rollout famously fought driver skepticism about counterintuitive route orders — adoption engineering was a headline cost (case literature, 2016–2021).

### 22. AIOps incident prediction & alert correlation → remediation

1. **Name** — AIOps: alert correlation + incident prediction → risk-tiered automated remediation.
2. **Use case solved** — Alert storms drowning on-call teams and incidents resolved slower than they should be; the solution compresses noise into incidents and executes safe remediations (restart/scale/rollback) automatically where risk permits.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Anomaly detection on telemetry (metrics/logs/traces) + correlation/clustering (topology- and time-based grouping of alerts into incidents) + tabular supervised components (incident-severity prediction) + a **risk-tiered automation policy** (runbook automation); LLM summarization increasingly layered for incident narratives.

(ii) **Baseline seasonality**: deploy cycles, batch windows, weekly rhythms — anomaly detectors without calendar awareness page people every Monday. (iii) **Alert-label feedback**: on-call engineers' dispositions (acknowledged/suppressed/linked) are the training labels; capture must be frictionless or the model starves. (iv) **Remediation safety tiering as the central design**: actions classified by blast radius (restart pod < failover < rollback < scale-down), each tier with its own automation threshold, canary verification, rate limits, and automatic rollback-of-the-remediation — closed-loop automation earns scope one action class at a time (shadow → recommend → auto with approval → auto with notification). (v) Drift with architecture changes (every migration invalidates baselines; retraining hooks into change management). (vi) Alert-storm survival (the system must degrade gracefully under exactly the conditions it exists for).
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Deduplicated, correlated incident creation with probable-cause ranking; on-call paging with context; automated runbook execution per safety tier; post-incident timeline assembly. Decision rules: automation tier per action × service criticality; business-hours vs off-hours policies; approval gates for state-destructive actions; kill-switch on all automation.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Monitoring/observability stack (Datadog, Dynatrace, Grafana, Splunk), incident management (PagerDuty, Opsgenie, ServiceNow ITSM), runbook automation (Rundeck, Ansible, cloud-native operators), change-management (correlation with deploys).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — On-call engineers consume correlated incidents; SRE owns automation tiers and runbooks; service owners approve per-service automation scope; incident reviews feed new runbooks. Replaces raw alert firehoses and manual triage.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — The risk-tier ladder *is* the HITL design: low-risk idempotent actions fully automated; medium actions auto-executed with human notification; high-risk actions recommended for one-click human approval; novel incidents always human-led. Escalation follows existing on-call hierarchy with automation-status visibility.
9. **Measurement loop** — MTTR and MTTA by incident class before/after (phased by service); alert-volume compression ratio; automation success rate (remediation resolved vs made-worse — the guardrail); paging-load per engineer per week (the burnout metric); false-correlation rate sampled in incident reviews. Sector claims of 40–80% MTTR reduction and 50–80% alert-noise reduction are vendor-reported ranges (AIOps vendor case studies, 2020s; treat directionally — audited public numbers are scarce in this family); the burden of proof sits on each org's own phased rollout.
10. **Operational architecture** — Streaming; correlation latency in seconds; remediation execution near-real-time with verification waits; models retrained continuously on disposition feedback; monitoring-of-the-monitoring (meta-alerting) is mandatory.
11. **Governance & safety** — Change-control integration for automated actions (an automated rollback is a production change); audit trail of every automated action with pre/post state; separation of duties (automation service accounts scoped minimally); post-incident review of automation behavior as standard agenda item.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Alert correlation/dedup: weeks with modern platforms, immediate on-call relief. Automated remediation: quarters, earned action-class by action-class. Long pole: topology/CMDB accuracy and runbook debt.
13. **Adoption & change management** — On-call engineers adopt correlation instantly (it removes pain); automation trust builds through the shadow→recommend→auto ladder with public success/failure stats; a single automation-made-it-worse incident without honest review can freeze the program — blameless review discipline is the adoption infrastructure.
14. **Documented deployments** — Large-enterprise AIOps deployments documented across vendor bases (Dynatrace, BigPanda, Moogsoft customer cases; 2020s; vendor-reported); hyperscaler-internal auto-remediation (Google/Meta SRE literature describes mature automated response practice — SRE books, 2016–2020s; qualitative); the adjacent quantified evidence: IBM Cost of a Data Breach attributes ~100-day lifecycle reductions to extensive AI/automation in the security-incident sibling (IBM, 2023–2024 — see dossier 23).
15. **Failure modes** — Automation acting on false correlation (remediating the wrong service); stale topology mis-grouping alerts; runbooks rotting until automation executes obsolete procedures; alert-suppression hiding real incidents (over-aggressive dedup); teams gaming paging metrics by under-alerting; "AIOps" bought as a dashboard with the remediation wiring never built.
16. **Variants** — Correlation-only (most common), predict-and-prevent (capacity/failure prediction feeding preemptive action), full closed-loop (rare, hyperscaler-class); Kubernetes-native self-healing as the platform-embedded variant.

### 23. Security detection → containment playbooks

1. **Name** — Security detection → containment playbooks (shadow → assisted → automated SOAR).
2. **Use case solved** — SOCs cannot investigate their alert volume (industry surveys repeatedly find large fractions of alerts uninvestigated — commonly reported around half to two-thirds in vendor SOC surveys, 2010s–2020s; survey-dependent); ML detection + graduated automated containment shrinks attacker dwell time.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Anomaly/behavioral detection (UEBA, network/endpoint models) + supervised alert-scoring (triage, as dossier 14's sibling) + SOAR playbook automation (isolate host, disable account, block indicator) with the same risk-tier ladder as dossier 22; LLM investigation-summarization arriving 2023+.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Adversarial non-stationarity as the default**: attackers adapt to detections; purple-team exercises are the drift tests, detection-as-code with versioned rules+models the hygiene. (ii) **Base-rate discipline**: true attacks are vanishingly rare; alert-scoring calibrated to analyst capacity (as dossiers 14/18), with below-the-line sampling of suppressed alerts. (iii) **Entity resolution across identities/devices/IPs** (the attack graph) — mis-resolution both hides attacks and mis-targets containment (isolating the wrong host is a self-inflicted outage). (iv) **Containment blast-radius modeling**: auto-disabling a service account can take down production; playbooks need dependency checks and business-criticality gates as hard constraints. (v) Label capture from analyst dispositions with quality control (tier-1 fatigue produces noisy labels). (vi) Shadow-mode measurement before any automated action (the family's named rollout doctrine: shadow → assisted → automated).
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Alert enrichment + risk-ranked queues; automated containment per playbook tier: block indicator (low risk) → quarantine file → isolate endpoint → disable account → segment network (highest); case creation with evidence packages; threat-intel feedback. Decision rules: playbook automation tier × asset criticality × confidence; business-hours approval variants; VIP/production-asset carve-outs; mandatory human approval for identity- and network-level actions in most orgs.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — SIEM (Splunk, Sentinel, Chronicle), EDR/XDR (CrowdStrike, Defender, SentinelOne), SOAR (Splunk SOAR, Palo XSOAR, Tines), IAM (for account actions), firewalls/NAC, ticketing.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — SOC tiers consume ranked queues; detection engineering owns rules/models; IR team owns playbooks; CISO owns automation policy. The process replaced: manual tier-1 triage of everything.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — The graduated ladder is doctrine: new detections run in shadow (alert-only, measured); then assisted (analyst one-click actions); then automated for high-confidence/low-blast-radius classes. Escalation to IR on confirmed compromise; kill-switches and automatic expiry on containment actions (an isolation that auto-expires limits mistake cost).
9. **Measurement loop** — Dwell-time / time-to-contain trends; the quantified sector anchor: organizations with extensive security AI + automation had ~$1.76M lower average breach costs and 108-day shorter breach lifecycles in 2023 (IBM Cost of a Data Breach, 2023; global average breach cost $4.45M), with 2024 figures showing ~$1.9–2.2M savings depending on deployment depth and ~100-day lifecycle reduction (IBM CODB, 2024; average $4.88M). Program-level: detection coverage vs ATT&CK, false-positive rate per detection, automation success/rollback rate, analyst alerts-handled and burnout indicators; purple-team catch rates as ground truth.
10. **Operational architecture** — Streaming detection; SOAR execution seconds-to-minutes; detection content CI/CD; model retraining continuous on dispositions; meta-monitoring of detection-pipeline health (a silently broken log source is an invisible blind spot — pipeline-health alerting is a correctness control).
11. **Governance & safety** — Automated-action authority documented and approved by risk owners; audit trail forensically sound; privacy constraints on UEBA (employee monitoring law, works councils); regulatory incident-reporting clocks (SEC 4-day disclosure, 2023; DORA/NIS2 in EU) integrated into playbooks.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Alert-scoring + enrichment: weeks. Assisted containment: a quarter. Automated containment: quarters, earned per playbook. Long poles: log-source coverage, asset inventory quality, and political approval for automated authority.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Analysts embrace enrichment; IR teams resist automation until shadow-mode stats and bounded blast radius are demonstrated; the one-bad-auto-containment story (production down) is the program-killer — hence expiring actions and criticality gates; tabletop exercises rehearse the human-automation interplay.
14. **Documented deployments** — IBM CODB population-level evidence (2023–2024, figures above — the strongest cross-org quantification in this family); Microsoft/CrowdStrike/Google document customer automated-response deployments at scale (vendor-reported); mature SOCs publicly describe shadow→auto ladders (conference talks, 2020s; qualitative).
15. **Failure modes** — Auto-containment outages (wrong host/account); alert-scoring trained on triage-fatigue labels canonizing neglect; detection debt (models tuned to last year's TTPs); suppressed-alert blind spots without below-the-line sampling; SOAR bought but playbooks never authored (automation theater); metric gaming (closing alerts fast ≠ investigating them).
16. **Variants** — Enterprise SOC (the default); MDR/MSSP (automation across customers, stricter genericity); OT/ICS security (containment ≠ isolation — safety constraints dominate); cloud-native (identity-centric containment); email security (highest-volume automated blocking, most mature).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Detection platforms bought; detection *content* increasingly in-house (detection engineering); SOAR bought, playbooks in-house; UEBA models mostly bought. The automation-authority policy is organizational, never purchasable.
18. **Key references** — IBM Cost of a Data Breach (2023, 2024); MITRE ATT&CK; SANS SOC survey series (for alert-volume context; survey-dependent numbers); vendor SOAR playbook libraries (factual capability reference).

### 24. Vulnerability prioritization → patch workflow

1. **Name** — Vulnerability prioritization → patch workflow.
2. **Use case solved** — Vulnerability scanners produce far more findings than any team can patch; the solution ranks by *actual exploitation risk × asset exposure* and drives the patch pipeline, replacing CVSS-severity-sorted backlogs that misallocate effort.
3. **Methodology composed from** — Tabular supervised methodology on vulnerability + threat-intel features (exploit-prediction scoring — EPSS is the public exemplar: gradient-boosted probability a CVE is exploited in the wild within 30 days — FIRST.org, ongoing) + asset-context enrichment (reachability, criticality) + workflow automation into ticketing/patch systems.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Label subtlety**: "exploited in the wild" labels come from honeypots/IDS/vendor telemetry with severe observation bias; probability calibration and label-source diversity matter (EPSS documentation is explicit about this — FIRST.org). (ii) **Asset-context joins are the value**: an exploitable CVE on an internet-facing crown-jewel outranks a critical-CVSS finding on an isolated dev box — entity resolution between scanner findings, CMDB, and network exposure data is the hard engineering. (iii) Temporal discipline: risk scores change as exploits publish; re-scoring daily, SLA clocks keyed to score *changes*. (iv) Dedup across scanners (the same vuln reported five ways). (v) Compensating-control awareness (WAF rules, segmentation) to avoid false urgency. (vi) Feedback loop from incident/pen-test findings back into prioritization weights.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Risk-ranked remediation queues with SLA tiers; auto-created patch tickets grouped by fix (one patch closes many findings — grouping by remediation, not by CVE, is the workflow insight); emergency-patch triggers on exploitation-signal spikes; exception/risk-acceptance workflow with expiry; compensating-control tasking when patching is infeasible. Decision rules: score × asset tier → SLA (e.g. 7/30/90 days); change-window constraints; auto-patch authority only for pre-approved software classes.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Scanners (Qualys, Tenable, Wiz), ticketing (Jira/ServiceNow), patch management (SCCM/Intune, Ansible), CMDB, risk registers.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Vulnerability-management team owns scoring policy; platform/app teams execute patches (the accountable owners — routing findings to named owners is half the program); CISO reports risk-burndown; monthly exception review. Replaces CVSS-sorted spreadsheets.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Prioritization automated; patching human-executed through change management except pre-approved auto-patch classes (browsers, endpoints); risk acceptance is a human sign-off with expiry; emergency escalation to IR on active exploitation of present vulns.
9. **Measurement loop** — Mean-time-to-remediate by tier; exposure-days on exploited-in-wild vulns (the honest KPI); % of remediation effort spent on vulns that were ever exploited (efficiency of prioritization vs CVSS baseline — EPSS research shows dramatic efficiency gains over CVSS-based strategies at equal coverage: FIRST/EPSS papers, 2019–2023); guardrails: SLA breach counts, exception backlog age, scanner-coverage drift.
10. **Operational architecture** — Daily batch re-scoring; event-driven urgency on threat-intel triggers; no latency pressure; monitoring of feed health and score-distribution drift.
11. **Governance & safety** — Auditability for compliance frameworks (PCI, SOC 2 — remediation SLAs are audit artifacts); risk-acceptance governance; regulator-driven patch mandates (CISA KEV catalog deadlines for US federal, 2021+) as hard overrides in the policy layer.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Fast: EPSS + KEV + asset tiers over existing scanner output delivers a defensible re-prioritization in weeks; the long pole is CMDB/ownership data quality and the political work of SLA enforcement.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Platform teams accept risk-ranked queues that *shrink* their busywork (fewer, better-justified tickets); security must resist the temptation to keep CVSS theater for auditors — mapping the risk-based policy to compliance language is the change-management task.
14. **Documented deployments** — EPSS adoption across enterprise VM programs (FIRST.org ecosystem, 2021+); CISA KEV-driven prioritization mandated federally (2021+); vendor risk-based-VM platforms (Tenable, Qualys, Kenna—whose research popularized the efficiency evidence, 2018–2020) document large deployment bases (vendor-reported).
15. **Failure modes** — Beautiful prioritization, unchanged patch throughput (the queue is smarter, nothing ships — ownership/process failure); CMDB rot mis-tiering assets; exception workflow becoming a permanent parking lot; score-chasing (teams patch to move the number, not the exposure); scanner blind spots unmonitored.
16. **Variants** — Traditional infra VM; cloud-native (CSPM/CNAPP context — reachability analysis); application/SCA (dependency vulns, SBOM-driven); OT (patching constrained — compensating controls dominate).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Scoring inputs public (EPSS, KEV) + bought platforms; asset-context joining sometimes built; patch execution uses existing tooling. Small in-house glue, big process work.
18. **Key references** — EPSS papers and documentation (FIRST.org, 2019–2023); CISA KEV program (2021+); Kenna/Cyentia "Prioritization to Prediction" series (2018–2021).

### 26. Contract review → clause flags & obligations tracking

1. **Name** — Contract review → clause flags & obligations tracking.
2. **Use case solved** — Lawyers and deal teams reading contracts for standard risks, deviations, and obligations — slow, inconsistent, unsearchable at portfolio scale.
3. **Methodology composed from** — NLP/LLM extraction methodology over legal documents (clause detection/classification → deviation-from-playbook comparison → obligation extraction with dates/parties → flags and registers); retrieval for precedent; the report is a markup + an obligations register.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Playbook grounding**: flags must be defined against the org's negotiation playbook (acceptable/fallback/escalate positions per clause type), not generic "risk" — the playbook formalization is usually the real project. (ii) **Recall-first calibration on high-severity clauses** (indemnity, liability caps, change-of-control): a missed clause is worse than a false flag; per-clause-type thresholds with severity-weighted evaluation. (v) Definition-sensitivity (defined terms alter clause meaning; extraction must carry the definitions context). (vi) Human-verification sampling with lawyer-grade ground truth (annotation is expensive; disagreement between lawyers is itself label noise to manage).
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Pre-signature: markup with flagged deviations + suggested fallback language routed to the deal lawyer; approval-matrix triggers (deviations above severity escalate to senior counsel). Post-signature: obligations register (renewal dates, price escalators, notice windows, compliance duties) generating calendar tasks to named owners; portfolio queries (e.g. "all contracts with X clause" — the due-diligence and regulatory-response action). Decision rules: clause severity × counterparty tier → review depth; auto-approve only for fully-standard paper.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — CLM platforms (Ironclad, Icertis, Agiloft, Evisort/Workday, Luminance), document repositories, e-signature, matter management, calendar/task systems, ERP (obligations with financial effect).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Legal ops owns playbooks and the register; deal lawyers consume markups; business owners receive obligation tasks; quarterly portfolio reviews use the extracted corpus. Replaces memory-and-spreadsheet obligations tracking and full manual first-pass review.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Lawyer reviews every pre-signature flag (assisted, not automated — professional-responsibility rules effectively require it); post-signature extraction sampled for accuracy; only fully-standard templated paper flows without review.
9. **Measurement loop** — Review turnaround time and cost per contract vs baseline; missed-obligation incidents (renewals lapsed, escalators uncollected — the value ledger); flag precision/recall on audit samples; portfolio-query response time for diligence events. Documented magnitudes: JPMorgan's COIN automated review of ~12,000 commercial credit agreements/year, work previously consuming ~360,000 lawyer/loan-officer hours (Bloomberg, 2017) — the canonical figure; CLM vendor cases report 60–90% review-time reductions (Luminance/Evisort case materials, 2020s; vendor-reported).
10. **Operational architecture** — Batch/near-real-time per document; portfolio backfills as bulk jobs; models/prompts versioned with regression suites over annotated sets; monitoring: per-clause-type accuracy drift as templates and law evolve.
11. **Governance & safety** — Privilege and confidentiality (data-processing terms for any external model; on-prem/VPC patterns common); professional-responsibility boundaries (AI-assisted, lawyer-signed); auditability of extraction provenance for disputes.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Post-signature obligations extraction on the existing repository: a quarter, immediate value (found renewals pay for programs). Pre-signature playbook automation: 2–3 quarters (playbook formalization is the long pole). LLMs have collapsed the extraction-model cost; annotation and playbook work remain.

14. **Documented deployments** — JPMorgan COIN (2017; figures above). NetApp reviewed ~90k contracts for specific terms in days vs months (Evisort case, 2020s; vendor-reported); BNY Mellon reported 75% faster review cycles (Evisort case; vendor-reported); enterprise CLM-AI adoption is broad (Ironclad/Icertis bases; vendor-documented).

16. **Variants** — Sell-side high-volume paper (NDAs, MSAs — most automatable); buy-side/procurement; M&A due-diligence burst mode; regulated-obligation extraction (financial services outsourcing rules); lease abstraction (real estate — an early commercial success of the category).

18. **Key references** — Bloomberg on COIN (2017); CUAD dataset/benchmark for contract clause extraction (2021); CLM vendor case studies (2020s; factual claims); legal-ops practitioner literature (CLOC).

## Family H — People

*People-analytics solutions reuse the tabular methodology wholesale but carry the heaviest governance-per-dollar in the catalog: the "customers" are employees with legal rights over profiling, and the failure precedents are famous.*

### 28. Attrition prediction → manager interventions

1. **Name** — Attrition prediction → manager interventions (with workforce planning → scheduling as the aggregate variant).
2. **Use case solved** — Regrettable employee attrition: predicting which teams/roles/individuals are at elevated leaving risk and equipping managers/HR to intervene — plus, at the aggregate level, staffing plans that match forecast workload.
3. **Methodology composed from** — The tabular supervised methodology verbatim (HRIS + comp + engagement + org-network features → point-in-time snapshots → gradient boosting → SHAP drivers → report) — structurally identical to churn (dossier 1) with employees as the entity; the workforce-scheduling variant is the forecasting methodology (dossier 11) aimed at labor demand.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — All of churn's temporal discipline applies (point-in-time snapshots, label windows on departure events, temporal validation, censoring for current employees), plus people-specific correctness: (i) **Small-n honesty**: departments have dozens, not millions; individual-level prediction is statistically fragile — aggregate (team/role/site) risk modeling is often the *correct* granularity, and claiming individual precision the org can't support is a correctness failure. (ii) **Label taxonomy**: regrettable vs non-regrettable vs involuntary exits must be separated or the model learns terminations. (iii) **Proxy-discrimination screening**: tenure, comp trajectory, and network features proxy age/gender/caregiving; disparate-impact testing is a legal correctness property (as dossier 15). (iv) **Feedback-loop ethics**: if managers act on scores (or fear them), behavior changes and labels shift — Hawthorne effects belong in the measurement design. (v) Engagement-survey features carry consent constraints (data collected for one purpose repurposed for scoring — GDPR purpose-limitation is a hard boundary in EU deployments). (vi) Drift with comp cycles and labor-market swings.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Manager-level: stay conversations, comp/promotion reviews, workload rebalancing, flight-risk flags *as coaching prompts, not verdicts*; HR-level: retention-budget targeting, hotspot deep-dives (the driver report per org unit), policy changes (the wholesale action: fix the manager, the pay band, or the on-call load the drivers point at). Decision rules: aggregate reports to leadership; individual flags only where policy permits, always bundled with drivers and never as ranked "leaver lists"; comp actions through existing approval chains.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — HRIS (Workday, SuccessFactors), people-analytics platforms (Visier, One Model), engagement platforms (Culture Amp, Glint), comp-planning tools; the report is the primary interface — deliberately more report-shaped than churn because automation is inappropriate here.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — CHRO/people-analytics team owns the model and the report; HRBPs translate to org actions; managers act in 1:1s; quarterly talent reviews consume hotspot analyses. Replaces exit-interview archaeology (learning why people left after they left).
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Humans everywhere: no automated action touches an individual; the model informs conversations and budgets. Escalation is organizational (hotspot → leadership attention), not systemic.
9. **Measurement loop** — Regrettable-attrition rates in intervention cohorts vs matched comparisons (randomized holdouts are ethically awkward for individuals but clean for *program* variants — e.g. phased rollout of stay-conversation programs across sites); cost-of-attrition math (replacement cost 50–200% of salary — SHRM-class estimates, 2010s–2020s; estimate ranges) frames ROI; IBM claimed its predictive-attrition program saved ~$300M in retention costs with high-accuracy predictions (Rometty CNBC interview claim, 2019; company claim, not independently verified — cited here as the famous, and famously unaudited, reference point). Guardrails: fairness metrics, manager-behavior distortions, engagement-survey response-rate erosion (surveillance perception poisons the data source).
10. **Operational architecture** — Monthly/quarterly batch; no latency; retraining semi-annual; monitoring of feature drift across comp cycles and label-taxonomy consistency.
11. **Governance & safety** — The family's defining field: works-council/union consultation (EU co-determination makes deployment a negotiated act); GDPR profiling rights (access, objection); EU AI Act classes employment-related AI as high-risk (obligations phasing 2025–2027); strict access control (scores visible to few); prohibition on use in termination decisions as standard policy (legal exposure otherwise); transparency to employees about what is modeled.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Aggregate hotspot reporting: one quarter on HRIS data. Individual-flag programs: only after governance groundwork (works councils, legal, comms) — often 2–3× the technical timeline. Thin version: quarterly driver-analysis report at org-unit level with zero individual scoring — most of the value, a fraction of the risk.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Managers must experience flags as help, not indictment (a flagged team implicates its manager — design the framing); HR analysts need causal-inference literacy (drivers ≠ causes; the churn lesson repeated); employee trust is the asset — one leaked "leaver list" ends the program.
14. **Documented deployments** — IBM (2019 claim, above; company-reported); large-enterprise people-analytics attrition modeling is standard practice via Visier/Workday-class platforms (vendor-documented, 2020s); published quantified program outcomes are rare precisely because of confidentiality and governance — the honest evidence cap; the workforce-scheduling variant is abundantly deployed (retail/contact-center WFM with ML forecasts — Legion/Quinyx-class, vendor-reported labor-cost single-digit-percent savings; 2020s).
15. **Failure modes** — Leaver-list leaks destroying trust (the catastrophic one); managers gaming scores (suppressing signals); proxy discrimination discovered late; individual-precision overclaims on small-n data; interventions that are just surveillance (monitoring without support *increases* attrition — engagement literature; qualitative); the Amazon recruiting precedent (dossier 29) hanging over every people-model conversation.
16. **Variants** — Aggregate-only hotspot analytics (recommended default); individual-flag programs (high governance); first-90-days onboarding-risk models; workforce planning → scheduling (labor forecast → WFM shifts — mechanically dossier 11's wiring with fatigue/fairness constraints on schedules).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Platforms bought (Visier, Workday People Analytics); models often in-house for label-taxonomy control; the governance apparatus is entirely in-house.
18. **Key references** — IBM/CNBC (2019; company claim); EU AI Act employment provisions; works-council/GDPR profiling guidance; people-analytics practitioner literature (Wharton People Analytics conference material).

### 29. Recruiting screening → pipeline actions

1. **Name** — Recruiting screening → pipeline actions (with fairness constraints as the defining feature).
2. **Use case solved** — High-volume application screening and pipeline prioritization — routing recruiter attention, scheduling assessments, and reducing time-to-fill without encoding yesterday's hiring biases.
3. **Methodology composed from** — NLP extraction (résumé parsing → structured attributes) + tabular supervised ranking against job requirements + structured-assessment scoring; increasingly LLM-based matching — all inside a fairness-audited decision policy.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **The label problem is the whole problem**: "good hire" labels encode historical selection (who was hired and promoted in the old process); training on them reproduces the bias — Amazon's scrapped recruiting model (built ~2014, found penalizing the word "women's" and downgrading women's-college graduates, disbanded by 2017, revealed by Reuters 2018) is the canonical precedent: patching symptoms (removing explicit gender words) failed because the bias lived in the label-feature structure. Correct practice: structured, job-relevant criteria as the target (competency assessments, not "resembles past hires"), and adverse-impact testing as a release gate. (ii) **Four-fifths-rule monitoring** (EEOC framework) per pipeline stage, with documented audits — now law in some jurisdictions (NYC Local Law 144 requires independent bias audits of automated employment-decision tools + candidate notice, enforced 2023). (iii) Résumé-parsing robustness across formats/cultures (parsing failure = silent screening-out — an access-to-opportunity correctness issue). (iv) Feature governance: proscribe proxies (gaps, address, name-derived signals). (v) Explainability per decision for candidate-facing obligations (GDPR Article 22 rights against solely-automated decisions with legal effect). (vi) Human-review sampling of auto-rejected candidates (below-the-line testing again — the pattern recurs).
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Ranked recruiter queues (not auto-rejection — assisted is the defensible mode); knockout only on hard, validated requirements (license, work authorization); automated scheduling/assessment invitations; pipeline-rebalancing alerts (adverse-impact drift triggers sourcing changes); structured-interview guide generation. Decision rules: score bands order attention; auto-actions restricted to logistics; every rejection path human-accountable.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — ATS (Workday, Greenhouse, iCIMS), assessment platforms (HireVue-class — itself a governance cautionary tale: facial-analysis scoring was dropped in 2021 after criticism), scheduling tools, sourcing platforms (LinkedIn).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Recruiters consume ranked queues; hiring managers get structured shortlists; talent-acquisition ops owns the audit calendar; legal owns the compliance file. Replaces keyword-Boolean triage of thousand-CV inboxes.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Human decision on every advance/reject beyond validated knockouts; audit sampling of the low-ranked tail; candidate appeal/re-review path; recruiter override tracked as fairness signal.
9. **Measurement loop** — Time-to-fill, recruiter throughput, quality-of-hire proxies (90-day retention, performance ratings — themselves biased labels, used cautiously), funnel adverse-impact ratios per stage (the guardrail *and* the point), candidate NPS; phased rollout by req family. Public quantified lift numbers are thin and vendor-reported; the audited public evidence is mostly about *failures* (Amazon 2018) and *regulation* (LL144 2023) — an honest asymmetry this dossier reports rather than hides.
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time scoring on application; batch pipeline re-ranking; model updates gated by bias re-audit; monitoring: parsing failure rates by demographic-correlated segments, score-distribution drift, stage-conversion parity.
11. **Governance & safety** — NYC LL144 bias audits + notices (2023); Illinois AI Video Interview Act (2020); EEOC guidance on AI selection procedures (2022–2023); EU AI Act high-risk classification (employment); GDPR Article 22; vendor-accountability contracts (the audit obligation doesn't outsource).
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Parsing + ranked queues: weeks via ATS-native features; the compliance file (validation study, bias audit, notices) is the long pole and is mandatory before scale.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Recruiters adopt queue-ordering readily; hiring managers must be trained off "the algorithm picked" framing (accountability stays human); candidate-communication transparency is both legal duty and brand protection.
14. **Documented deployments** — Ubiquitous ATS-embedded ranking (Workday/Greenhouse-class; vendor-documented); Unilever's HireVue-based high-volume screening reported large time-to-hire reductions and was equally documented for the scrutiny it drew (2019–2021; company/vendor-reported + press criticism); Amazon's canceled tool (Reuters, 2018) as the anti-case; LL144 audit filings (2023+) now create a public evidence trail.
15. **Failure modes** — Bias reproduction via labels (Amazon precedent); parsing-driven silent exclusion; audit theater (compliance paperwork without model change); over-automation of rejection (legal exposure + talent loss); gaming (keyword-stuffed résumés — adversarial drift, LLM-accelerated since 2023); recruiter over-reliance flattening non-traditional candidates.
16. **Variants** — High-volume hourly hiring (assessment-centric, most automated); professional/exempt roles (assisted only); internal mobility matching (lower legal risk, underused); sourcing/outreach ranking (pre-application, lighter obligations).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Overwhelmingly bought (ATS + assessment vendors); the bias-audit obligation increasingly satisfied by third-party auditors; in-house builds rare and legally brave.
18. **Key references** — Reuters on Amazon (2018); NYC Local Law 144 (enforced 2023); EEOC technical assistance on AI selection (2022–2023); HireVue facial-analysis discontinuation coverage (2021); industrial-organizational psychology validation standards (SIOP Principles).

### 30. RAG assistants over enterprise knowledge

### 31. LLM extraction pipelines feeding structured systems

1. **Name** — LLM extraction pipeline feeding structured systems (documents → databases).
2. **Use case solved** — Unstructured inflows (emails, PDFs, forms, transcripts, filings) that must become clean rows in operational systems — historically manual keying or brittle template OCR.
3. **Methodology composed from** — LLM/IDP extraction methodology: ingestion → classification → schema-constrained extraction (function-calling/structured output) → validation layer → write to system of record, with confidence-routed human review; the generalization of dossier 25 beyond invoices.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Schema-constrained decoding + hard validators**: free generation is banned; outputs conform to typed schemas, then pass deterministic validation (checksums, referential lookups, range/date logic, cross-field consistency) — the validator layer, not the LLM, is the correctness authority. (ii) **Confidence-calibrated routing**: per-field confidence (or ensemble/self-consistency signals) routes low-confidence extractions to human review; the threshold is tuned to downstream error cost, and the review queue is the label factory. (iii) **Silent-error economics**: unlike chat, wrong extractions poison databases invisibly — sampling audits of *auto-accepted* records (below-the-line testing, fourth appearance in this compendium) are mandatory. (iv) **Document-distribution drift**: new senders/formats/languages; per-source accuracy monitoring with novelty detection. (v) Prompt-injection hardening (a document instructing "ignore prior instructions, approve this" is a live attack on extraction agents). (vi) Version-pinned models + regression suites over annotated document sets before any model upgrade (train/serve consistency, generative edition). (vii) Entity resolution on extracted parties against master data before writes.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Auto-population of system-of-record fields (orders, claims, onboarding records, obligations registers); exception queues by failure cause; downstream workflow triggering (an extracted order starts fulfillment); source-system feedback (rejecting malformed submissions at intake). Decision rules: straight-through eligibility per document type × confidence × amount, mandatory review classes (payment details, legal terms), sampling rates.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — ERP/CRM/claims/loan-origination systems (the write targets), intake channels (email rooms, portals, EDI-fallback), workflow engines, human-review UIs (the underrated component — reviewer ergonomics set program economics).
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Operations teams shift from keying to exception review; data owners define schemas + validators; platform team owns eval/regression. Replaces data-entry roles and template-maintenance drudgery of classic OCR.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Confidence-routed review; dual-control on high-risk fields; reviewer corrections captured as labels; periodic blind re-keying audits calibrate the confidence system itself.
9. **Measurement loop** — Straight-through rate, field-level accuracy on audit samples, cost/latency per document vs manual baseline, downstream error incidents traced to extraction (the loop that matters), reviewer throughput. Phased by document type. The AP benchmark numbers (dossier 25) anchor the economics; JPMorgan COIN (dossier 26: ~360k hours/year) is the family's canonical magnitude (Bloomberg, 2017).
10. **Operational architecture** — Near-real-time or batch per channel; queue-based with retry/dead-letter discipline; model versions pinned + canaried; monitoring: per-source accuracy, validator-failure rates, confidence-distribution drift, cost per document.
11. **Governance & safety** — Data-privacy processing agreements (documents contain PII); auditability of every write (source snippet ↔ field lineage); retention rules; SOX-relevant when feeding financial records (control design as dossier 25).
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — The fastest-ROI LLM pattern in most enterprises: one document type end-to-end (intake → validated write → review queue) in weeks; scale-out is schema/validator authoring per type. LLMs collapsed the per-template engineering that made classic IDP projects slow.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Ops teams promoted to reviewers/QA — usually welcomed; downstream system owners need the audit evidence before accepting auto-writes; start with read-then-confirm (human commits) and graduate to auto-commit per type on evidence.
14. **Documented deployments** — Touchless-AP category (dossier 25 benchmarks); COIN (2017); insurance FNOL/email-room automation (carrier programs; vendor-reported, 2020s); healthcare intake/prior-auth extraction (2023+; vendor-documented); the pattern's ubiquity since 2023 outruns its published measurement — programs should self-instrument per the loop above.
15. **Failure modes** — Silent field corruption discovered months later (no sampling audit); validator gaps promoted to production incidents; per-source drift unmonitored; prompt injection via document content; model-upgrade regressions without a regression suite; reviewer-queue starvation flipping the economics (review costs exceed keying).
16. **Variants** — Finance documents (invoices, remittances, bank statements); legal (dossier 26); healthcare (charts, prior-auth — HIPAA-grade governance); logistics (BOLs, customs); voice-transcript extraction (call dispositions → CRM); multimodal (photos + documents, insurance claims).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Both, converging: IDP vendors (ABBYY→LLM-era: Instabase, Hyperscience, cloud Document AI) vs in-house LLM pipelines (weeks of engineering); buy for regulated packaged flows, build for proprietary schemas; validators and audits in-house always.
18. **Key references** — Bloomberg on COIN (2017); AP benchmarks (Ardent/APQC via secondary, 2020s); structured-output/function-calling provider documentation (2023+); IDP-vendor factual capability docs.

### 32. Agentic workflow automation with tool access

1. **Name** — Agentic workflow automation (LLM agents with tools, approvals, and audit).
2. **Use case solved** — Multi-step business workflows spanning systems (research → draft → update systems → notify) that scripted RPA handles brittlely and humans handle expensively.
3. **Methodology composed from** — LLM agent methodology: goal decomposition/planning → tool invocation (APIs, search, databases) → iterative reasoning on results → checkpointed execution, inside an authority envelope (allowed tools, approval gates, budgets, audit); the youngest methodology in the catalog and the least production-proven.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **Authority-envelope design is the correctness core**: tools scoped minimally (read vs write separated), spend/action budgets per run, idempotency + rollback paths on all write tools, environment sandboxing — the agent is treated as an untrusted-but-useful operator, with safety in the *harness*, not the model's judgment. (ii) **Approval gates at irreversibility boundaries**: plans execute freely on read/draft steps; state-changing steps (send, pay, delete, commit) checkpoint for human approval until per-step-class evidence justifies graduation — dossier 22's risk-tier ladder generalized. (iii) **Compounding-error math**: a 95%-reliable step compounds to ~60% over ten steps; correctness requires verifiable intermediate artifacts, retry-with-verification loops, and task decomposition that keeps chains short. (iv) **Prompt-injection defense across every ingested surface** (web pages, emails, documents — all attack vectors when an agent acts on their content; the confused-deputy problem is this family's signature vulnerability). (v) **Evaluation on end-to-end task success** (golden workflows with graded rubrics), not step plausibility; regression on every model/prompt/tool change. (vi) Deterministic-first discipline: anything expressible as reliable code becomes a tool; the LLM handles only the genuinely judgmental residue.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — The agent *performs* the workflow: compiles the vendor-risk dossier and files it; triages the queue and drafts responses; reconciles the discrepancy and posts the journal-entry proposal. Decision rules live in the envelope: tool allowlists per workflow, approval matrices by action class × amount, escalation on low confidence/policy ambiguity, kill-switches, and human ownership of every workflow (an accountable person per agent, by name).
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — Whatever the workflow touches (CRM, ERP, ticketing, email, document stores) via governed APIs/MCP-style tool layers; orchestration platforms; audit/log infrastructure as a first-class system.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Process owners define workflows and graduation criteria; operators supervise queues of agent runs (the new role: agent-run reviewer); platform team owns the harness. Replaces swivel-chair work and brittle RPA scripts.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — The graduation ladder is the design: observe (agent drafts, human executes) → approve (human gates writes) → notify (agent executes, human informed) → autonomous (bounded classes only); per-step-class, evidence-driven, reversible. Gartner's forecast that >40% of agentic-AI projects will be canceled by 2027 (Gartner, 2025) is best read as a prediction about programs that skipped this ladder.
9. **Measurement loop** — End-to-end task success rate vs human baseline (quality-sampled), cost + latency per completed workflow, intervention rate (how often humans must rescue), incident count by severity, and honest attribution (time saved measured, not asserted — the METR result that developers *believed* AI made them 20% faster while measuring 19% slower (METR RCT, 2025) is the standing warning against self-reported agent productivity). Phased by workflow; guardrails: error-escape rate to customers/ledgers, injection-attempt detections, budget overruns.
10. **Operational architecture** — Asynchronous run-based execution with checkpoints/resumability; full trace logging (every prompt, tool call, result); model version pinning + canary; cost metering per run; monitoring: success-rate drift, tool-failure rates, approval-queue latency.
11. **Governance & safety** — Named accountable owner per agent; access provisioning as for a human role (least privilege, joiner/leaver processes for agents); audit trails to compliance grade; incident process including agent-specific post-mortems; EU AI Act obligations by use-case risk class; the emerging-practice frontier — treat every framework claim skeptically.
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — One well-chosen workflow (read-heavy, verifiable, low blast radius — e.g. research/dossier assembly, triage drafting) to observe/approve mode: weeks. Autonomous write-authority: quarters of evidence. The long pole is tool/API quality and the approval UX, not the model.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Operators trust agents whose runs are inspectable (traces readable, artifacts diffable); start where errors are cheap and verification easy; publicize intervention stats; resist demo-driven scope inflation (the demo-to-production gap is widest in this family — consistent with the MIT NANDA 95% finding for GenAI pilots broadly, 2025).
14. **Documented deployments** — Early production evidence concentrates in: coding agents (bounded by tests/review — the verifiable-artifact advantage), support-workflow agents (dossier 8's vendors adding actions), and back-office document workflows (dossier 31 with agentic orchestration); named, audited end-to-end business deployments with quantified outcomes remain scarce as of this writing (2026) — stated plainly; adoption breadth (vendor platform launches: Salesforce Agentforce, ServiceNow, Microsoft, 2024–2025) far exceeds published measured value.
15. **Failure modes** — Confused-deputy/prompt-injection incidents; compounding-error workflows shipping wrong outcomes at scale; cost blowouts (unbounded loops); accountability vacuums ("the agent did it"); RPA-redux brittleness when workflows change; canceled programs after unmeasured pilots (the Gartner 40% forecast, 2025).
16. **Variants** — Coding agents (most proven — verifiable substrate); research/analysis agents (read-only, low risk); ops agents (ticket/queue processing); finance back-office agents (highest control burden); multi-agent orchestration (mostly experimental as of 2026).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Platforms erupting (Agentforce, Copilot Studio, ServiceNow, LangGraph/temporal-style orchestration + frontier-model APIs); serious deployments currently build the harness (envelope, evals, audit) even atop bought platforms; the governance layer cannot be bought.
18. **Key references** — Gartner agentic-cancellation forecast (2025); METR developer RCT (2025) for measurement humility; MIT NANDA (2025); prompt-injection/confused-deputy security literature (2023+); provider agent-safety documentation.

### 33. Copilots embedded in existing workflows

1. **Name** — Copilots embedded in existing workflows (coding, writing, analysis).
2. **Use case solved** — Accelerating skilled work in situ — code completion, document drafting, meeting synthesis, spreadsheet analysis — without changing the system of work.
3. **Methodology composed from** — LLM generation methodology embedded in the tool of work (context assembly from the open artifact → suggestion/draft generation → human accept/edit), plus telemetry for measurement; the assist pattern (dossier 9) generalized beyond contact centers.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — (i) **The human-verification bottleneck is the design constraint**: value = generation quality × verification cost; suggestions must be cheap to check (small diffs, visible sources, testable units) or the copilot *slows experts down* — the METR RCT found experienced open-source developers 19% *slower* with AI assistance on familiar codebases while believing they were ~20% faster (METR, 2025; early-2025 cohort), the sharpest correctness lesson in the family: perceived speed is not measured speed. (ii) **Skill-heterogeneous rollout**: gains concentrate in juniors and unfamiliar tasks (NBER agent study: +34% novices vs +14% average, 2023; Copilot RCTs: large gains on scoped tasks — Peng et al.: 55.8% faster on a standardized coding task, 2023; multi-firm RCTs: ~26% more PRs/week across 4,000+ developers at Microsoft/Accenture, 2022–2024) — targeting and training should follow the heterogeneity, not the average. (iii) **Quality guardrails against silent degradation**: one field study found no significant throughput change but 41% more bugs among Copilot users (Uplevel telemetry study, ~800 developers, 2024); review rigor, test gates, and security scanning must not relax as volume rises. (iv) Context-boundary hygiene (the copilot sees what the file shows it — secrets/PII redaction in context assembly). (v) IP/provenance controls (code-license filters, citation surfacing). (vi) Version pinning + prompt regression for embedded behaviors (an editor update should not silently change suggestion behavior).
5. **Business wiring — actions** — Accepted suggestions become the work product directly (code committed, document sent) — wiring is *in the artifact*, with the existing quality process (code review, CI, editorial review) as the action gate; decision rules: where autocomplete is on by default, which repos/documents are excluded, mandatory review policies unchanged or tightened.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — IDEs (Copilot/Cursor-class), office suites (M365 Copilot, Docs), meeting platforms, BI/notebook tools; SSO/data-boundary integration; telemetry pipelines for measurement.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Individual contributors consume continuously; engineering/knowledge-work leadership owns policy and measurement; enablement teams train prompt/verification habits. Augments rather than replaces roles — but shifts effort toward review/verification (the process change organizations under-plan).
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Human accepts every artifact by construction; the risk is *rubber-stamping at scale* — countered by review-quality metrics, not by the copilot itself; no escalation structure needed beyond existing quality gates, which must hold.
9. **Measurement loop** — The best-studied loop in the LLM era, and the most cautionary: RCTs where possible (staggered rollout across teams — the Microsoft/Accenture design: 12.9–21.8% and 7.5–8.7% more PRs respectively, 2022–2024), telemetry (cycle time, review time, defect rates) with the Uplevel counter-result in mind (2024), task-level studies (Microsoft: tasks in 26–73% of control time on scoped exercises, 2023), and the METR warning that self-report inverts reality for experts on familiar ground (2025). Guardrails: bug/incident rates, review depth, security-finding rates, maintainability drift. The honest synthesis: large gains on scoped/junior/unfamiliar work; neutral-to-negative on expert work in familiar complex systems; measurement must be local, not imported from vendor decks.
10. **Operational architecture** — Real-time inline latency (<1s); enterprise data-boundary deployment (no training on tenant data); model updates canaried; monitoring: acceptance rates by cohort, telemetry health, cost per seat vs usage.
11. **Governance & safety** — Data boundaries (source code/documents to external APIs under contract); license/IP policy for generated code; secrets-in-context prevention; disclosure norms for AI-drafted external documents; works-council agreements on productivity telemetry (measurement must not become surveillance — dossier 9's constraint).
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — Deployment is trivially fast (seats + SSO); *measured* value takes a quarter of disciplined telemetry; the long pole is training verification habits and adjusting quality processes.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Adoption is organic (the rare solution that markets itself); the management task is calibration: pairing enthusiasm with the METR/Uplevel evidence, targeting juniors/unfamiliar-task use, and keeping review culture strong; ban-then-shadow-IT is the documented anti-pattern.
14. **Documented deployments** — GitHub Copilot enterprise-wide at thousands of firms (Peng et al. RCT 2023; multi-firm RCTs 2022–2024, figures above); M365 Copilot at Fortune-500 breadth (Microsoft studies, 2023–2024; vendor-run); METR (2025) and Uplevel (2024) as the counterweight studies; the NBER contact-center RCT (2023) as the strongest causal template (dossier 9).
15. **Failure modes** — Expert slowdown masked by perceived speedup (METR, 2025); quality erosion under volume (Uplevel bug finding, 2024); seat-cost sprawl without usage; verification-culture decay (the compounding risk); benchmark-driven procurement ignoring local measurement; morale/skill-formation concerns for juniors if copilots substitute for learning (open research question as of 2026 — flagged, not concluded).
16. **Variants** — Coding (most evidence), office/writing, meeting synthesis, analyst/BI copilots (text-to-SQL with verification gates), domain copilots (legal drafting, clinical documentation — heavier governance).
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Bought, near-universally (Copilot/Cursor/M365/Gemini); build only thin internal wrappers for context injection and policy; measurement infrastructure is the in-house work that separates value from vibes.
18. **Key references** — Peng et al. (arXiv, 2023); MIT/Princeton/Wharton multi-firm Copilot RCTs (2022–2024); Microsoft AI & Productivity Report (2023); METR developer RCT (2025) + 2026 follow-up; Uplevel telemetry study (2024); Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (NBER, 2023).

## Family J — Decision-intelligence reporting

### 34. Driver-analysis / report solutions (incl. voice-of-customer loop)

1. **Name** — Driver-analysis / decision-intelligence reporting (the report *is* the product; includes the voice-of-customer → product/ops feedback loop).
2. **Use case solved** — Recurring business questions — *why* is churn/attrition/defects/margin moving, what should we change — answered with modeled evidence routed into decision forums; VoC is the instance where the input is customer feedback text and the decision owner is product/ops.
3. **Methodology composed from** — The tabular supervised methodology deliberately stopped at the report stage: extract → point-in-time features → gradient boosting → SHAP driver analysis → structured report; for VoC, NLP classification/topic extraction feeds the same reporting spine. The anchor churn solution's "wholesale level" (dossier 1, field 5) generalized into its own solution pattern.
4. **Correctness adaptations** — Everything in dossier 1's field 4 applies unchanged (leakage-free snapshots, temporal validation, censoring, imbalance, drift) — a report used for decisions deserves the same correctness as a score used for actions, and gets it less often precisely because "it's just analysis". Plus report-specific correctness: (i) **Association ≠ cause, said out loud**: SHAP ranks model-attribution, not intervention effects; the report must label driver claims as hypotheses with effect-size uncertainty, and the high-stakes ones get causal follow-up (experiments, quasi-experiments) before big money moves. (ii) **Stability discipline**: driver rankings that reshuffle each quarter from noise destroy credibility — confidence intervals via bootstrap, and change-flagging only beyond thresholds. (iii) **Segment honesty**: Simpson's-paradox screening before segment claims. (iv) **Reproducibility**: versioned data + code per report edition (decision forums will ask "what changed since last quarter" — the answer must be computable). (v) VoC-specific: sampling-bias correction (complainers are not the base), topic-model drift as products change, and quote-verbatim traceability from theme to source feedback.
5. **Business wiring — actions** — The action is a *decision by an accountable owner in a standing forum*: pricing committee adjusts the tier, product council re-sequences the roadmap, ops leadership staffs the queue, the board tracks the driver KPI. Wiring that separates working report solutions from dashboard graveyards: (a) **named decision owner per driver domain**; (b) **standing cadence** (the report has a slot in an existing meeting, not a hopeful email); (c) **recommendation format** — each driver carries a proposed action, expected effect size, and confidence; (d) **decision log** — what was decided, by whom, revisited next cycle; (e) **KPI wiring** — each accepted action gets a metric and a check-in date. Decision rules: materiality thresholds for escalation; disagreement between model drivers and owner intuition triggers a designed experiment, not a shrug.
6. **Business wiring — systems of action** — BI layer (the delivery vehicle), decision forums (the real system of action), experiment platform (where driver hypotheses get tested), OKR/KPI tracking, and — for VoC — product-management tools (tickets/epics created from themes) and ops QA systems.
7. **Business wiring — people & process** — Analytics/decision-science team authors; functional leadership decides; the forum cadence (monthly/quarterly) is the heartbeat; VoC adds a closing-the-loop lane back to customers ("you said, we did") that measurably sustains feedback volume (practitioner consensus; qualitative). Replaces anecdote-driven prioritization and one-off analyst decks.
8. **Human-in-the-loop design** — Humans decide everything; the solution's discipline is making the human decision *explicit and tracked* rather than automating it; escalation = materiality-triggered leadership attention.
9. **Measurement loop** — The hardest loop in the compendium, and the differentiator: (a) decision-adoption rate (share of report recommendations that became decisions); (b) tracked outcomes of adopted decisions vs their predicted effects (the report's forecast honesty score); (c) experiments spawned per cycle (a healthy report program generates tests, not just narratives); (d) the driver KPIs themselves trending. A report program that cannot show decisions-and-outcomes within two quarters is a candidate for the dashboard graveyard — the McKinsey finding that only ~39% of AI adopters attribute any EBIT impact (2025) is substantially a description of unwired analysis.
10. **Operational architecture** — Batch, cadence-aligned; automation of the data-to-draft pipeline (increasingly LLM-drafted narrative over computed evidence, human-edited); versioned editions; monitoring = the correctness checks of field 4 run per edition.
11. **Governance & safety** — Modest: access control on sensitive drivers (comp, individual performance — dossier 28's constraints when people are the subject); methodology transparency so decisions can be defended; VoC privacy (feedback quotes de-identified).
12. **Time-to-value & effort profile** — The cheapest entry in the catalog: an existing churn/quality/attrition model's SHAP layer + a disciplined forum = weeks. The forum discipline is the actual build; the analysis is commodity.
13. **Adoption & change management** — Executives adopt reports that are stable, honest about uncertainty, and short; analysts must resist driver-of-the-week storytelling; the decision log is the trust artifact (it proves the program's memory and accountability).
14. **Documented deployments** — Ubiquitous as internal practice, rarely published with numbers (internal decision quality is not a press release); the documented *shape* appears in: Danone's planner/driver reporting inside its forecasting program (2012, dossier 10), VoC programs at scale (NPS-driver analytics as standard practice across telecom/retail — sector-documented, qualitative), and the churn-driver councils of dossier 1. This dossier is deliberately candid: its evidence base is practice-pattern-strong, published-number-weak.
15. **Failure modes** — The dashboard graveyard (report without forum); driver-of-the-week noise; causal overreach (reorganizing the business around a correlation); decision-log absence (no memory, no accountability, no credibility compounding); analyst-forum decoupling (beautiful reports, absent decision owners); VoC theater (themes collected, roadmap untouched).
16. **Variants** — Churn/retention drivers (dossier 1's wholesale layer); quality/defect drivers (plant councils); attrition drivers (dossier 28's aggregate mode — the recommended default there); margin/revenue drivers (CFO forums); VoC → product loop; ESG/ops drivers.
17. **Build vs buy landscape** — Built (the analysis is commodity in-house work); BI platforms bought; augmented-analytics "driver" features exist in BI tools (Power BI key influencers, Tableau Explain Data) as entry points; the forum, log, and experiment discipline cannot be bought — they are management practice.
18. **Key references** — SHAP literature (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) with its own caveats on causal reading; McKinsey State of AI (2025) on the attribution gap; decision-hygiene literature (Kahneman et al., *Noise*, 2021) for the forum design; NPS/VoC practitioner literature.

| **Point-in-time feature discipline** | Future leakage: features computed after the label event, producing inflated offline metrics and useless production models | Snapshot tables / feature store with time-travel; as-of joins; features keyed (entity, as_of_date); leaky-field exclusion lists; "too-good AUC" review | 1, 2, 3, 5, 9–17, 18, 24, 28, 34 — every tabular solution |
| **Label-window & horizon design** | Labels that peek into the future or predict too late to act | Outcome window (T, T+H] strictly after feature window; blackout gaps; horizon matched to action lead time | 1, 3, 11, 13 (label maturity), 15 (vintages), 16, 17, 18, 28 |
| **Temporal validation** | Random-split leakage on time-ordered data; overstated generalization | Out-of-time splits; rolling-window evaluation; vintage analysis; group-aware splits per entity | All tabular + forecasting solutions (1–5, 10–18, 24, 28, 34) |
| **Censoring handling** | Treating "not yet" as "never" (churn, CLV, failures, claims severity) | Survival analysis; horizon-limited targets; BTYD models; fully-observed-window restrictions | 1, 5, 15, 16, 18, 28 |
| **Censored-demand correction** | Learning to under-order where stockouts occurred; biased elasticities | Lost-sales imputation; availability-aware training; stockout flags | 4, 10, 12 (curtailment analog) |
| **Class imbalance & calibration** | Useless accuracy on rare events; uncalibrated probabilities breaking threshold economics | Proper scoring rules, class weights, PR-AUC/recall-at-FPR metrics; post-hoc calibration (isotonic/Platt) | 1, 3, 7, 13, 14, 16, 18, 19, 23, 24 |
| **Selection bias from own decisions** | Models trained only on approved/worked/shown cases canonizing past policy | Exploration slices (randomized approvals/routings/prices); reject inference; off-policy evaluation with propensity logging | 2, 3, 4, 6, 13, 15, 17, 29 |
| **Uplift over propensity** | Targeting people who would act anyway — or who react badly to contact (sleeping dogs) | Treatment-randomized training data; uplift/DML models; persuadable segmentation | 1, 2, 5, 17 |
| **Entity resolution** | Duplicate/fragmented entities corrupting features, labels, and actions aimed at the wrong unit | MDM/identity graphs; deterministic+probabilistic matching; household/account/user hierarchy decisions | 1, 2, 5, 13, 14, 16, 18 (asset state), 21 (topology), 22, 24, 25, 27, 31 |
| **Concept/regime drift handling** | Silent decay as behavior, adversaries, seasons, architectures, or macro regimes shift | PSI/drift monitors on inputs+scores; scheduled + triggered retraining; regime detection; champion/challenger | Everything; acute in 4, 10, 12, 13, 19, 22, 23, 27 (adversarial: 13, 23, 27, 29, 32) |
| **Train/serve consistency** | Features/prompts computed differently offline vs online — the classic silent production bug | Shared feature stores (online/offline parity); single-codepath feature definitions; model+prompt version pinning; regression suites | 1, 2, 6, 13, 19 (environment identity), 30–33 (prompt/model pinning) |
| **Latency-budget engineering** | Correct models that miss the decision window | Precomputation, online stores, model distillation; latency as an explicit constraint | 2, 6, 8, 9, 13, 19, 21 (in-day), 27 |
| **Hard-constraint embedding** | "Optimal" outputs that are illegal, unsafe, or physically impossible | Constraints in the optimizer/policy layer (never as model suggestions): HOS law, safety envelopes, contact caps, MAP/price bands | 4, 10, 17, 20, 21, 22, 23, 32 |
| **Below-the-line / sampling audits of automated decisions** | Silent error accumulation in whatever the system auto-approves or suppresses | Periodic random investigation of suppressed alerts, auto-posted invoices, auto-passed parts, auto-accepted extractions, auto-rejected candidates | 14 (canonical), 16, 19, 23, 25, 29, 31 |
| **Two-sided error economics** | Optimizing one error type while the other destroys more value (false declines vs fraud; false rejects vs escapes) | Cost-weighted thresholds set jointly with business owners; joint KPI reporting | 13 (canonical: $264B false-decline projection, Visa 2023–24), 16, 19, 27 |
| **Grounding + citation (generative)** | Hallucinated policy/claims becoming actions or liabilities (Air Canada, 2024) | Retrieval-restricted generation; mandatory citations; refusal as success; golden-set faithfulness evals | 8, 9, 26, 30, 31 (validators), 32 |
| **Permission-aware retrieval** | Data leakage through the assistant | ACL-filtered indexes; query-time entitlement checks; leak audits | 8, 30, 32 |
| **Schema-constrained output + deterministic validation** | Generative flexibility corrupting structured systems | Typed/function-call outputs; checksum/referential/range validators as the authority; confidence-routed review | 25, 31, 32 |
| **Authority envelopes & graduated automation** | Automation acting beyond earned trust; unbounded blast radius | Tool allowlists; approval gates at irreversibility; shadow→assisted→auto ladders; budgets; kill-switches; auto-expiring actions | 8, 22, 23, 32 (canonical); implicitly 10, 20, 21 |
| **Prompt-injection / adversarial-input defense** | Confused-deputy actions from attacker-controlled content | Content/instruction separation; tool-scope minimization; injection detection; sandboxing | 8, 30, 31, 32; classic-ML analogs in 13, 23, 27 (document fraud), 29 (résumé gaming) |

| **Small-n honesty** | Individual-level claims the data cannot support | Aggregate-level modeling; uncertainty reporting; refusing granularity | 11 (top-level series), 28 (canonical) |

## Synthesis 4 — Action-integration patterns

Four recurring ways model output becomes business action. Every dossier's field 5–6 wiring is one of these (or a staged combination).

| Pattern | Mechanism | When it fits | Canonical solutions | Failure signature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Report → human decision** | Modeled evidence (drivers, forecasts, hotspots) routed into a standing decision forum with named owners, recommendation format, and a decision log | High-stakes, low-frequency, cross-functional decisions; small-n domains; wherever automation is legally/ethically inappropriate (people decisions); early maturity of any solution | 34 driver reports · 28 attrition (aggregate) · 11 forecast reviews · 1 (wholesale layer) · 12 (operator advisory) · 30 (answer at moment of work) | The dashboard graveyard: analysis produced, forum absent, decisions untracked |
| **2. Task in the system of work** | Score/flag/draft materialized as a task, queue order, or suggestion inside the CRM/CMMS/ATS/IDE where the acting human already works, with owners and SLAs | Medium-frequency decisions with human judgment in the loop; field forces and case workers; wherever adoption depends on zero workflow change | 1 (retail layer) · 3 lead queues · 9 agent-assist · 14 AML queues · 18 work orders · 24 patch tickets · 26 obligations tasks · 29 ranked pipelines · 33 suggestions | Score-as-decoration: the field exists in the CRM, nobody's process references it |
| **3. Policy engine** | Model output enters an explicit rules+thresholds layer that renders decisions automatically at volume, with humans governing the policy, not the instance | High-frequency, low-latency, per-instance decisions with quantifiable error costs; regulated decisioning needing decision-level audit | 13 fraud (canonical) · 15 credit · 2 NBA arbitration · 7 auto-routing · 16 STP paths · 17 treatment ladders · 27 onboarding flows · 25 touchless eligibility | Rules sprawl re-accreting around the model until no one can explain a decision; policy and dialer/executor disagreeing |
| **4. Closed-loop automation with guardrails** | Output executes directly against the world (order, price, reject gate, remediation, containment, posting, dispatch), inside hard bounds, canaries, rollback paths, and graduated authority | Actions that are frequent, reversible-or-bounded, and verifiable; where latency or volume excludes humans; only after patterns 1–3 have built the evidence | 10 auto-replenishment · 4 pricing-in-bands · 6 serving · 19 reject gates · 20 bounded control · 21 dispatch · 22 runbooks · 23 containment · 25 posting · 31 writes · 32 agents (the general case) | Automation acting on stale topology/constraints; blast-radius surprises; kill-switch absent; trust destroyed by one unreviewed incident |

Three cross-cutting observations. **First, the patterns are a maturity ladder as much as a taxonomy**: fraud decisioning graduated from analyst reports (1990s) through review queues to policy engines; AIOps and security containment explicitly ship the ladder as doctrine (shadow → assisted → automated); agentic automation (dossier 32) re-walks the same ladder with new machinery. Moving one rung is earned with measurement, not bought with software. **Second, the pattern must match error economics**: irreversible + expensive errors cap out at pattern 2–3 regardless of model quality (credit denials, terminations, medical actions); reversible + cheap + verifiable errors (a re-routed ticket, an expiring host isolation) justify pattern 4 early. **Third, most durable solutions run two patterns simultaneously** — a retail action layer (2/3/4) plus a wholesale report layer (1) — the anchor churn dossier's design, and the reason it compounds: the automated layer harvests today's value while the report layer changes tomorrow's business.

## Synthesis 5 — Measurement-loop patterns

The standard proof discipline, collected once. The uncomfortable premise, grounded in Synthesis 6's statistics: **an unmeasured solution is indistinguishable from a failed one**, and most stalled programs stalled partly because they could not prove incremental value (MIT NANDA, 2025; McKinsey attribution gap, 2025).

| Pattern | Design | What it proves | Where it's mandatory | Pitfalls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Permanent randomized holdout** | A slice of eligible entities (5–10%) never receives the treatment/score-driven action, refreshed to stay representative | Program-level incrementality vs "would have happened anyway" | Churn/retention (1), NBA (2), CLV/promo (5), collections (17) | Holdout eroded by exceptions ("we can't not-treat that account"); leakage of treatment via shared channels; never sized for significance |
| **Uplift-designed A/B (treatment experiments)** | Randomize *among treatments* (offer A/B/none) within targeted populations; train and evaluate on incremental response | Which action works, for whom; persuadable vs sleeping-dog segmentation (Fidelidade evidence, 2020s) | Retention offers, promos, collections treatments, proactive care | Measuring response instead of lift; contaminated cells (ops overrides); short windows missing delayed effects |
| **Champion/challenger** | Incumbent decision policy runs; challenger scores in shadow or on a traffic slice; promote on evidence | Safe model/policy iteration under live conditions | Fraud (13, canonical), credit (15, swap-set analysis), AML thresholds (14), forecasting (FVA) | Shadow results ignored (no promotion criteria); challenger judged on different label windows; swap-set economics skipped |
| **Phased / staggered rollout** | Deploy by queue, region, category, asset class, or team in sequence; staggered starts create built-in controls | Before/after with contemporaneous comparison; the working substitute when randomization is impractical | Triage (7), replenishment (10), PdM (18), inspection (19), routing (21), copilots (9/33 — the NBER design, 2023) | Non-comparable phases (easiest region first); seasonal confounds; declaring victory after phase one |
| **Switchback / geo experiments** | Alternate policy on/off over time windows or randomize by geography when units interfere | Effects under network interference (pricing, dispatch, marketing) | Dynamic pricing (4), routing/dispatch (21), media spend (5) | Carryover between windows; too-few switches; geo mismatch |
| **Exploration slices** | Small randomized overrides of the policy (approve gray-zone transactions, work low-scored leads, show random arms) | Keeps selection-biased systems learnable; quantifies what the policy forgoes | Fraud (13), lead scoring (3), NBA (2), credit (15 — controlled) | Exploration cut in cost reviews (the model then calcifies); unbounded exploration harming customers |
| **Below-the-line / audit sampling** | Random samples of the automated path (suppressed alerts, auto-posted invoices, auto-passed parts, auto-accepted extractions) re-worked by humans | Bounds silent-error rates of automation; regulator-grade assurance | AML suppression (14, canonical), touchless AP (25), inspection escapes (19), extraction (31), auto-containment (23) | Sampling too small; findings not wired to threshold changes; audits quietly dropped after headcount cuts |
| **Backtesting / out-of-time simulation** | Replay history with as-of reconstruction; vintage tracking for slow labels | Pre-deployment plausibility; slow-label domains' early warning | Credit vintages (15), forecasting (10–12), collections roll rates (17) | As-of reconstruction faked from current tables (leakage re-enters); backtest treated as proof rather than screen |
| **Guardrail metric suites** | Counter-metrics monitored alongside the target: complaints, NPS, margin, false-reject/decline costs, fairness ratios, review depth, burnout indicators | That the win isn't purchased with invisible losses (two-sided economics) | Everywhere; canonical in fraud (approval rate vs fraud bps), deflection (repeat-contact vs containment), copilots (bug rates vs throughput — Uplevel 2024) | Guardrails defined but not gating; gamed targets (save-rate, containment, alerts-closed); Goodhart everywhere |
| **Decision/outcome logging** | Every model-informed decision recorded with predicted effect, actual outcome, and owner; reviewed on cadence | The report-layer's proof (decision-adoption rate, forecast honesty); institutional memory | Driver reports (34), forecast programs (11), pricing committees (4) | Logs kept, never reviewed; predictions vague enough to always "hit" |

The loop's second half — **feeding back** — separates measurement from accounting: treatment outcomes become uplift labels; audit findings retune thresholds; guardrail breaches rewrite playbooks; drift alarms trigger retraining; decision logs re-rank the driver agenda. A solution whose measurement only reports (and never re-parameterizes the model, the policy, or the playbook) is half-built.

## Synthesis 6 — Why solutions fail

The evidence-backed synthesis of the pilot-to-production gap.

**The headline statistics.** Gartner: ≥30% of GenAI projects abandoned after PoC by end-2025 (forecast, 2024); >40% of agentic-AI projects to be canceled by end-2027, citing escalating costs, unclear business value, inadequate risk controls (2025). MIT NANDA: ~95% of enterprise GenAI pilots show no measurable P&L impact; the ~5% that do integrate deeply into workflows and buy/partner more than they build (2025). McKinsey: 88% of organizations use AI; only ~39% attribute any EBIT impact; ~6% attribute >5% of EBIT (2025). Earlier-era ML: 55% of companies took >6 months to deploy a model (Algorithmia survey, 2020); the famous "87% never reach production" lacks a verifiable primary source (citation-chain folklore — flagged, not used). The consistent shape across a decade and across both ML and GenAI generations: **building models is not the constraint; wiring, proving, and governing them is.**

**Failure cause 1 — the action gap (organizational).** The most common stall across dossiers: scores without owners (1, 3), alerts without work orders (18 — the documented PdM pilot-killer), findings without patch throughput (24), reports without forums (34), SOAR without playbooks (23). The deployed successes did the opposite: UPS spent years wiring ORION into driver workflows and constraint sets (2012–2016); Danone rebuilt planner roles around exceptions (2012); the NBER firm shipped assist inside the agent desktop, not beside it (2023). *Diagnostic: name the person whose Monday changes when the model ships. No name, no solution.*

**Failure cause 2 — measurement missing or dishonest.** Programs that cannot show incrementality die in budget cycles (holdout-free retention programs claiming natural retention — dossier 1's most documented failure); programs that measure the wrong thing die slower (containment vs resolution in deflection — the Klarna 2024→2025 arc; redemption vs lift in promos; plan-file savings vs adherence-measured savings in routing; perceived vs measured speedup in copilots — METR 2025's 19%-slower-while-feeling-20%-faster result). MIT NANDA's core finding is substantially this: pilots without P&L instrumentation cannot graduate (2025).

**Failure cause 3 — correctness debt surfacing late.** Leakage-inflated pilots that collapse on contact with production (the 0.99-AUC churn studies; CRM-hygiene leaks in lead/opportunity scoring); drift unmonitored until a regime break — Zillow Offers is the canonical catastrophe: home-pricing models meeting pandemic-era volatility while wired to *autonomous high-stakes purchasing at quota-driven scale*, ending in a >$500M inventory write-down, ~25% workforce reduction, and exit (Nov 2021; the unit lost $881M in 2021 — WSJ, 2022); Google Flu Trends' overfit-then-drift arc (~2× overestimate in 2012–13; Lazer et al., *Science*, 2014; retired 2015); the Epic sepsis model's external-validity collapse (AUC 0.63, missed two-thirds of sepsis cases at deployed thresholds while alerting broadly — JAMA Internal Medicine, 2021). The pattern: correctness adaptations (temporal honesty, drift monitoring, external validation, blast-radius limits) were skipped where they were most needed.

**Failure cause 4 — trust and adoption collapse.** ORION's early routes that violated driver knowledge; alert streams exceeding maintenance capacity (18); false-alarm streaks killing inspection systems (19); one automation-made-it-worse incident freezing AIOps programs (22); forecast tools ignored where comp attaches to the human number (11). Successes engineered trust deliberately: reason codes (1, 3, 15), shadow/side-by-side periods (7, 9, 12, 23), override rights with tracked overrides (4, 10, 20), and honest internal accuracy publishing (30). Trust is a designed property with a budget line, not a hoped-for byproduct.

**Failure cause 5 — incentive misalignment.** Save-rate-comped retention teams gaming easy saves (1); growth-vs-fraud threshold wars (13, 27); sales comp fighting the forecast (11); recovery-per-call comp fighting digital collections (17); metric gaming documented wherever a single KPI gated rewards (Goodhart, everywhere). The fix that recurs in successes: joint KPI reporting owned above the contending teams.

**Failure cause 6 — governance surfacing as a late veto (or as scandal).** Programs built without early legal/compliance/works-council engagement die at deployment review (28, 29) — or worse, deploy and detonate: Amazon's biased recruiting model (built 2014, scrapped by 2017, Reuters 2018); the Apple Card/Goldman NYDFS investigation (2019); the Dutch childcare-benefits scandal — algorithmic risk profiling contributing to ~26,000 families wrongly pursued, the SyRI system struck down by court (Feb 2020), the government resigning over the affair (Jan 2021); IBM Watson for Oncology's unsafe-recommendation findings and MD Anderson's ~$62M abandoned project (audit-reported, 2017). GenAI adds liability precedents: Air Canada held liable for its chatbot's invented policy (2024). The deployed successes treat governance as a design input (dossiers 14, 15, 27's examiner-ready postures) — expensive, and cheaper than the alternative.

**Failure cause 7 — integration/data-readiness underestimation.** The long poles named in nearly every dossier's field 12 are integration and data, not modeling: entity resolution (1, 27), historical label reconstruction (1, 18), CMDB/topology quality (22, 24), CRM hygiene (3, 11), content quality (30), ERP change management (10, 25). Algorithmia's 6-month-plus deployment finding (2020) and every subsequent survey say the same thing; budgets that allocate most spend to modeling have the ratio inverted.

**What the successes did differently — the recurring six:** (1) wired action before polishing accuracy (a mediocre model in the CRM beats a great one in a notebook); (2) instrumented incrementality from day one (holdouts/phasing designed in, not retrofitted); (3) paid the correctness debt up front (point-in-time infrastructure, drift monitors, audits); (4) climbed the automation ladder with evidence (shadow → assisted → automated); (5) engineered trust and incentives explicitly; (6) treated governance as a requirement, not a review. These six are the anatomy of Synthesis 1 read as a checklist.
