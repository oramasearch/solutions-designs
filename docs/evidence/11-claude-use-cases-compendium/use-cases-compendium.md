## Executive orientation

**What a use case is.** Per the governing vocabulary, a **use case is the business problem being solved** — stated independently of any way of solving it. Churn, invoice fraud, demand forecasting, ticket triage: each is a *generic problem type* that thousands of companies instantiate on their own data. This compendium catalogs that **problem layer** only: what the problems are, who has them, what they are worth, what data they naturally produce, what actions a correct decision enables, and what it takes for them to be attackable. Methodologies (the arranged AI methods) appear only as a one-line orientation per entry (field 16); solutions (methodology + correct application + business wiring) are out of scope.

**How the space is organized.** Twelve families: eleven business-function families (A–K: customer/revenue, service & support, marketing, finance & accounting, fraud/risk/compliance, supply chain, manufacturing, IT/engineering/security, HR, legal/document, executive/cross-functional) plus one family (L) of industry-specific problems (healthcare, banking, insurance, retail/CPG, telecom, energy, public sector, real estate/construction). The compendium enumerates **124 distinct use cases** across these families. Almost every use case reduces to the same skeleton: an **entity** (customer, transaction, machine, ticket, SKU, claim, document), a **decision** made about it repeatedly, a **label** that defines the right answer, and a **horizon** over which the decision matters — this is the shape that makes the problems tractable and comparable.

**Where value concentrates.** McKinsey Global Institute sizes generative AI's potential at **$2.6–4.4T annually** across 63 use cases, with ~75% of that concentrated in **customer operations, marketing & sales, software engineering, and R&D** (McKinsey, *The Economic Potential of Generative AI*, 2023 — a projection, not a measurement). The same report puts non-generative AI/analytics value at **$11–17.7T annually** — i.e., the classic tabular decision problems (forecasting, risk scoring, pricing, maintenance) still carry the larger pool. In practice, measured value clusters where (a) the decision repeats at high frequency on data a system of record already captures, (b) a concrete action system exists to execute the decision, and (c) a hard operating KPI already measures the outcome. That triple — data exhaust, actionability, measurability — recurs in every dossier below and drives the feasibility ranking. Adoption evidence agrees: 88% of organizations now use AI in at least one function, but only ~one-third have begun scaling and just 7% report AI fully scaled (McKinsey State of AI, 2025); Gartner finds only ~48% of AI projects reach production (2024); MIT's Project NANDA found ~95% of GenAI pilots produced no measurable P&L impact within six months (2025, methodology contested — see Synthesis 7).

**Format of this compendium (two-tier).** Within each family, the **2–4 flagship use cases** (highest value and prevalence) get **full 17-field dossiers** following the template in the research prompt. The remaining use cases in the family are covered in a **compact per-family table** that still carries the core fields: entity/decision/label/horizon · data exhaust · actions enabled · KPIs · typical methodology one-liner · prevalence note. Churn is the first full dossier — the anchoring example. All quantitative claims cite source and year inline; **measured results are distinguished from projections**; anything that could not be source-verified is stated qualitatively or marked "(estimate; not source-verified)". After the twelve family sections come the seven cross-cutting syntheses.

## A. Customer / revenue

12 use cases. Full dossiers: **churn**, **pricing & discount optimization**, **personalization & recommendations**, **lead scoring**. Compact table: CLV, next-best-action, opportunity/win prediction, sales forecasting, promotion effectiveness, market-basket/cross-sell, customer feedback & sentiment, voice-of-customer mining.

### A1. Churn prediction & retention — FULL DOSSIER (anchoring example)

1. **Name + aliases** — Churn; attrition; customer retention; defection prediction; lapse prediction (insurance); dormancy/silent churn (banking, retail); logo churn vs revenue churn (SaaS); non-renewal risk.
2. **Problem statement** — Customers leave, downgrade, or go dormant, destroying recurring revenue and wasting the acquisition cost already sunk into them. The business wants to know **which customers will leave, when, and why**, early enough that a targeted intervention can keep them.
3. **Who has it** — Every subscription or repeat-relationship business: SaaS, telecom, banking, insurance, media/streaming, utilities, fitness, e-commerce/DTC, gaming, payments/merchant acquiring, healthcare payers. Lives in **customer success / retention / growth**; the outcome is owned by a VP Customer Success, CRO, or retention lead (telco: base management; insurance: renewal/persistency team).
4. **Economic pool** — Acquiring a new customer costs **5–25× more** than retaining one (HBR/Bain, Markey, 2014); a **5-point retention increase raises profits 25–95%** (Reichheld & Sasser via Bain, HBR 1990/2014). Typical annual churn: B2B SaaS ~5–7% (ProfitWell/Vena benchmarks, 2020s), telecom ~15–25% (Deloitte-cited benchmarks), retail banking ~10–15%, insurance ~10–17% (J.D. Power 2024, McKinsey-cited benchmarks). Illustrative scale: cutting churn from 10%→8% on $10M ARR compounds to ~$1.3M added revenue over 5 years (ProfitWell model). For a large telco, each point of churn is typically worth hundreds of millions in enterprise value (estimate; not source-verified).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the account / subscriber / policyholder / merchant (B2B: account, sometimes seat). **Decision:** intervene or not, and with what treatment. **Label:** churned within the horizon (cancellation event, non-renewal, or inactivity definition — the label definition is itself a design decision). **Horizon:** 30–90 days (consumer/telco), 1 renewal cycle (B2B SaaS/insurance, 3–12 months); scored weekly or monthly.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Every active customer is re-scored each cycle: a mid-size telco scores millions of subscribers monthly; a B2B SaaS scores thousands of accounts weekly. Value concentrates in the **high-CLV top decile** — intervention budgets only cover a small fraction of the base, so ranking quality at the top matters far more than global accuracy.
7. **Data exhaust** — Product usage/telemetry (logins, feature events, declining engagement), billing & payments (plan, invoices, failed payments, discounts), support tickets and complaint history, CRM (contacts, health notes, QBRs), NPS/CSAT, contract/renewal dates, marketing engagement. Systems of record: **CRM (Salesforce/HubSpot), billing (Stripe/Zuora/BSS), product analytics (Amplitude/Segment/warehouse events), support (Zendesk)**. This exhaust almost always exists — churn is among the best-served problems by default data capture; the common gap is *joining* it at the account level.
8. **Actionability** — Ranked risk list + churn drivers → CSM outreach task (CRM queue), save-desk routing, targeted offer/discount, service-recovery play after bad support experience, product nudge campaigns, renewal escalation, win-back sequencing. Runs through CRM tasks, marketing automation, and save-desk workflows. Actionability is high *if* drivers are attributed (why, not just who) — a bare score without reasons routinely goes unused.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs already tracked: gross/net churn rate, net revenue retention (NRR), renewal rate, save rate, CLV. Impact proven by **holdout/A-B on the intervention** (treat half the flagged accounts), or before/after on cohort churn. The clean design (uplift on treated-vs-control among at-risk) is well understood, which makes churn one of the most provable use cases.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — 12–24 months of history with churn events; a defensible churn label (hard in non-contractual retail — churn must be *defined*); point-in-time feature discipline (leakage is the classic failure: using post-cancellation signals); an owner with an intervention budget. Feasibility is high: tabular data, natural labels, existing KPIs. The organizational prerequisite — someone empowered to act on the list — fails more often than the model.
11. **Industry variants** — **SaaS:** revenue churn ≠ logo churn; expansion offsets; renewal-date driven. **Telecom:** contract-end cliffs, handset upgrade cycles, network-quality drivers; dual prepaid (inactivity label) vs postpaid (cancel label). **Banking:** silent attrition (balances drift to zero without closure) — label is behavioral; regulation limits offers. **Insurance:** lapse/persistency; price-driven at renewal, agent-mediated. **Gaming/media:** engagement-decay churn on daily telemetry. **B2B:** few, large accounts → small-N problem; champion departure is a top driver.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — CLV (churn is one input to lifetime value), next-best-action (treatment choice after risk is known), dunning/involuntary churn (failed payments — a distinct, more mechanical problem), win-back (post-churn), engagement scoring/health scores (leading indicator, weaker label). Boundary: churn = *will they leave*; NBA = *what do we do about it*.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — One of the most-deployed predictive use cases in subscription industries; telco churn modeling has been standard practice for two decades (case-study density is very high). Sits inside "marketing & sales" and "service operations," consistently the top-adopting functions in McKinsey's State of AI surveys (2024–2025). Effectively table stakes in telecom and mature SaaS; still mostly manual in SMB and long-tail retail.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Telecom operators using predictive churn + targeted offers: ~**15% churn reduction and ~10% ARPU uplift** within ~a year (Deloitte-derived telecom benchmarks cited in churn-statistics compilations, 2020s — measured but anonymized). (b) Netflix attributes **>$1B/year** of value to its recommendation/personalization system primarily via **retention** (Netflix engineering estimates, mid-2010s — company estimate). (c) Vodafone reports churn down **8%** after AI-driven personalized plan recommendations (company-reported, 2020s, secondary source). (d) ProfitWell modeling: 2-point churn cut on $10M ARR → ~$1.3M over 5 years (model, not measurement). Note: rigorously published third-party-audited churn RCTs are rare; most evidence is company-reported.
15. **Failure modes** — Label leakage (features that only exist after the decision to leave); predicting churn that is not addressable (price-shoppers, business closures); score-without-drivers → no one acts; intervention budget missing; offer cannibalization (discounting customers who would have stayed); survivorship-biased training data; treating a ranking problem as a classification-accuracy problem; no holdout, so impact can never be proven and the program dies at budget review.
16. **Typical methodology** — The canonical tabular pipeline: extract → point-in-time feature engineering → gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost) → SHAP driver analysis → ranked report; survival analysis or uplift modeling as refinements.
17. **Key references** — Reichheld & Sasser, "Zero Defections" (HBR 1990); Markey, "The Value of Keeping the Right Customers" (HBR 2014); recurring churn-benchmark reports (Recurly, ProfitWell/Paddle, Vena 2024–2025); telecom churn literature (Deloitte benchmarks); McKinsey State of AI (2024, 2025) for function-level adoption.

### A2. Pricing & discount optimization — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Price optimization; dynamic pricing; markdown optimization (retail); deal/discount guidance (B2B); revenue management (travel/hospitality); price-pack architecture (CPG).
2. **Problem statement** — Prices and discounts are set by rules of thumb, list-price anchoring, or sales-rep discretion, leaving margin on the table on some transactions and losing volume on others. The business wants each price/discount set to what the customer, channel, and moment will actually bear.
3. **Who has it** — Universal wherever price is a lever: retail/e-commerce, CPG, B2B distribution and manufacturing, software, airlines/hotels (mature revenue management), insurance (rate-making, regulated), logistics. Lives in **pricing/revenue management**, commercial ops, or finance; owned by a CPO (pricing), CRO, or GM of revenue management.
4. **Economic pool** — A **1% price improvement raises operating profit ~8%** for the average S&P 1500 firm (McKinsey, "The Power of Pricing"), **8.7%** for the Global 1200 (McKinsey pricing infrastructure study) — arithmetic on income statements, not a projection. Across 1,000+ B2B pricing initiatives, typical **return-on-sales uplift is 2–7 points** (McKinsey B2B pricing ebook). Pricing is among the highest leverage-per-decision pools in the catalog.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** SKU × channel × time (B2C) or deal/quote (B2B). **Decision:** the price, discount ceiling, or markdown timing. **Label:** realized demand/win at the offered price (accept/reject, units sold) — partially censored: you only observe demand at prices actually offered. **Horizon:** real-time to weekly (e-commerce/revenue management); per-quote (B2B); seasonal (markdowns).
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Extreme: a large retailer reprices millions of SKU-store combinations weekly; airlines reprice continuously; a B2B firm prices hundreds of quotes daily. Value concentrates in high-volume SKUs and large deals — the top decile of quotes typically carries most of the discount leakage.
7. **Data exhaust** — Transaction history (price, quantity, discount, win/loss), quote/CPQ records, competitor prices (scraped/syndicated), cost data (ERP), inventory position, customer segment attributes, promotion calendars. Systems of record: **ERP, CPQ (Salesforce CPQ), e-commerce platform, POS**. Transaction exhaust is excellent; the recurring gaps are clean **cost-to-serve** data and win/loss labels on lost quotes.
8. **Actionability** — Price recommendations flow directly into a **pricing engine, CPQ guidance (target/floor/stretch per deal), markdown schedule, or dynamic-pricing rule set** — among the most directly actionable use cases because the action *is* a number in a system. B2B guidance requires sales-force adoption (deal desk enforcement or incentive alignment).
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: realized margin %, pocket price, discount depth, win rate, sell-through (markdowns), RevPAR (hospitality). Provable with **A/B by region/category or matched control groups**; McKinsey reports **4–8-point return-on-sales gains vs same-company control groups** in tech B2B deal scoring (McKinsey big-data pricing paper) — measured with controls.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — 1–2+ years of transaction history with true realized prices (pocket price, not list); cost data; elasticity estimation needs price *variation* in history (flat pricing history is a real blocker); B2B needs loss labels. Organizationally: pricing authority must be centralized enough that recommendations stick. Feasibility high in e-commerce/retail; medium in B2B (adoption friction); regulated in insurance/lending.
11. **Industry variants** — **Retail/e-comm:** dynamic + markdown; competitor-reactive. **B2B:** deal-level guidance, discount-leakage control. **Airlines/hotels:** mature revenue management (decades old, OR-based). **Insurance:** actuarial rate-making, heavily regulated, fairness-constrained. **CPG:** trade-promotion + price-pack, retailer-mediated. **SaaS:** packaging/tiering more than per-deal pricing.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Promotion effectiveness (temporary price moves), demand forecasting (shared elasticity core), CLV (price effects on retention), contract/price leakage detection (compliance to negotiated price). Boundary: pricing sets the number; promotion effectiveness evaluates temporary levers; leakage detection audits execution.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Ubiquitous in airlines/hotels (decades), widespread in large retail/e-commerce; B2B AI pricing is actively scaling but far from universal — McKinsey positions B2B pricing AI as a current frontier (2024–2025). Case-study density high; vendor market mature (PROS, Pricefx, Zilliant).
14. **Documented results** — (a) Tech-sector deal scoring: **+4–8 points return on sales vs control** (McKinsey, big-data pricing, 2010s — measured). (b) European building-materials firm: SKU-granular pricing, **margin +3–8 points overall, up to +20% on selected products** (McKinsey case, measured). (c) Qymatix B2B study: sales reps with AI pricing guidance made **+10% profit vs control group** (Qymatix, 2020s — measured, small scale). (d) LTPlabs B2B pilot: **up to +10 points gross-margin delta vs control**, ~€1.5M annualized (vendor case, 2020s). (e) McKinsey (2025): agentic pricing adds **~50+ bps margin on top of ~200 bps from traditional AI** (consultant-reported range).
15. **Failure modes** — Elasticity estimated from confounded observational data (price moves correlated with demand shocks); no price variation in history; ignoring competitor reaction; sales force overrides guidance (incentives unchanged); customer-fairness/PR blowups from visible dynamic pricing; regulatory exposure (collusion via shared algorithms, insurance rate fairness); optimizing margin while destroying share because volume effects were mis-modeled.
16. **Typical methodology** — Tabular demand/elasticity modeling (GBMs or Bayesian hierarchical models) + constrained optimization; B2B: win-probability model + price-guidance bands + SHAP-style deal drivers.
17. **Key references** — McKinsey "The Power of Pricing"; McKinsey "Using Big Data to Make Better Pricing Decisions"; McKinsey B2B pricing ebook (1,000+ initiatives); BCG B2B software pricing (2025); revenue-management literature (Talluri & van Ryzin).

### A3. Personalization & recommendations — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Recommendations; recsys; next-best-offer; personalization at scale; product/content ranking; "customers also bought."
2. **Problem statement** — Customers face far more products/content than they can evaluate; generic experiences waste attention and demand. The business wants each customer shown the items, content, and offers most likely to be relevant to *them*, at each touchpoint.
3. **Who has it** — E-commerce/retail, media & streaming, marketplaces, banking (offer targeting), telco, travel, grocery, B2B distribution (reorder suggestions). Lives in **digital product / e-commerce / growth marketing**; owned by head of digital product or e-commerce GM.
4. **Economic pool** — McKinsey (2013, widely cited) estimated **~35% of Amazon's revenue** is driven by its recommendation engine (analyst estimate, not company disclosure). Netflix estimates its recommender is worth **>$1B/year** via retention (Netflix engineering, mid-2010s — company estimate). McKinsey personalization research: **5–15% revenue lift and 10–30% marketing-spend efficiency** from personalization at scale (McKinsey, mid-2010s onward — consultant estimate ranges, widely reproduced).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the customer × context (session, page, email slot). **Decision:** which K items/offers to rank/show. **Label:** engagement/conversion (click, add-to-cart, purchase, watch time) — abundant implicit labels. **Horizon:** real-time (session) to weekly (email); decided continuously.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — The highest-frequency use case in the catalog: millions–billions of ranking decisions daily at large digital businesses. Value concentrates in high-traffic surfaces (home page, PDP, search results, checkout cross-sell).
7. **Data exhaust** — Clickstream/behavioral events, purchase history, catalog metadata, search queries, ratings/reviews, session context. Systems of record: **e-commerce platform, CDP/product analytics, data warehouse event streams**. Digital businesses have this exhaust in abundance; brick-and-mortar-first firms often lack identity-resolved behavioral data (loyalty programs are the workaround).
8. **Actionability** — Recommendations render directly into the experience (site slots, email content, push, call-center offer prompts) — action latency is zero and no human needs to comply. This "the prediction *is* the action" property is why recsys value compounds.
9. **Measurability** — Gold standard: **continuous A/B testing** on conversion, revenue-per-session, engagement, retention — mature experimentation culture usually already exists where this use case matters. Attribution beyond the session (long-run CLV effects) is harder and often debated.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Identity resolution across sessions/channels; event instrumentation; enough traffic for experimentation; catalog hygiene. Very feasible for digital-native firms; the blocker for others is data plumbing (CDP), not modeling.
11. **Industry variants** — **Streaming/media:** engagement-time objective, cold-start on new content. **Retail/grocery:** basket-aware, repeat-purchase cycles, in-store vs online. **Banking:** offer eligibility + suitability regulation; fewer, higher-stakes offers. **Marketplaces:** two-sided (buyer relevance vs seller fairness/liquidity). **B2B distribution:** reorder prediction dominates discovery.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Next-best-action (adds treatment/channel/timing on top of item relevance), market-basket/cross-sell (transaction-level association vs user-level ranking), search relevance, churn (retention-oriented personalization). Boundary: recsys ranks items; NBA chooses among heterogeneous actions (offer vs service vs nothing).
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Deployed at scale everywhere in digital commerce and media; effectively a solved-infrastructure category for large players and a vendor/platform feature (Amazon Personalize, Algolia, Dynamic Yield) for mid-market. Marketing & sales is the #1 gen-AI adoption function (McKinsey State of AI 2025), with personalization the leading sub-use-case.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Amazon: ~35% of revenue attributed to recommendations (McKinsey estimate, 2013). (b) Netflix: **>$1B/yr** retention value (company estimate, ~2015–2016). (c) Broad meta-claims: 5–15% revenue uplift from personalization (McKinsey — estimate). (d) Vodafone: churn −8% from personalized plan recommendations (company-reported, secondary). Caveat: the headline numbers here are estimates by analysts/companies, not audited measurements; but the underlying A/B evidence culture at digital leaders is strong even where numbers aren't public.
15. **Failure modes** — Popularity bias/feedback loops (recommender trains on its own output); cold start (new users/items); optimizing clicks while cannibalizing margin or long-run satisfaction; identity fragmentation across channels; privacy constraints (consent, GDPR) shrinking usable signal; "creepiness" backlash; offline metrics that don't transfer to online A/B results.
16. **Typical methodology** — Two-stage retrieval + ranking (embeddings/collaborative filtering → GBM/neural ranker), bandits for exploration; LLM-augmented content personalization is the current frontier.
17. **Key references** — McKinsey personalization research (2013 onward); Netflix recommender-systems papers (Gomez-Uribe & Hunt, 2015); industry A/B testing literature (Kohavi et al.); Amazon Personalize case material.

### A4. Lead scoring & prioritization — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Lead scoring; predictive lead scoring; MQL/PQL scoring; deal scoring; propensity-to-buy; sales prioritization.
2. **Problem statement** — Sales capacity is scarce and leads/opportunities vastly outnumber the attention available; reps work leads in the wrong order, chasing unwinnable deals while hot prospects go cold. The business wants inbound leads and open opportunities ranked by likelihood (and size) of conversion so effort lands where it pays.
3. **Who has it** — Any business with a sales team and lead volume: B2B software, financial services, insurance (agent channels), telecom B2B, industrial distribution, education, real estate. Lives in **sales operations / revenue operations / demand gen**; owned by CRO or head of RevOps.
4. **Economic pool** — Pool = sales productivity + conversion uplift on existing demand. Credible measured anchors: McKinsey tech-B2B **deal scoring delivered +4–8 points return-on-sales vs control** (McKinsey pricing/deal-scoring work); Qymatix B2B study: **+10% profit vs control** from AI-guided sales decisions (2020s). Vendor-reported lead-scoring lifts of 10–30% in conversion are common but rarely controlled (estimates; not source-verified individually).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the lead / opportunity / account. **Decision:** work it now, nurture, or discard; who works it. **Label:** converted (MQL→SQL→closed-won) within the horizon. **Horizon:** days–weeks (inbound leads) to quarters (enterprise opportunities); scored on arrival and re-scored on activity.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Hundreds to tens of thousands of leads/day depending on motion; every open opportunity re-ranked weekly. Value concentrates in high-ACV segments and speed-to-lead on inbound (minutes matter).
7. **Data exhaust** — CRM history (stages, outcomes, activities), marketing automation engagement (email, web visits), firmographics/enrichment (industry, size), product usage (PQL motions), call/email metadata. Systems of record: **CRM (Salesforce/HubSpot), marketing automation (Marketo), enrichment vendors (ZoomInfo/Clearbit)**. Exhaust exists wherever CRM discipline exists — CRM hygiene is the universal weak point.
8. **Actionability** — Score → routing/queue order in CRM, SLA triggers on hot leads, nurture-track assignment, territory/capacity allocation, discard automation. Runs through CRM workflow rules and SDR cadences. Requires rep trust; a score reps ignore is worthless.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: lead→opportunity conversion, win rate, sales cycle length, pipeline coverage, revenue per rep. Provable by **randomized routing (scored order vs FIFO)** — cheap to run, rarely actually run; most orgs settle for before/after.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — 1–2 years of closed-loop CRM outcomes; consistent stage definitions; enough volume (small-N enterprise motions defeat statistical scoring); leakage discipline (don't use post-qualification fields). High feasibility technically; the friction is process/CRM data quality — one of the most attackable use cases for a mid-market company.
11. **Industry variants** — **B2B SaaS:** PQL (product signals) vs MQL (marketing signals). **Insurance/financial services:** compliance constraints on differential treatment. **Telecom/utilities B2B:** account-based, small-N. **Real estate/education:** speed-to-lead dominates.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Opportunity/win prediction (later funnel stage, same skeleton), sales forecasting (aggregate of deal probabilities), next-best-action (what to do with the lead), pricing/deal guidance (after qualification). Boundary: lead scoring ranks *whom to work*; win prediction estimates *whether an open deal closes*.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Very widespread as a feature (native scoring in Salesforce Einstein/HubSpot); custom builds common in mid/large B2B. Marketing & sales consistently among top-2 AI-adopting functions (McKinsey State of AI, 2024–2025). More deployed than validated — many deployments never measure uplift.
14. **Documented results** — (a) McKinsey tech-B2B deal scoring: +4–8 pts RoS vs control (measured). (b) Qymatix: +10% profit vs control (measured, small sample). (c) CFPB-documented analog in lending conversion (Upstart, 2019) shows the same ranking machinery validated in a regulated setting. Third-party-controlled lead-scoring studies are thin — most published numbers are vendor claims (noted explicitly).
15. **Failure modes** — Leakage (using fields populated after qualification); self-fulfilling labels (reps only work high scores, so only high scores convert); CRM garbage-in; score without explanation → rep distrust; optimizing MQL volume instead of revenue; small-N enterprise motions where judgment beats statistics; no routing change, so the score changes nothing.
16. **Typical methodology** — Tabular classification (GBM) on CRM+engagement features with SHAP reason codes, feeding CRM routing; uplift modeling where treatment capacity is scarce.
17. **Key references** — McKinsey deal-scoring/pricing publications; Salesforce/HubSpot predictive scoring documentation (product-level prevalence); RevOps practitioner literature; Qymatix B2B study.

## B. Customer service & support

8 use cases. Full dossiers: **ticket triage & routing**, **resolution automation / response drafting**, **agent assist**. Compact table: intent detection, proactive care, call-center analytics, CSAT/escalation prediction, knowledge-base retrieval.

### B1. Ticket triage & routing — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Ticket triage; case classification & routing; intelligent routing; queue assignment; email/case categorization; skill-based routing augmentation.
2. **Problem statement** — Incoming support volume (tickets, emails, chats, calls) must be understood, prioritized, and sent to the right team before anyone can help; done manually it is slow, inconsistent, and expensive, and misroutes multiply handle time and customer frustration.
3. **Who has it** — Every organization with a support queue: software, telecom, banking, insurance, retail, utilities, healthcare, public sector, internal IT/HR help desks. Lives in **customer support / service operations**; owned by VP Support or head of service ops.
4. **Economic pool** — Pool = support labor + cost of misrouting. A human-handled contact costs roughly **$3–8 per call** (industry benchmarks, 2026 compilations; wider $5–15 ranges cited for complex support), so triage/routing quality directly scales a cost line that is often a top-3 operating expense in service-heavy firms. Gartner-attributed forecasts put conversational AI at **~$80B in reduced contact-center labor cost in 2026** (secondary-cited Gartner projection). Deflecting or shortening even a small share of billions of annual interactions is a multi-$B pool industry-wide.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the ticket/case/interaction. **Decision:** category, priority, queue/agent assignment. **Label:** final resolved category and team (historical tickets are naturally labeled by how they were resolved). **Horizon:** immediate (seconds–minutes at arrival), decided continuously.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Thousands–millions of tickets/month depending on scale; every single inbound item is a decision. Value concentrates in high-volume routine categories and in catching the small share of urgent/high-value cases fast (outage reports, VIP accounts, regulatory complaints).
7. **Data exhaust** — Full ticket history with text, category, priority, assignments, reassignments, timestamps, resolution codes; CSAT per ticket. Systems of record: **ITSM/CS platforms (Zendesk, ServiceNow, Salesforce Service Cloud, Freshdesk)**. Exhaust quality is unusually good — labels come free from operations — though category taxonomies drift and legacy labels are noisy.
8. **Actionability** — Predicted category/priority writes directly into the ticket system's routing rules — assignment, SLA clock, escalation path. No human adoption barrier: the action is native platform workflow. Misroute reduction shortens time-to-resolution mechanically.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs already tracked: first-response time, time-to-resolution, reassignment/transfer rate, SLA compliance, cost per ticket, CSAT. Impact provable by A/B on routing (auto vs manual queues) or before/after on transfer rate — among the cleanest measurement setups in the catalog.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Months of labeled ticket history (nearly always exists); a stable-enough taxonomy; platform integration (native AI features increasingly built-in). One of the **most feasible** use cases anywhere: natural labels, immediate action path, hard KPIs. LLMs have removed the last barrier (no per-category training data needed).
11. **Industry variants** — **Banking/insurance:** complaint identification is regulatory (mis-triage has compliance cost). **Healthcare:** urgency triage borders on clinical risk. **Public sector:** case prioritization under statutory deadlines. **Internal IT:** ITIL category trees, integration with incident management (family H overlap).
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Intent detection (the classification core, shared with bots), resolution automation (next step after routing), CSAT/escalation prediction (risk overlay on the same entity), incident triage in AIOps (same skeleton, different domain). Boundary: triage decides *where the ticket goes*; resolution automation decides *the answer itself*.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Very high and rising fast: service operations is a top-3 AI adoption function in every McKinsey State of AI wave (2023–2025), and triage/routing is the entry-level deployment. Native platform features (Zendesk intelligent triage, ServiceNow predictive intelligence) make this the most commoditized use case in the family.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Klarna's OpenAI-based assistant (which subsumes triage) handled **2.3M conversations in month one, ≈700 FTE of work**, resolution time 11 min → <2 min, repeat inquiries −25%, projected **+$40M profit in 2024** (Klarna/OpenAI, 2024 — company-reported; Klarna later publicly moderated its AI-first staffing stance in 2025, rehiring for quality). (b) Contact-center automation benchmarks: containment of **30–55%** of inbound volume in well-deployed programs (industry benchmarks, 2026 — vendor-adjacent). (c) Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (NBER, 2023) — the adjacent agent-assist RCT — showed **+14% productivity** from AI assistance, evidence that the interaction layer responds to AI at scale (measured, quasi-experimental).
15. **Failure modes** — Taxonomy drift (model trained on last year's categories); automating triage while leaving downstream queues understaffed (no end-to-end gain); over-trust in priority prediction burying genuinely urgent edge cases; measurement theater (deflection counted as success while customers churn silently — CSAT must be co-tracked); noisy historical labels teaching yesterday's misroutes.
16. **Typical methodology** — Text classification (fine-tuned transformer or LLM prompt-based) + rules for compliance categories → platform routing API; historically GBM on bag-of-words.
17. **Key references** — McKinsey State of AI (2024–2025) service-operations adoption; Klarna/OpenAI case (2024); Zendesk/ServiceNow product benchmark reports; Brynjolfsson et al., "Generative AI at Work" (NBER WP 31161, 2023).

### B2. Resolution automation & response drafting — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Self-service automation; chatbot/virtual agent; conversational AI; case deflection; auto-response; ticket resolution automation; "level-0 support."
2. **Problem statement** — A large share of support contacts are repetitive questions with known answers; paying humans to retype known answers is expensive and slow, and customers wait in queues for resolutions that could be instant. The business wants routine contacts resolved end-to-end without an agent, and non-routine ones answered faster with drafted responses.
3. **Who has it** — Same population as B1 — any support operation; most acute in high-volume consumer businesses (fintech, e-commerce, telco, travel, marketplaces). Lives in **customer support/CX**; owned by VP Support/CX, increasingly with a dedicated "AI in service" lead.
4. **Economic pool** — Direct labor substitution: human-handled contacts at ~$3–8+ each vs automated interactions commonly cited at **$0.20–1.50** (industry benchmarks, 2026) — an order-of-magnitude unit-cost gap. Klarna projected **$40M annual profit improvement** from one assistant (2024, company-reported). Gartner-attributed projection: **$80B reduction in contact-center labor costs in 2026** (secondary-cited). McKinsey (2023) places customer operations among the four functions carrying ~75% of gen-AI value (projection).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the individual contact/conversation. **Decision:** resolve automatically, draft-and-send, or hand to human (and what answer to give). **Label:** resolved-without-reopen + CSAT (containment alone is a false label — see 15). **Horizon:** real time, continuous.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Highest interaction frequency in the enterprise after recsys: every inbound chat/email. Value concentrates in the top-20 contact reasons, which typically cover the majority of volume (industry rule of thumb; not source-verified).
7. **Data exhaust** — Historical transcripts + resolutions, macros/canned responses, knowledge base, order/account systems for context, resolution codes, CSAT. Systems of record: **CCaaS/helpdesk platforms, knowledge bases, order-management/CRM for grounding**. The knowledge base is the weak link — automation exposes stale/contradictory KB content instantly.
8. **Actionability** — The prediction *is* the action: the answer is delivered directly, or actions execute via API (refund, reset, rebook) within policy limits. Guardrails (escalation triggers, action allow-lists) are the design surface. Highest-actionability family in the catalog alongside recsys and pricing engines.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: containment/deflection rate, resolution rate without reopen, CSAT on automated contacts, cost per resolution, escalation rate. Provable by A/B (bot vs queue) — routinely run. The discipline is measuring **resolution quality**, not just deflection.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Curated, current knowledge base; API access to act (identity, orders, billing); policy definition for what the bot may do; escalation design. Feasibility jumped dramatically with LLMs (no intent-tree engineering), but grounding + guardrail work is real. Brand-risk tolerance is an organizational prerequisite (hallucinated answers are public).
11. **Industry variants** — **Fintech/banking:** regulated advice boundaries, strong authentication before action. **Telco:** device/network troubleshooting trees. **E-commerce/travel:** order actions (refund/rebook) dominate. **B2B software:** technical depth, lower volume — drafting for agents beats full automation. **Public sector:** multilingual, accessibility obligations.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Ticket triage (upstream), agent assist (same generation capability, human-in-loop), KB retrieval (the grounding layer), proactive care (prevent the contact entirely). Boundary vs agent assist: who sends the answer — the machine (here) or the human (B3).
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — The single most visible enterprise gen-AI deployment category, 2023–2026. Service operations top-3 in McKinsey adoption waves; Gartner (2024) reported gen AI as the most-deployed AI solution type overall, with virtual assistants a leading form. Yet full end-to-end resolution at scale remains concentrated in digital-native consumer firms; most enterprises run drafting + partial containment.
14. **Documented results** — (a) **Klarna (2024):** 2.3M conversations/month, ≈700 FTE equivalent, 11→<2 min resolution, −25% repeat inquiries, +$40M projected profit (company-reported; later strategy moderation in 2025 noted — quality trade-offs are real). (b) Vodafone's TOBi: **~70% of customer queries handled autonomously**, call-center load −35%, CSAT +19 pts (company-reported, secondary source). (c) Industry containment benchmarks 30–55% for voice AI (2026 compilations, vendor-adjacent). All are company/vendor-reported; independent audits are scarce — treat magnitudes as directional.
15. **Failure modes** — Deflection theater (containment up, CSAT and churn worse); hallucinated answers creating liability (Air Canada chatbot ruling, 2024 — airline held liable for its bot's wrong policy answer); stale KB garbage-in; automating the easy 60% while the hard 40% still sets staffing floor; no escalation path frustration loops; cost being pushed to customers as effort; measuring the bot but not the end-to-end journey.
16. **Typical methodology** — RAG over the knowledge base + LLM generation with tool/action calling and guardrails; escalation classifier; legacy: intent trees + dialog flows.
17. **Key references** — OpenAI/Klarna case study (2024); Brynjolfsson et al. NBER 31161 (2023); Gartner gen-AI deployment survey (2024); McKinsey economic-potential report (2023) customer-operations chapter; Air Canada v. Moffatt tribunal decision coverage (2024).

### B3. Agent assist (copilot for support agents) — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Agent assist; agent copilot; real-time guidance; suggested responses; auto-summarization/wrap-up; whisper coaching.
2. **Problem statement** — Human agents spend large fractions of handle time searching knowledge, writing similar responses repeatedly, and doing after-call wrap-up; new agents take months to reach proficiency. The business wants every agent operating at the speed and quality of its best agents.
3. **Who has it** — Any organization with >50 support/service agents; most acute in high-turnover contact centers (BPO, telco, banking, insurance, healthcare payers). Lives in **service operations / contact center management**; owned by VP Contact Center or COO of service.
4. **Economic pool** — Labor productivity on one of the largest hourly workforces in the economy. The key measured anchor: **+14% agent productivity on average, +34% for novice agents** from a gen-AI assistant in a 5,000-agent contact center (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, NBER WP 31161, 2023 — quasi-experimental, the best-measured gen-AI result in any function). Also reduced attrition and improved customer sentiment in the same study. Auto-wrap-up alone typically saves ~30–60 seconds/call (vendor-reported; not source-verified).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the live interaction (call/chat) × moment. **Decision:** what to suggest (answer, KB article, next step, compliance prompt); what summary to write. **Label:** agent acceptance of suggestions + downstream resolution/CSAT/AHT. **Horizon:** real time within the interaction.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Continuous within every interaction — effectively per-utterance. Value concentrates in long-handle-time interaction types and in the novice cohort (steepest gains, per NBER study).
7. **Data exhaust** — Live transcripts (ASR), historical resolved interactions, KB, CRM context, QA scorecards. Systems of record: **CCaaS (Genesys, NICE, Five9, Amazon Connect), helpdesk, KB**. Transcript exhaust is now cheap (ASR commoditized) — historically the blocker.
8. **Actionability** — Suggestions render in the agent desktop; agent accepts/edits — human-in-the-loop by construction, so risk is low and adoption is the whole game. Auto-summary writes to CRM directly (pure time recovery, no judgment needed).
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: AHT, first-contact resolution, CSAT, QA scores, time-to-proficiency, agent attrition. Provable by staggered rollout across agents/teams — exactly what the NBER study did. Measurement is unusually credible here because agent-level randomization is natural.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Real-time transcription; desktop integration; curated KB; QA process to validate suggestion quality. High feasibility; native features in every major CCaaS platform. Change management with agents (surveillance concerns) is the soft prerequisite.
11. **Industry variants** — **Regulated (banking/insurance/pharma):** compliance prompts and disclosure checklists are the killer feature. **Healthcare payers:** benefits-language complexity. **BPO:** productivity per seat is the contract economics. **Tech support:** deep-troubleshooting retrieval.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Resolution automation (same generation core, machine-sends vs human-sends), call-center analytics (offline mining of the same transcripts), KB retrieval (the retrieval layer), QA automation. Boundary: assist improves the human; automation replaces the interaction.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Rapidly mainstreaming 2023–2026; the safest first gen-AI deployment for service organizations (human-in-loop). Featured natively across CCaaS vendors; McKinsey service-operations adoption reflects it. Deployed-at-scale examples concentrated in large contact centers.
14. **Documented results** — (a) **NBER 2023 (Brynjolfsson et al.): +14% issues resolved per hour on average, +34% for least-experienced agents; improved customer sentiment; reduced attrition** — measured, 5,000+ agents. (b) Klarna's numbers (B2) partially reflect assist-mode economics (2024, company-reported). (c) Elastic-reported alert/noise analogs aside, vendor case studies routinely claim 10–25% AHT reduction (vendor-reported; not independently verified).
15. **Failure modes** — Suggestion quality below agent skill → ignored UI furniture; wrong suggestions confidently accepted by novices (automation bias); agent surveillance framing poisons adoption; measuring AHT while quality erodes; KB staleness propagated at scale; per-seat licensing cost exceeding measured gain.
16. **Typical methodology** — Real-time ASR → retrieval-augmented LLM suggestion + summarization; acceptance telemetry closes the loop.
17. **Key references** — Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, "Generative AI at Work" (NBER 31161, 2023); CCaaS vendor documentation (Genesys/NICE); McKinsey State of AI service-ops data (2024–2025).

## C. Marketing

7 use cases. Full dossiers: **marketing-mix modeling & attribution**, **content generation & optimization**. Compact table: audience segmentation, campaign response modeling, SEO/keyword intelligence, creative testing, email send-time/frequency optimization.

### C1. Attribution & marketing-mix modeling (MMM) — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Marketing-mix modeling (MMM); media-mix modeling; multi-touch attribution (MTA); marketing effectiveness measurement; budget allocation optimization.
2. **Problem statement** — Marketing budgets are allocated across channels without knowing which spend actually causes revenue; a substantial share is wasted, and privacy changes (cookie deprecation, ATT) broke the tracking-based answers. The business wants to know the incremental contribution of each channel/campaign and reallocate budget accordingly.
3. **Who has it** — Every significant advertiser: CPG, retail, DTC, financial services, telecom, travel, entertainment. Lives in **marketing analytics / growth**; owned by CMO or head of marketing analytics.
4. **Economic pool** — Analyses associated with Rakuten Advertising and Proxima have put **wasted marketing spend around 40%** (and up to 40–60% in some Proxima framings) — survey/attribution-based estimates, not experiments (secondary-cited; treat as directional). Global ad spend is on the order of $1T, so even single-digit reallocation efficiency is a large pool. MMM-driven reallocation is commonly credited with **10–30% marketing-ROI improvement** (synthesized industry ranges; not a single audited study — estimate).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** channel × campaign × period (budget cell). **Decision:** how much to spend where next period. **Label:** incremental revenue/conversions attributable to the cell (fundamentally a causal quantity — the label must be constructed, not logged). **Horizon:** weekly–quarterly reallocation; annual planning.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Dozens–hundreds of budget cells decided monthly/quarterly; a small number of high-stakes decisions (unlike the high-frequency families) but each moves millions.
7. **Data exhaust** — Spend by channel/campaign (ad platforms), impressions/clicks, sales/conversions (commerce + CRM), pricing/promo calendars, seasonality, competitor/macroeconomic context. Systems of record: **ad platforms (Google/Meta), web analytics, CDP, data warehouse, finance actuals**. Spend and outcome data exist; the joining and the *causal identification* are the hard parts.
8. **Actionability** — Output is a budget-reallocation plan executed through media buying — concrete, but mediated by planning cycles, agency contracts, and committed buys; latency between insight and action is weeks. Geo-experiments can operationalize recommendations credibly.
9. **Measurability** — The use case *is* measurement — which is circular: MMM's own validity must be established via holdout geo-experiments/incrementality tests. KPIs: ROAS/ROI by channel, CAC, incremental conversion lift. Best practice pairs MMM with periodic randomized geo-lift tests.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — 2–3 years of weekly spend/outcome history with real variation in spend; promo/price controls; statistical maturity to avoid confounded regressions. Medium feasibility: data usually exists, but causal validity is genuinely hard and expertise-dependent. Open-source tooling (Meta Robyn, Google Meridian) lowered the entry bar (2023–2024).
11. **Industry variants** — **CPG:** the original MMM home (retailer-mediated sales, syndicated data). **DTC/e-commerce:** MTA→MMM migration post-privacy-changes. **B2B:** long cycles make attribution notoriously weak — pipeline-based proxies. **Regulated (pharma/finserv):** channel constraints shape the mix.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Campaign response modeling (individual-level propensity vs aggregate causal allocation), promotion effectiveness (A-family, trade-side), creative testing (within-channel optimization), CLV (value target of allocation). Boundary: MMM allocates *between* channels; response models target *within* audiences.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — MMM is decades old in CPG and undergoing a renaissance since cookie deprecation (2023–2025); MTA is receding. Marketing is a top gen-AI/AI adoption function overall (McKinsey 2025; Deloitte gen-AI function data 2024: marketing/sales/service ~41%), but rigorous MMM specifically remains concentrated in large advertisers.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Industry syntheses report 10–30% ROI improvement from MMM-driven reallocation (aggregated case ranges — estimate, not audited). (b) ~40% wasted-spend estimates (Rakuten/Proxima-associated, secondary — directional). (c) Meta/Google publishing open MMM frameworks (Robyn 2021+, Meridian 2024) documents platform-level validation practice rather than a single headline number. Evidence quality note: this use case has surprisingly thin *public* quantified outcomes relative to its prevalence — most results are proprietary.
15. **Failure modes** — Confounding (spend rises with demand seasonally → inflated ROI estimates); attribution theater (last-click numbers steering budget); model overfit to 100 weekly observations with 30 channels; incentive conflict (agencies grading their own homework); recommendations ignored because committed media buys can't move; privacy changes silently breaking input feeds.
16. **Typical methodology** — Bayesian hierarchical regression with adstock/saturation transforms (MMM) + geo-experiments for calibration; LLMs only at the reporting layer.
17. **Key references** — Meta Robyn and Google Meridian documentation (2021–2024); Rakuten/Proxima wasted-spend analyses (secondary); CPG MMM literature; post-cookie measurement practitioner writing (2023–2025).

### C2. Content generation & optimization — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Marketing content generation; copy generation; creative production at scale; content localization/versioning; SEO content automation; product-description generation.
2. **Problem statement** — Marketing needs far more content variants than teams can produce — per channel, segment, language, and format — and production cost/turnaround throttles campaign velocity. The business wants on-brand content produced and iterated at a fraction of the cost and time.
3. **Who has it** — Every marketing organization; most acute in e-commerce (product descriptions at catalog scale), CPG (retail-media variants), media, travel, and global firms (localization). Lives in **brand/content/growth marketing**; owned by CMO or head of content.
4. **Economic pool** — Production labor + agency fees + speed-to-market. McKinsey (2023) sizes gen AI in marketing & sales at **$0.76–1.2T annually** across use cases, with content generation the largest driver (projection). Content production is typically 20–40% of marketing budgets (industry estimate; not source-verified). Deloitte function data shows marketing among the top gen-AI adopters (~41% of orgs, 2024).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the content asset/variant (× audience × channel). **Decision:** what to produce/publish. **Label:** engagement/conversion performance of the asset; brand-compliance pass. **Horizon:** hours–days production cycles; continuous testing.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Thousands of assets/variants per month at large advertisers; catalog-scale (millions of product descriptions) in e-commerce. Value concentrates where variant count × traffic is highest (paid social creative, PDP copy).
7. **Data exhaust** — Historical assets + performance metrics, brand guidelines, product data (PIM), SEO data, past campaign results. Systems of record: **DAM/CMS, PIM, ad platforms, web analytics**. Exhaust exists but is scattered; brand guidelines are often not machine-usable — a real prerequisite gap.
8. **Actionability** — Generated content flows into CMS/ad platforms directly; with human review it ships in hours. Action path is short; the gate is brand/legal review — the workflow redesign (review-at-scale) is the actual project.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: content production cost/asset, turnaround time, engagement/conversion per asset, test velocity. Provable by A/B at the asset level (native to ad platforms) and by production-cost accounting. Quality/brand-equity effects are harder to measure and lag.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Machine-readable brand voice/claims constraints; review workflow; product-data quality (for catalog generation); legal claims database in regulated industries. High feasibility — the top gen-AI quick win by adoption — but *measured* P&L impact is much rarer than deployment (MIT NANDA 2025 pattern).
11. **Industry variants** — **E-commerce:** product descriptions/attributes at catalog scale — the clearest ROI. **Regulated (pharma/finserv):** claims review dominates; MLR (medical-legal-regulatory) throughput becomes the bottleneck being optimized. **Global firms:** localization/transcreation. **B2B:** long-form thought leadership, quality bar issues.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Creative testing (evaluation of what generation produces), SEO/keyword intelligence (what to write about), personalization (which variant to serve to whom — A3). Boundary: generation makes assets; personalization/serving decides exposure; testing closes the loop.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — The most widely adopted gen-AI use case in the enterprise: marketing & sales is consistently #1–2 in McKinsey State of AI gen-AI usage (2024–2025). Deployment is near-universal at the tool level (copilots); systematic, measured content ops are far rarer.
14. **Documented results** — (a) McKinsey $0.76–1.2T marketing/sales gen-AI potential (2023 — projection). (b) Widespread company-reported production-cost/turnaround reductions (commonly 30–70% for first-draft workflows — vendor/company-reported ranges; not independently audited). (c) The honest note: this is the family's prevalence leader but its *measured-outcome* evidence base is thin relative to adoption — most public numbers are self-reported efficiency claims, not revenue-verified results.
15. **Failure modes** — Brand-voice drift and factual/claims errors (regulated-industry liability); review bottleneck simply relocating the constraint; content flooding with declining marginal returns (SEO spam penalties — Google policy responses 2024); measuring volume not performance; skill atrophy/agency conflicts; hallucinated product attributes at catalog scale.
16. **Typical methodology** — LLM generation constrained by brand/claims context (RAG over guidelines + PIM) → human review → A/B testing loop.

## D. Finance & accounting

10 use cases. Full dossiers: **invoice matching & touchless AP**, **collections prioritization & DSO reduction**, **credit risk scoring**. Compact table: cash-flow/P&L forecasting, AP/AR exception handling, expense-fraud detection, journal-entry anomaly detection, financial-close automation, working-capital optimization, FP&A scenario modeling.

### D1. Invoice matching & touchless AP processing — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Touchless invoice processing; AP automation; invoice data capture & 2/3-way matching; straight-through processing (finance); e-invoicing exception reduction.
2. **Problem statement** — Supplier invoices arrive in inconsistent formats and must be captured, coded, matched to POs/receipts, and approved; done manually this is slow, error-prone, and expensive, delaying payments, losing early-payment discounts, and inviting fraud and duplicate payment.
3. **Who has it** — Every organization with meaningful supplier volume; most acute in manufacturing, retail, healthcare systems, construction, logistics, and shared-services organizations. Lives in **AP within controllership/shared services**; owned by controller or head of P2P/shared services.
4. **Economic pool** — Manual invoice processing costs **~$10.18 per invoice on average vs ~$2.36 best-in-class** (Ardent Partners/APQC-style benchmarks, 2023–2024) — a 4–5× unit-cost gap; best-in-class AP teams are **78% cheaper per invoice and 82% faster** (Ardent Partners, 2024). Cycle time: best-in-class ~2.8–3.1 days vs ~17.4 days laggards (benchmarks, 2024–2026). Add lost early-payment discounts, duplicate payments, and audit cost. For an enterprise processing 1M invoices/year, the labor delta alone is ~$8M/yr (arithmetic on the benchmark figures).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the invoice (line-item level). **Decision:** capture fields, GL-code, match to PO/receipt, approve/route exception. **Label:** correct extraction/match/coding — verified by human correction history (excellent natural labels). **Horizon:** hours; decided continuously as invoices arrive.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Thousands–millions of invoices/year; each one is several decisions (fields × match × code). Value concentrates in the exception tail: top performers hit ~49–80% touchless rates (Ardent/benchmark ranges, 2024–2026), so the cost lives in the remaining exceptions.
7. **Data exhaust** — Invoice images/PDFs/EDI, PO and goods-receipt data, supplier master, GL history of coding decisions, approval trails. Systems of record: **ERP (SAP/Oracle/NetSuite), P2P suites (Coupa/Ariba), OCR/capture layers**. Exhaust is complete and labeled by operations — one of the best data situations in the catalog; supplier-master hygiene is the classic weak point.
8. **Actionability** — Extracted/matched/coded results post directly into ERP workflow — pure straight-through processing; exceptions route to humans with reasons. No adoption problem: the action is the process itself.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: cost per invoice, touchless rate, cycle time, exception rate, duplicate-payment rate, discount capture. Before/after on these hard operating metrics is standard and credible; benchmarks exist for comparison (Ardent Partners annual studies).
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — ERP integration; PO discipline (3-way match requires POs to exist — "no-PO" cultures cap the ceiling); supplier-master cleanup. Very high feasibility — mature vendor market, decades of practice, LLM/vision models now handle long-tail formats that defeated template OCR.
11. **Industry variants** — **Manufacturing/retail:** high PO coverage → matching-centric. **Healthcare:** complex GPO pricing, high exception rates. **Construction:** progress billing, retention — messy. **Public sector:** compliance/audit emphasis. **Cross-border:** e-invoicing mandates (EU, LatAm) shifting capture upstream.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — AP/AR exception handling (the residual), expense-fraud/duplicate detection (control overlay on the same flow), procurement spend analytics (uses the coded output), payments-fraud screening. Boundary: touchless processing is throughput; fraud detection is adversarial control on the same pipe.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Among the most-deployed enterprise automation categories (pre-dating the gen-AI wave via OCR/RPA); Deloitte gen-AI adoption in finance ~25% of orgs (2024) — mid-pack, but AP automation specifically is mainstream in large enterprises and actively penetrating mid-market.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Benchmark deltas above (Ardent Partners 2023–2024 — measured across cohorts). (b) Touchless-rate benchmarks: ~49% best-in-class average, 70–90% for clean PO-backed flows (benchmark compilations, 2024–2026 — measured). (c) JPMorgan COIN (adjacent document-automation anchor): ~**360,000 hours/year** of loan-agreement review automated (JPMorgan annual-report coverage, 2017 — company-reported).
15. **Failure modes** — Automating capture while approval workflows stay manual (cycle time unchanged); exception queues ballooning because root causes (PO discipline, supplier data) weren't fixed; template-OCR brittleness on long-tail formats; duplicate-payment risk from over-trusted auto-matching; savings claimed on headcount that never actually redeploys.
16. **Typical methodology** — Document AI (vision-LLM extraction) + deterministic matching rules + GBM/LLM coding suggestions + exception classifier; RPA glue where APIs are missing.
17. **Key references** — Ardent Partners "State of ePayables" (2023–2024); APQC P2P benchmarks; e-invoicing mandate literature (EU ViDA); vendor-neutral AP-automation benchmark compilations (2024–2026).

### D2. Collections prioritization & DSO reduction — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Collections optimization; AR prioritization; payment-date prediction; dunning optimization; order-to-cash intelligence; delinquency management (B2B AR).
2. **Problem statement** — Receivables age because collectors work accounts by size or age rather than by likelihood and influenceability of payment; cash is trapped in working capital, bad debt accrues, and collector capacity is wasted on accounts that would pay anyway. The business wants each collector-hour aimed at the invoice/customer where it moves cash most.
3. **Who has it** — Any B2B business extending trade credit: manufacturing, distribution, business services, telecom/utilities (consumer collections variant), healthcare providers (patient AR), banking (post-charge-off collections is a separate lending variant). Lives in **order-to-cash within finance/shared services**; owned by treasurer, controller, or head of O2C.
4. **Economic pool** — Working capital: receivables tie up trillions globally on corporate balance sheets (PwC/Hackett working-capital studies quantify this annually — figure not source-verified here; stated qualitatively). Each day of DSO on $1B revenue ≈ **$2.7M cash** (arithmetic). Add bad-debt expense and collector labor. Interest-rate environments post-2022 sharply raised the value of each DSO day.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the open invoice / customer account. **Decision:** whom to contact, when, through which channel, with what treatment (reminder, call, hold, plan). **Label:** paid within X days / broke promise / went delinquent — natural labels from AR history. **Horizon:** days–weeks; work queues decided daily.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Hundreds–hundreds of thousands of open invoices at any time; daily queue construction per collector. Value concentrates in the small share of accounts that are both high-balance and *influenceable* (would pay late without action, will pay with it) — an uplift-shaped problem.
7. **Data exhaust** — AR ledger (invoices, terms, payments, disputes), dunning/contact history, promises-to-pay, credit ratings, order history, dispute codes. Systems of record: **ERP AR module, collections platforms (HighRadius, Billtrust), credit bureaus (D&B)**. Exhaust is complete and labeled; dispute-reason data quality varies.
8. **Actionability** — Ranked work queues + recommended treatments flow into collector CRM/dialer; automated dunning sequences execute directly; credit holds trigger in ERP. Strong action path through existing O2C workflow.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: DSO, % AR current, bad-debt write-offs, collector productivity (cash collected per FTE), promise-kept rate. Provable by A/B on queue ordering or matched-portfolio comparison; DSO is a hard balance-sheet metric CFOs already watch.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — 12–24 months of AR + contact history; dispute-code discipline; collections workflow system to consume the queue. High feasibility — tabular, well-labeled, hard-KPI; a mature vendor category (HighRadius et al.) validates the pattern. Under-attacked relative to its feasibility in mid-market.
11. **Industry variants** — **B2B trade credit:** invoice-level, dispute-driven. **Utilities/telco consumer:** regulatory constraints on disconnection, hardship rules. **Healthcare provider:** patient-responsibility AR after insurance — propensity-to-pay + charity-care screening (compliance-sensitive). **Banking/lending:** charge-off collections with strict regulation (FDCPA) — treated in family L (banking).
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Cash-flow forecasting (payment-date predictions aggregate into it), credit risk scoring (limit-setting upstream of collections), AR exception/dispute handling, working-capital optimization (the umbrella). Boundary: credit risk decides *whether to extend terms*; collections decides *how to recover what's owed*.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Mainstream in large enterprises via O2C platform vendors; a top-3 finance AI use case in vendor deployment counts (vendor-reported; not source-verified). Finance overall is a mid-pack adopter (Deloitte 2024: ~25% gen-AI adoption), but classic-ML collections scoring predates the gen-AI wave.
14. **Documented results** — Vendor case studies routinely claim 10–20% DSO-day reductions and 20–30% collector-productivity gains (HighRadius-class vendor claims — not independently verified; flagged as such). Independent published RCTs are scarce — evidence quality here is **thin relative to the mechanism's plausibility**, a point noted in the value ranking (Synthesis 2).
15. **Failure modes** — Chasing accounts that would pay anyway (propensity instead of uplift); harming customer relationships with aggressive automation; dispute root causes (billing errors!) unaddressed — collections optimizing the symptom of an invoicing-quality problem; collector incentive misalignment (cash targets vs queue compliance); payment-date models degrading when macro conditions shift.
16. **Typical methodology** — Tabular payment-date/delinquency GBM + treatment-uplift ranking → daily work queues + automated dunning; SHAP reason codes for collector context.
17. **Key references** — PwC/Hackett annual working-capital studies (framework; figures not re-verified here); Ardent/IOFM O2C benchmarks; HighRadius/Billtrust case literature (vendor); credit-management practitioner literature.

### D3. Credit risk scoring — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Credit scoring; underwriting models; PD/LGD/EAD modeling; application scoring vs behavioral scoring; trade-credit limit setting (B2B).
2. **Problem statement** — Lending and credit extension require estimating whether a counterparty will repay; too conservative loses good customers and revenue, too permissive produces defaults. The business wants each application/limit decision to reflect the applicant's true risk — including applicants whom traditional bureau-score cutoffs misjudge.
3. **Who has it** — Banks, fintechs, card issuers, BNPL, auto/mortgage lenders, telcos (postpaid), utilities (deposits), insurers (premium credit), and any B2B firm setting trade-credit limits. Lives in **credit risk / underwriting**; owned by CRO/chief credit officer; heavily regulated (ECOA/fair lending in the US, IFRS 9/Basel model governance).
4. **Economic pool** — The pool is the interest margin + loss rate on trillions in consumer/commercial credit. The best-documented ML delta: Upstart's CFPB-reviewed model approved **27% more applicants at the same loss rate with 16% lower average APR** vs a traditional model, and ~2× approvals in the 620–660 FICO band (CFPB no-action-letter update, 2019 — regulator-summarized, measured). Expanding approvals without raising losses is direct revenue; loss-rate basis points are direct margin.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the application / account / counterparty. **Decision:** approve/decline, limit, price (risk-based). **Label:** default/delinquency within the performance window (12–36 months) — slow but unambiguous labels. **Horizon:** decision in seconds–days; label matures over years (the defining tension: feedback is delayed and censored — declined applicants never generate labels: reject inference problem).
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Card issuers score millions of applications and re-score whole portfolios monthly (behavioral scoring); B2B credit desks decide hundreds of limits weekly. Value concentrates near the approval boundary — the marginal applicant band where model quality changes decisions.
7. **Data exhaust** — Bureau data, application data, transaction/cash-flow history (open banking), payment behavior on existing accounts, alternative data (education/employment — fairness-sensitive). Systems of record: **loan origination systems, core banking, bureaus (Experian/Equifax/TransUnion), open-banking aggregators**. Rich but access-regulated exhaust.
8. **Actionability** — Score → automated decisioning in origination systems, limit assignments, risk-based pricing tiers, adverse-action reason codes (legally required — explanation is not optional here). Fully wired action path; the constraint is model governance, not plumbing.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: approval rate, default/charge-off rate, loss given default, risk-adjusted margin, vintage curves. Champion/challenger deployment is standard industry practice — this is the most measurement-mature use case in the catalog (decades of scorecard discipline).
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Years of performance history; reject-inference handling; fair-lending compliance (disparate-impact testing); model risk management (SR 11-7, EU AI Act high-risk class). Technically very feasible; **regulatorily gated** — the binding constraint is validation/governance, not modeling.
11. **Industry variants** — **Consumer lending:** bureau + alternative data, fairness scrutiny highest. **SME lending:** thin-file problem, cash-flow underwriting via open banking. **B2B trade credit:** D&B-style ratings + own payment experience. **BNPL:** instant, thin-data decisions at checkout. **Telco/utility:** deposit/postpaid gating — lightweight variant.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Collections (post-approval recovery), transaction fraud (different adversary: fraudster vs honest-but-risky), AML/KYC (identity/compliance, not willingness-to-pay), limit management (ongoing variant, family L banking). Boundary: credit risk = will they *repay*; fraud = are they *who they claim*.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Universal in lending (statistical scoring predates "AI" by decades — FICO since 1950s); the live frontier is ML upgrades to scorecards and alternative-data underwriting. Every major issuer runs champion/challenger ML programs (industry practice; specific penetration figures not source-verified).
14. **Documented results** — (a) **Upstart/CFPB (2019): +27% approvals, −16% APR at equal loss rates; ~2× approvals for FICO 620–660; no fair-lending disparities found under the tested compliance plan** (regulator-summarized — the single best-documented ML underwriting result). (b) Upstart internal: +3× approval capability 2014→2017; ~560 bps APR advantage from non-traditional variables (company-reported, 2017). (c) Broad industry loss-rate improvements from ML behavioral scoring are widely claimed but rarely published (noted as evidence gap).
15. **Failure modes** — Disparate impact/fair-lending violations (career-ending); reject inference done badly (model learns only from approved population); macro regime shifts invalidating vintages (2008, 2020); alternative data that proxies protected classes; unexplainable models failing adverse-action requirements; model-governance backlog throttling iteration speed.
16. **Typical methodology** — Regulated tabular pipeline: feature engineering → scorecard/GBM with constrained/explainable structure → reason codes (SHAP-adjacent) → champion/challenger governance.
17. **Key references** — CFPB No-Action Letter update on Upstart (2019); Basel/IFRS 9 model-risk frameworks; SR 11-7 model risk guidance; fair-lending (ECOA/Reg B) literature; EU AI Act high-risk classification (2024).

## E. Fraud, risk & compliance

10 use cases. Full dossiers: **transaction fraud detection**, **AML / financial-crime detection**, **insurance claims fraud**. Compact table: account-takeover, KYC screening, underwriting risk, model risk & regulatory reporting, sanctions/adverse-media screening, policy compliance monitoring, contract-clause risk review.

### E1. Transaction fraud detection — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Payment fraud detection; card fraud screening; card-not-present (CNP) fraud; real-time transaction risk scoring; chargeback prevention; merchant fraud screening.
2. **Problem statement** — A small fraction of payment transactions are fraudulent, but that fraction costs billions and erodes customer trust; blocking too aggressively declines legitimate customers (false declines often cost more than the fraud). The business must decide, in milliseconds, which transactions to approve, challenge, or block.
3. **Who has it** — Card issuers, acquirers, payment processors/PSPs, e-commerce merchants, marketplaces, digital wallets, BNPL. Lives in **fraud/risk operations**; owned by head of fraud risk or chief risk officer; merchant-side often under payments/finance.
4. **Economic pool** — Global card fraud losses: **$33.41B in 2024** (Nilson Report, 2026 reporting on 2024), ~**6.43¢ per $100** of volume on $51.9T global card volume; US-issued cards absorb ~42% of global losses (Nilson). False declines are widely believed to cost multiples of fraud itself in lost legitimate sales (industry claim; magnitude not source-verified). Cumulative next-decade card fraud projected ~$400B (Nilson projection, 2021).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the transaction (contextualized by card/account/device/merchant). **Decision:** approve / step-up authenticate / decline. **Label:** confirmed fraud via chargebacks and reports — reliable but delayed 30–90 days. **Horizon:** real-time (<100ms), continuously; labels mature over weeks.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — The highest-frequency decision in the enterprise world: large processors score tens of thousands of transactions/second. Extreme class imbalance (fraud ≈ bps of volume); value concentrates in high-ticket CNP segments and new fraud-pattern windows (attacks are bursty).
7. **Data exhaust** — Full transaction streams (amount, merchant, geography, time), device/browser fingerprints, behavioral biometrics, account history, chargeback outcomes, consortium/network signals. Systems of record: **payment switches, fraud platforms (Falcon, Stripe Radar, Forter), issuer core systems**. Data exhaust is exceptional — complete, timestamped, outcome-labeled — the canonical adversarial ML domain.
8. **Actionability** — Score wires directly into the authorization decision — approve/challenge(3DS)/decline executes automatically in-line. Nothing in the catalog has a tighter prediction→action loop. Secondary actions: case creation for analyst review, card reissue, merchant investigation.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: fraud loss rate (bps), fraud catch rate at fixed false-positive rate, false-decline rate, chargeback rate, manual-review rate. Measured continuously against matured labels; champion/challenger is universal practice. Highly measurable.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Real-time scoring infrastructure (latency budget); chargeback label feeds; case-management for review queues. For merchants, the build/buy answer is usually buy (network-effect data favors processors — Stripe Radar trains across millions of businesses). Feasibility high; the arms race means maintenance is perpetual.
11. **Industry variants** — **Issuer vs merchant vs PSP:** different loss liability and data views. **CNP e-commerce:** the growth segment. **Real-time payments (RTP/FedNow, Pix, UPI):** irrevocable rails → authorized-push-payment scams becoming the frontier problem (social engineering, not stolen credentials). **Marketplaces:** collusion/seller fraud. **Crypto/fintech:** on/off-ramp fraud.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Account takeover (identity compromise upstream of transactions), AML (post-hoc pattern compliance vs real-time loss prevention), chargeback dispute automation, credit abuse/first-party fraud (the blurry boundary: "friendly fraud" behaves like credit risk). Boundary vs AML: fraud protects the firm's money; AML enforces the law about *whose* money.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Deployed at scale essentially everywhere in payments — arguably the **most mature production ML domain in industry** (neural scoring at FICO/HNC since the 1990s). Not a question of adoption but of generation: rules → GBMs → deep sequence models → graph/consortium features.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Nilson: fraud per $100 fell from 6.58¢ (2023) to 6.43¢ (2024) — system-level improvement amid rising volume (measured). (b) Stripe Radar publicly claims large chargeback reductions vs rules-only baselines (commonly cited ~50%+ for merchants adopting ML screening — vendor-reported; not independently audited). (c) PayPal engineering talks describe halving fraud loss rates on specific flows after rules→ML migrations (conference-reported; not independently audited). Public, audited head-to-heads are rare because performance details are adversarially sensitive — an inherent evidence limitation of the domain.
15. **Failure modes** — Adversarial drift (fraud patterns shift within days of a control change); training-serving skew in real-time features; over-blocking killing conversion (silent revenue loss dwarfing visible fraud savings); label delay masking new attack vectors; feedback loops (blocked fraud never labels); scam-type fraud (victim authorizes payment) evading transaction-level signals entirely.
16. **Typical methodology** — Real-time feature pipelines + gradient-boosted/deep sequence models with consortium features, rules overlay for policy, case-management loop; graph methods for rings.
17. **Key references** — Nilson Report card-fraud series (2024 data, 2026 publication); Stripe Radar technical documentation; FICO Falcon history; payments-fraud practitioner literature on false-decline economics; UK Finance APP-scam reporting (for the RTP frontier).

### E2. AML & financial-crime detection — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Anti-money-laundering transaction monitoring; financial-crime compliance (FinCrime); suspicious-activity detection; SAR/STR generation support; counter-terrorist-financing (CTF).
2. **Problem statement** — Banks are legally obligated to detect and report money laundering flowing through their systems. Legacy rule-based monitoring buries compliance teams in alerts that are overwhelmingly false positives while sophisticated laundering passes through — exposing the bank to multibillion-dollar fines, and society to unchecked financial crime.
3. **Who has it** — All banks, payment institutions, broker-dealers, crypto exchanges, money-service businesses, increasingly fintechs; insurance and casinos in scope in many jurisdictions. Lives in **financial-crime compliance**; owned by the chief compliance officer / head of FinCrime, under direct regulatory supervision.
4. **Economic pool** — Global financial-crime compliance costs: **~$206.1B/year** (LexisNexis Risk Solutions, True Cost of Financial Crime Compliance, 2023). AML fines: **$4.6B in 2024** (Fenergo), with TD Bank's $3.1B settlement alone 67% of it; >$69B cumulative AML enforcement since 2007 (industry trackers). False positives in traditional rule-based monitoring run **>90–95%** (RUSI/academic-cited compilations) — meaning the $200B+ is mostly spent investigating innocent activity. The pool is (a) analyst labor efficiency, (b) fine avoidance, (c) actual crime caught.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the transaction pattern / customer / network of accounts (increasingly the *network*, not the transaction). **Decision:** alert → investigate → file SAR or close. **Label:** weak and asymmetric — SAR filings are a proxy (few get law-enforcement feedback); true laundering that passed undetected is never labeled. **Horizon:** daily/weekly batch monitoring; investigations take days–weeks.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Large banks generate tens of thousands of alerts/month; every alert consumes analyst hours (e.g., 100k alerts/month at 95% FP = ~95k wasted investigations). Value concentrates in triage: ranking which alerts deserve human hours.
7. **Data exhaust** — Full transaction ledgers, KYC/customer profiles, account networks/counterparties, alert-and-disposition history (rich labels *for alert triage*, weak for true crime), watchlists. Systems of record: **core banking, AML platforms (Actimize, SAS, Oracle FCCM), case managers**. Alert-disposition exhaust is excellent for the triage sub-problem — which is why triage is where ML lands first.
8. **Actionability** — Alert-risk ranking reorders analyst queues; auto-closure of low-risk alert classes (with regulator-accepted validation); network analytics packages cases for investigators; SAR-narrative drafting (gen-AI, human-reviewed). Actions run through the case-management system; every action must be audit-defensible.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: false-positive rate, alerts per analyst, SAR conversion rate, investigation cycle time, backlog age; regulators effectively audit the measurement. True-crime recall is unmeasurable (unknown denominator) — the domain's permanent epistemic hole; efficiency metrics carry the business case.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Regulator-grade model governance and explainability; historical alert dispositions; entity resolution across accounts (hard, high-payoff); appetite for supervisory dialogue (model changes need validation evidence). Feasibility of *triage augmentation* is high and proven; replacing rules wholesale is regulatorily slow.
11. **Industry variants** — **Retail banking:** structuring/mule networks. **Correspondent banking:** cross-border nesting — the fine magnet. **Crypto:** chain analytics, distinct vendor stack (Chainalysis). **Payments/fintech:** velocity + synthetic identities. **Trade finance:** document-based laundering — document AI applies (family J overlap).
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Sanctions/adverse-media screening (list-matching, a separate sub-problem), KYC (onboarding-time identity), transaction fraud (loss prevention vs legal compliance), mule-account detection (bridges fraud and AML). Boundary: fraud = protect the bank's money; AML = report crime in *any* money.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Rule-based monitoring is universal (mandated); ML alert-triage overlays are mainstream at large banks and spreading (regulator posture warmed: US agencies' 2018 joint innovation statement; UK FCA tech-sprints). Full ML-native monitoring remains minority practice — governance friction, not modeling difficulty.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Banks introducing ML behavioral/network triage report **30–60% false-positive reduction at equal-or-better detection** and 20–40% faster case handling (aggregated industry case ranges, 2020s — bank/vendor-reported, not independently audited; flagged). (b) System-level: $206B compliance cost vs $4.6B fines (LexisNexis 2023; Fenergo 2024 — measured context figures). (c) Published, named-bank before/afters are scarce because compliance performance is supervisory information (evidence limitation inherent to the domain).
15. **Failure modes** — Optimizing SAR volume instead of crime caught (defensive filing floods FinCEN); model governance stalling deployment for years; entity-resolution failures fragmenting networks; feedback vacuum (no ground truth) letting silent degradation pass; de-risking (dropping whole customer classes) as the "safe" alternative to intelligence; auto-closure thresholds set by cost pressure rather than risk.
16. **Typical methodology** — Alert-triage GBM on alert/customer features + graph analytics for network detection + LLM-drafted SAR narratives; all wrapped in regulated model-risk governance.
17. **Key references** — LexisNexis True Cost of Financial Crime Compliance (2023); Fenergo AML fines reports (2024); RUSI false-positive analyses; US interagency statement on AML innovation (2018); FATF digital-transformation papers.

### E3. Insurance claims fraud detection — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Claims fraud detection; SIU referral scoring (Special Investigations Unit); soft-fraud/build-up detection; provider fraud detection (health); staged-accident/ring detection.
2. **Problem statement** — A meaningful share of insurance claims are inflated or fabricated; paying them raises loss ratios and honest customers' premiums, while investigating honest claimants slows service and destroys goodwill. The insurer must decide which claims merit investigation — quickly, and without insulting legitimate claimants.
3. **Who has it** — P&C insurers (auto, property), health insurers/payers, workers' comp, life/disability, government programs (see family L public sector). Lives in **claims organization + SIU**; owned by chief claims officer / head of SIU.
4. **Economic pool** — US insurance fraud is widely cited at **~$308B/year across all lines** (Coalition Against Insurance Fraud estimate, early-2020s — widely attributed, primary not re-verified here; historic P&C-only figure ~$80B). Fraud is commonly estimated at ~10% of P&C claim costs (industry estimate; not source-verified). Investigation capacity is the scarce resource: SIUs can touch only a small share of claims.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the claim (and increasingly the network: claimant × provider × body shop × clinic). **Decision:** fast-track pay, standard handling, or SIU referral. **Label:** confirmed fraud from SIU outcomes — biased (only investigated claims get labels) and slow. **Horizon:** at FNOL (first notice of loss) and throughout claim life; decisions daily.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Thousands–millions of claims/year per carrier; SIU referral capacity in the low single-digit % of claims. Value concentrates in organized rings (small count, large dollars) and systematic soft fraud (large count, small dollars each).
7. **Data exhaust** — Claims histories (structured + adjuster notes), policy data, FNOL narratives, photos/documents, provider/repair-network billing patterns, third-party data (ISO ClaimSearch industry database), telematics. Systems of record: **claims platforms (Guidewire/Duck Creek), SIU case managers, industry consortium databases**. Text-heavy exhaust — adjuster notes are the classic underused signal, now LLM-accessible.
8. **Actionability** — Referral scores route claims to SIU queues with reason codes; low-risk claims fast-track to straight-through payment (the dual benefit: fraud control *and* CX speed); network alerts trigger ring investigations; provider flags feed network management (health).
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: SIU hit rate (confirmed fraud per referral), fraud savings $, false-referral rate, cycle time on clean claims, loss ratio. Measurable via referral-quality tracking and holdout comparisons; label bias complicates true-recall claims.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — SIU outcome history; text mining of adjuster notes; consortium data access; careful referral-bias handling. Feasibility medium-high: labels exist but are biased; the fast-track side (paying clean claims faster) is the politically easy win that funds the program.
11. **Industry variants** — **Auto:** staged accidents, ring structures — graph-shaped. **Property:** inflation/build-up after real events — severity-shaped. **Health:** provider billing fraud (upcoding, phantom billing) — the largest dollar pool, provider-entity-centric. **Workers' comp:** malingering/duration fraud — longitudinal. **Life/disability:** identity and medical-record fraud — document-shaped.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Claims triage & severity prediction (family L insurance — same claim entity, different question), subrogation detection (recovery opportunity, not fraud), underwriting risk (pre-bind), payment-integrity in health (L healthcare overlap). Boundary: triage optimizes handling of honest claims; fraud detection finds dishonest ones — sharing one scoring pipeline in practice.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Analytics-assisted SIU referral is mainstream at large carriers (vendor category: Shift Technology, FRISS, SAS); penetration thinner mid-market. Insurance overall is a moderate AI adopter; claims is its most-automated function (industry surveys; specific percentages not source-verified).
14. **Documented results** — (a) Vendor-reported: Shift/FRISS-class deployments routinely claim 2–3× SIU hit-rate improvements (vendor case studies — not independently audited; flagged). (b) Straight-through-processing context: Lemonade publicly reports paying simple claims in seconds via automated claim handling with embedded fraud checks (company-reported, 2010s–2020s; STP percentages not source-verified). (c) CAIF $308B pool figure (estimate). Independent audited results are rare — evidence rests on vendor/carrier self-reports (noted in Synthesis 2).
15. **Failure modes** — Referral-label bias entrenching yesterday's detection pattern; false accusations of legitimate claimants (regulatory + brand damage — some jurisdictions penalize claim delay); adversarial adaptation by rings; adjuster distrust of scores; fraud savings double-counted against claims-cost KPIs; over-rotating to fraud while cycle time on honest claims (the bigger CX lever) suffers.
16. **Typical methodology** — Tabular GBM on claim/policy features + NLP/LLM over notes + graph analytics for rings → SIU queue with reasons; fast-track rules for low scores.
17. **Key references** — Coalition Against Insurance Fraud estimates (2020s); ISO ClaimSearch consortium documentation; Shift Technology/FRISS case literature (vendor); health-care fraud enforcement reporting (DOJ/OIG annual reports).

## F. Supply chain, operations & logistics

12 use cases. Full dossiers: **demand forecasting**, **inventory optimization & replenishment**, **route optimization**. Compact table: supply planning, procurement spend analytics, supplier risk & selection, price/contract leakage detection, ETA prediction, fleet management & telematics, warehouse slotting & labor planning, order-promise optimization, S&OP support.

### F1. Demand forecasting — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Demand forecasting; demand planning; sales & operations forecasting; demand sensing (short-horizon variant); baseline forecasting.
2. **Problem statement** — Every ordering, production, staffing, and budgeting decision embeds a guess about future demand; when the guess is wrong, the business either loses sales (stockout) or eats cost (overstock, markdown, waste). The business wants demand predicted per item/location/period accurately enough to plan against — with known uncertainty.
3. **Who has it** — Retail, CPG, manufacturing, distribution, food service, pharma, e-commerce, utilities (load — family L), telecom (capacity). Lives in **supply chain planning / demand planning**; owned by VP supply chain or IBP/S&OP leader.
4. **Economic pool** — Retail inventory distortion (out-of-stocks + overstocks) cost **$1.77T globally in 2023: $1.2T out-of-stocks + $562B overstocks** (IHL Group, 2023). McKinsey: AI-driven forecasting can cut supply-chain forecast errors **20–50%** and reduce lost sales/product unavailability **up to 65%** (McKinsey, AI-driven operations forecasting, 2024 — consultant estimate range grounded in engagements). Early AI supply-chain adopters: logistics costs −15%, inventory −35%, service +65% vs slower competitors (McKinsey, Succeeding in the AI supply-chain revolution, 2024 — cohort comparison).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** SKU × location × period (the forecast cell). **Decision:** the number handed to ordering/production/staffing (with uncertainty bands). **Label:** actual demand — abundant, but censored by stockouts (sales ≠ demand when shelves were empty). **Horizon:** days (demand sensing) to 18+ months (capacity); re-forecast weekly/daily.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — A grocery chain forecasts millions of SKU-store-day cells daily — among the largest-scale prediction problems in industry. Value concentrates in A-items (volume) and in promo/new-item cells where naive methods fail worst.
7. **Data exhaust** — POS/sales history, orders/shipments, promotions & pricing calendars, seasonality/holidays, weather, distribution status, new-item attributes; increasingly downstream retailer data for CPG. Systems of record: **ERP, planning systems (Blue Yonder, o9, Kinaxis, SAP IBP), POS/data-sharing portals**. History exists everywhere; the classic gaps are promo-flag hygiene and stockout censoring.
8. **Actionability** — The forecast feeds replenishment, production scheduling, procurement, logistics capacity, and financial planning *by system design* — planning platforms consume it automatically. Highest structural actionability of any prediction: it is the input contract to half the F-family.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: forecast error (MAPE/WMAPE/bias) by level, service level/fill rate, lost sales, inventory turns, waste/markdown. Forecast error is tracked as a standing metric with clean baselines (prior method) — impact attribution is unusually straightforward.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — 2–3 years of clean sales history with promo/price flags; stockout correction; master-data quality (item hierarchies); planner workflow integration (forecast value dies if planners override everything). High feasibility — decades of practice, mature platforms — with well-known statistical pitfalls.
11. **Industry variants** — **Grocery/fresh:** short shelf life, weather, waste — daily granularity. **Fashion:** new-item/short-lifecycle dominance — attribute-based cold-start. **CPG:** promo-driven, retailer-mediated. **Manufacturing/B2B:** lumpy, few-customer demand — intermittent methods. **Pharma:** tender/regulatory events. **E-commerce:** long tail, fast drift.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Inventory optimization (consumes the forecast + uncertainty), supply planning (translates to supply), S&OP (the consensus process around it), pricing (elasticity shared), load forecasting (utilities variant, family L). Boundary: forecasting says *what demand will be*; inventory optimization says *what to hold given that uncertainty*.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Universal as a process; statistical forecasting mainstream for decades; ML upgrades now standard at scale players (Amazon, Walmart operate deep-learning forecasting at extreme scale — company engineering publications). Among the most-deployed "classic AI" use cases in McKinsey State of AI industry data.
14. **Documented results** — (a) **Danone: −30% lost sales** from ML demand forecasting (reported 2024 — company case, secondary source). (b) McKinsey engagement ranges: forecast error −20–50%, lost sales −65% (2024 — consultant estimates). (c) McKinsey early-adopter cohort: inventory −35%, logistics cost −15%, service +65% (2024 — cohort self-reports). (d) IHL $1.77T distortion pool (2023 — survey-based estimate). Note: (a)–(c) are company/consultant-reported, not third-party audits; the pool figure (d) is a modeled estimate.
15. **Failure modes** — Judging on accuracy while ignoring bias (systematic over-forecast is invisible in MAPE); stockout-censored history teaching under-forecasts; promo cannibalization double-counting; planner override culture destroying model value (forecast-value-added tracking is the antidote); one-number forecasts hiding the uncertainty that inventory decisions actually need; new-item cold start handled by copying the wrong analog.
16. **Typical methodology** — Hierarchical time-series ML (GBM on lag/calendar/promo features; deep learning at scale) + forecast-value-added governance → planning-system integration.
17. **Key references** — IHL inventory-distortion studies (2023); McKinsey AI supply-chain publications (2024); M5 forecasting competition literature (methodological state of the art); Amazon/Walmart forecasting engineering papers; Blue Yonder/o9 platform documentation.

### F2. Inventory optimization & replenishment — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Inventory optimization; safety-stock optimization; multi-echelon inventory optimization (MEIO); auto-replenishment; stock rebalancing; spare-parts optimization (MRO variant).
2. **Problem statement** — Given uncertain demand and supply lead times, how much of each item should sit at each node — and when/how much to reorder? Too much ties up cash and creates waste/markdowns; too little loses sales and service. Most firms answer with static rules that are wrong item-by-item.
3. **Who has it** — Retail, distribution, manufacturing (raw/WIP/finished + MRO spares), e-commerce, pharma/med-device (expiry), aftermarket parts. Lives in **supply chain / inventory management / merchandising**; owned by VP supply chain or inventory director; CFO cares (working capital).
4. **Economic pool** — Same $1.77T distortion pool as F1 (IHL 2023) plus working-capital carrying cost (capital, storage, obsolescence — typically 15–30% of inventory value/year; industry rule of thumb, not source-verified). McKinsey AI-in-distribution: **inventory −20–30%, logistics cost −5–20%, procurement spend −5–15%** for adopters (McKinsey, 2024 — engagement-based ranges). Freeing 20% of inventory at a $2B-inventory retailer = $400M cash (arithmetic).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** SKU × node (store/DC/plant). **Decision:** reorder point/quantity, safety stock, allocation/rebalancing. **Label:** realized service level + carrying cost (the objective is a cost trade-off, not a single label). **Horizon:** daily–weekly replenishment; seasonal builds; continuous review.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Millions of SKU-node reorder decisions weekly at large retailers, mostly auto-executed. Value concentrates in A-items (working capital) and long-lead-time/volatile items (service risk), plus slow-moving spares (obsolescence).
7. **Data exhaust** — On-hand/on-order inventory, sales/consumption, lead-time history (POs vs receipts), supplier fill rates, costs, shelf-life/expiry. Systems of record: **ERP, WMS, planning systems**. Exhaust is complete but *inventory-accuracy* is the notorious gap (system stock ≠ physical stock — cycle-count data quality is the real prerequisite).
8. **Actionability** — Reorder recommendations execute directly as POs/transfer orders through ERP — auto-replenishment is standard practice, so the action loop is closed by construction. Human gates only on exceptions/large commitments.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: inventory turns, days of supply, service level/fill rate, stockout rate, obsolescence/waste, GMROI. Hard balance-sheet + service metrics tracked already; before/after and side-by-side category pilots are standard proof designs.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Trustworthy forecast with uncertainty (F1 upstream); inventory-record accuracy; lead-time data; ERP execution integration. High feasibility where F1 is in place; MEIO across echelons is harder but well-trodden (mature OR discipline + vendors).
11. **Industry variants** — **Retail:** presentation stock, planogram minimums. **Fresh/pharma:** expiry-aware (FEFO). **Manufacturing MRO:** intermittent demand, criticality-weighted (downtime cost >> holding cost). **Aftermarket:** slow movers, last-time-buy decisions. **E-commerce:** multi-node placement doubles as delivery-speed strategy.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Demand forecasting (input), supply planning (capacity side), order-promise (real-time availability), warehouse slotting (physical placement), working-capital optimization (finance umbrella, D-family). Boundary: F1 predicts; F2 decides stock policy under that prediction.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Reorder-point automation is universal; *optimized* (service-cost trade-off, multi-echelon) inventory is mainstream in large retail/distribution and growing in manufacturing. A top classic-AI deployment in supply-chain surveys (McKinsey supply-chain AI reports, 2024).
14. **Documented results** — (a) McKinsey adopter ranges: inventory −20–35% with service maintained/improved (2024 — engagement/cohort-based). (b) IHL: overstocks $562B globally (2023 — modeled estimate). (c) Named single-company audited results are scarce in public (most are vendor case studies; flagged as evidence gap — the aggregate consultant evidence is stronger than the named-case evidence).
15. **Failure modes** — Optimizing against phantom inventory (record inaccuracy); service-level targets set politically rather than economically; forecast uncertainty ignored (deterministic planning); bullwhip from local optimization without echelon view; planner overrides reintroducing gut-feel buffers; obsolescence pushed downstream (channel stuffing) and celebrated as turns improvement.
16. **Typical methodology** — Probabilistic forecast (F1) → stochastic inventory optimization (newsvendor/MEIO) → auto-replenishment execution; GBM lead-time prediction as input.
17. **Key references** — IHL distortion studies (2023); McKinsey AI-in-distribution/supply-chain (2024); MEIO literature (Graves, Willems); ToolsGroup/Blue Yonder/Lokad practitioner material.

### F3. Route optimization — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Route optimization; vehicle routing (VRP); last-mile optimization; dynamic dispatch; territory/route planning; delivery sequencing.
2. **Problem statement** — Fleets drive more miles than necessary because human-planned routes can't evaluate the combinatorial space of stop sequences, time windows, and capacities; every unnecessary mile is fuel, labor, vehicle wear, and emissions. The business wants each day's stops sequenced and assigned near-optimally, adapting to real-time conditions.
3. **Who has it** — Parcel/postal, LTL/FTL trucking, food/grocery delivery, field service (utility/telecom techs), waste management, distribution fleets, home services. Lives in **logistics/transportation operations**; owned by VP transportation/operations.
4. **Economic pool** — The canonical measured anchor: **UPS ORION saves $300–400M/year, ~100M miles/year, ~10M gallons of fuel/year, ~100k tonnes CO₂** at full deployment (INFORMS/UPS, 2016 — measured program results); 1 mile/driver/day at UPS ≈ **$50M/year** (UPS). Transportation is typically 5–10% of revenue in physical-goods businesses (industry rule of thumb; not source-verified); miles scale linearly to cost.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the route/day (vehicle × stop set); micro-entity: each stop assignment/sequence. **Decision:** stop assignment, sequence, timing, and (dynamic) re-dispatch. **Label:** realized miles/time/cost vs plan; on-time performance — telematics measures ground truth automatically. **Horizon:** nightly plan + intraday re-optimization.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Thousands of routes replanned daily at large fleets; each route is a combinatorial decision over dozens–hundreds of stops. Value concentrates in dense-stop urban operations and in reducing failed/repeat deliveries.
7. **Data exhaust** — Historical routes + GPS traces (telematics), stop/service times, order/delivery data, traffic/road networks, driver schedules, failure/exception events. Systems of record: **TMS, telematics platforms, dispatch systems**. Exhaust is superb — GPS makes actuals automatic; service-time estimation is the ML-shaped sub-problem inside an OR-shaped problem.
8. **Actionability** — Routes push directly to driver devices/dispatch — the plan *is* the action. Compliance is the classic adoption battle (drivers deviating from counterintuitive sequences — ORION's famous change-management lesson).
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: miles per stop/route, cost per delivery, stops per hour, on-time %, fuel; telematics gives automatic, objective before/after. Among the most credibly measured use cases anywhere (ORION's numbers were program-audited).
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Digitized stops/orders, telematics, driver-device integration, accurate address/geocode data. High feasibility: mature commercial solvers (Google OR-Tools, vendor TMS features) — a buy-not-build category below parcel scale.
11. **Industry variants** — **Parcel:** density + time windows at extreme scale. **Field service:** skills/parts constraints joined to scheduling (see L telecom dispatch). **Food delivery:** real-time stochastic matching (Uber/DoorDash-class). **Waste:** fixed-route rationalization. **Middle mile:** load consolidation + linehaul planning.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — ETA prediction (the customer-facing twin), fleet telematics (input + maintenance overlay), warehouse labor planning (upstream), field-service dispatch (L telecom). Boundary: routing decides the plan; ETA predicts its realization for customers.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Deployed at scale across parcel/delivery leaders for a decade+; TMS-embedded optimization is mainstream; sophisticated dynamic re-optimization concentrated at scale players. One of the least-hyped, best-proven categories in the catalog.
14. **Documented results** — (a) **UPS ORION: $300–400M/yr, 100M miles/yr, 10M gallons/yr** (INFORMS Franz Edelman finalist documentation, 2016 — audited program results; $320M cumulative by 2015). (b) Widely replicated at smaller scale via TMS vendors (case density high; individual numbers vendor-reported). This family has the strongest single audited case study in the compendium.
15. **Failure modes** — Solver-optimal but driver-implausible routes (compliance collapse without change management — ORION's multi-year lesson); garbage geocodes/service times corrupting plans; optimizing miles while breaking customer time windows; static optimization in dynamic operations; savings measured against inflated baselines.
16. **Typical methodology** — OR (VRP metaheuristics/solvers) + ML-predicted inputs (service times, traffic, failure risk); real-time re-optimization at scale players. (A reminder that "AI use case" sometimes resolves to OR with ML inputs.)
17. **Key references** — INFORMS ORION case documentation (2016); UPS public statements; Google OR-Tools/routing literature; last-mile logistics research.

## G. Manufacturing & industrial

8 use cases. Full dossiers: **predictive maintenance**, **visual quality inspection**, **yield/process optimization**. Compact table: asset-failure prediction (fleet-level), scrap & rework reduction, energy optimization, production scheduling, digital-twin simulation.

### G1. Predictive maintenance — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Predictive maintenance (PdM); condition-based maintenance (CBM); asset-health monitoring; failure prediction; prognostics & health management (PHM); remaining-useful-life (RUL) estimation.
2. **Problem statement** — Equipment fails unexpectedly, stopping production at enormous cost — while scheduled preventive maintenance replaces healthy parts and still misses failures. The business wants to know which asset will fail, when, and from what cause, so maintenance happens exactly when needed and unplanned downtime approaches zero.
3. **Who has it** — Manufacturing (all), oil & gas, mining, utilities/power gen, rail/aviation/fleet operators, data centers, buildings (HVAC/elevators), telecom infrastructure. Lives in **maintenance/reliability engineering within operations**; owned by plant manager, VP manufacturing, or head of asset management.
4. **Economic pool** — Unplanned downtime costs large plants an average **~$532k/hour** (Siemens/Senseye "True Cost of Downtime," 2023–2024), **$2.3M/hour in automotive** (Siemens, 2024); Fortune Global 500 industrials lose **~$864B/year (~3.3M production hours)** to unplanned downtime (Senseye/Siemens, 2023–2024 — modeled survey estimate). Benefit ranges: Deloitte cites **5–10% maintenance-cost reduction, 70% fewer breakdowns, 10–20% uptime improvement** (Deloitte PdM position papers, 2024); McKinsey-attributed summaries cite maintenance cost −18–25% and unplanned downtime −30–50% (secondary-cited ranges; primary not re-verified).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the asset/component (pump, motor, spindle, transformer). **Decision:** intervene now / schedule at next window / keep running. **Label:** failure events + failure mode from CMMS work orders — **scarce by nature** (good plants have few failures) and noisily coded. **Horizon:** days–weeks of warning (useful lead time), monitored continuously.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Thousands of monitored assets per site; daily health assessment, weekly maintenance-planning cycles. Value concentrates in the small set of **critical-path assets** whose failure stops the whole line — criticality analysis matters more than model coverage.
7. **Data exhaust** — Sensor telemetry (vibration, temperature, current, pressure — historian data), CMMS work orders/failure codes, operating context (load, product, speed), inspection notes. Systems of record: **historians (PI/OSIsoft), CMMS/EAM (Maximo, SAP PM), condition-monitoring systems**. Telemetry exhaust is strong on instrumented assets; the failure-label side is the chronic weakness (few failures + inconsistent work-order coding).
8. **Actionability** — Alert → work order in CMMS with suspected failure mode + parts list; scheduling into planned windows; spare-parts pre-positioning; operating-parameter derating to survive to the window. Mature action path through existing maintenance planning.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: unplanned downtime hours, OEE availability, maintenance cost/asset, MTBF/MTTR, catch rate (failures with prior warning), false-alert rate. Before/after on downtime and cost is standard; per-asset baselines exist in CMMS. Credible but confounded by other reliability work — attribution needs care.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Instrumented critical assets (retrofit sensor cost is real); 12+ months of telemetry + failure/work-order history; reliability-engineering ownership; CMMS integration. Feasibility **medium**: data acquisition and label scarcity make this harder than tabular families — the classic gap between pilot success (one asset class) and scaled rollout (heterogeneous fleet).
11. **Industry variants** — **Discrete manufacturing:** rotating equipment, tooling wear. **Process (chem/refining):** continuous processes, safety-critical, alarm-flood context. **Power/utilities:** transformers/turbines — catastrophic-failure economics (family L energy). **Rail/aviation:** regulated maintenance regimes constrain interventions. **Fleet:** high asset counts, standardized failure modes (best label situation).
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Asset-failure prediction fleet-level (statistical sibling below), quality inspection (process health vs product health), energy optimization (same sensors), AIOps (the IT twin of the same pattern), fleet telematics (F-family). Boundary: PdM predicts asset failure; process/yield optimization tunes operation between failures.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Among the most-piloted industrial AI use cases for a decade; scaled deployments concentrated in sophisticated operators. Survey evidence shows persistent pilot-to-scale gap in industrial AI broadly (McKinsey industrial AI surveys; MIT/BCG industrial AI reports — pattern-level citation). Vendor market consolidated (Senseye→Siemens, Augury, Uptake-era survivors).
14. **Documented results** — (a) Deloitte extruder pilot: **−80% unplanned downtime, ~$300k savings/asset** (Deloitte case material, 2024 — consultant-reported). (b) German turbine-context deployment: 31% less unplanned downtime, €18.4M annual savings, 97% 7-day failure prediction accuracy (case compilation, 2020s — secondary source, not independently audited). (c) Deloitte aggregate: breakdowns −70%, maintenance cost −5–10% (2024 — consultant aggregate). (d) Siemens/Senseye downtime-cost context figures (2023–2024 — modeled survey).
15. **Failure modes** — Too few failure labels to learn from (the defining constraint — anomaly detection without failure attribution produces alert noise); alert fatigue breeding distrust; predicting failures with no lead time to act; sensoring cost exceeding downtime value on non-critical assets; pilot-on-one-asset-class success that doesn't transfer across heterogeneous fleets; maintenance culture (run-to-fail incentives, planner skepticism) unaddressed.
16. **Typical methodology** — Sensor anomaly detection + survival/RUL models per failure mode, fused with work-order NLP; alarm-management discipline; increasingly foundation-model approaches to vibration/current signatures.
17. **Key references** — Siemens/Senseye True Cost of Downtime (2023–2024); Deloitte predictive-maintenance position papers (2024); NASA PHM/C-MAPSS research lineage; ISO 55000 asset management; McKinsey industrial-AI surveys.

### G2. Quality inspection (visual & sensor) — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Automated visual inspection; AI quality control; defect detection; machine-vision QC; surface-inspection; AOI (automated optical inspection) enhancement.
2. **Problem statement** — Human inspectors catch only a fraction of product defects, inconsistently and expensively; escaped defects become warranty claims, recalls, and brand damage, while over-rejection scraps good product. The business wants every unit inspected, consistently, at line speed.
3. **Who has it** — Automotive (paint, welds, assemblies), electronics/semiconductor, pharma (fill/finish, packaging), food & beverage, metals/paper (surface), textiles, medical devices. Lives in **quality within manufacturing operations**; owned by plant quality director / VP quality.
4. **Economic pool** — Human inspection detects only **~60–80% of defects** under production conditions vs **90–99.5% for mature AI vision** (industry compilations, 2024–2025); cost of quality (prevention+appraisal+failure) is commonly estimated at 5–15% of revenue in manufacturing (industry rule of thumb; not source-verified). Deployments commonly report **20–40% quality-cost reduction in year one** (industry case compilations — vendor-adjacent). Warranty and recall exposure (auto recalls routinely cost $100M+) sits behind escape rates.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the unit/surface/weld/board (per item, per station). **Decision:** pass / fail / rework / route-to-human. **Label:** confirmed defect (human adjudication, downstream test results, warranty returns) — good labels, though rare-defect classes are few-shot by nature. **Horizon:** milliseconds–seconds at line speed, continuous.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Every unit at every inspected station: thousands–millions of inspections/day per plant. Value concentrates at stations guarding expensive downstream value-add (inspect before you add cost) and at final gates (escape prevention).
7. **Data exhaust** — Images from line cameras, sensor traces (ultrasonic, X-ray, thermal), pass/fail history, rework/scrap records, warranty/field-failure data (the delayed ground truth). Systems of record: **MES, vision systems, QMS**. Image exhaust is generated at will (cameras are cheap); labeled *defect* examples are the scarce resource — defect libraries are the asset.
8. **Actionability** — Fail decision triggers reject/divert mechanisms, rework routing, and — the deeper value — statistical feedback to upstream process control (defect patterns → process drift alarms). Fully automated action at line speed.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: escape rate, false-reject rate, first-pass yield, scrap/rework cost, warranty claims (lagged). Measured continuously against human-adjudicated samples; before/after designs standard. Strong measurability.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Camera/lighting engineering (half the project); defect libraries with rare-class handling; MES integration; re-validation processes in regulated industries (pharma GMP). Feasibility high and improving — few-shot/anomaly approaches reduced labeled-defect requirements substantially (2022–2025).
11. **Industry variants** — **Semiconductor/electronics:** AOI mature for decades — AI reduces false rejects on marginal cases. **Automotive:** paint/surface (Landing AI-class), weld integrity. **Pharma:** regulated validation burden dominates timelines. **Food:** foreign-object + grading, hygiene environments. **Metals/paper:** continuous-web surface inspection at high speed.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Yield/process optimization (defect patterns as symptoms of process causes), scrap & rework reduction (the cost view), predictive maintenance (equipment cause of defects), digital twin (offline what-if). Boundary: inspection detects defects; yield optimization removes their causes.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Machine vision has decades of installed base; deep-learning inspection is now mainstream in electronics/automotive and expanding fast; a top-cited scaled industrial AI use case (case density high across BMW, Foxconn-class manufacturers).
14. **Documented results** — (a) **BMW stud/weld inspection: accuracy 95%→99%, inspection manpower −50%, >$1M savings/plant/year, operating cost −⅓** (BMW communications, ~2021 — company-reported). (b) **BMW paint inspection with Landing AI: −40% defect escape rate, <2s per body** (Landing AI case, 2020s — vendor/company-reported). (c) German automotive case: detection +35%, false positives −28%, inspection time −41%, ~€12M/year warranty savings (case compilation — secondary). (d) Human-baseline 60–80% detection (industry compilations, 2024–2025).
15. **Failure modes** — Lighting/positioning drift silently degrading accuracy; training on yesterday's defect classes missing novel defects (anomaly-detection gap); false-reject creep scrapping good product (the hidden cost); validation burden in regulated environments stalling updates; treating inspection as the fix while the process keeps producing defects; defect-library hoarding preventing transfer across lines/plants.
16. **Typical methodology** — CNN/vision-transformer classification + anomaly detection (autoencoder/embedding) for novel defects, few-shot workflows for rare classes → MES-integrated reject/route + SPC feedback.
17. **Key references** — BMW AI quality communications (2021+); Landing AI case documentation; McKinsey smart-factory research; AOI/machine-vision industry literature (VDMA); Voxel51 industrial-vision landscape (2025).

### G3. Yield & process optimization — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Process optimization; yield optimization/enhancement; golden-batch analysis; advanced process control (APC) augmentation; throughput optimization; recipe/parameter optimization.
2. **Problem statement** — Complex production processes run below their achievable yield/throughput/energy performance because the true relationships between hundreds of process parameters and outcomes exceed human analysis; the same line produces different results on different days without anyone knowing why. The business wants the process run at its demonstrated-best operating envelope, consistently.
3. **Who has it** — Semiconductor (the canonical case), chemicals/refining, pharma (batch), food & beverage, pulp & paper, metals/steel, cement/glass, batteries (the current hot spot). Lives in **process/manufacturing engineering**; owned by plant manager or VP manufacturing excellence.
4. **Economic pool** — Yield points in capital-intensive industries are worth enormous sums: a single yield point at a semiconductor fab is commonly valued in the tens of millions of dollars per year (industry rule of thumb; not source-verified). McKinsey smart-factory/Industry 4.0 aggregates report throughput +10–30% and quality-cost reductions across lighthouse factories (WEF/McKinsey Lighthouse program reports, 2019–2024 — cohort self-reports). The adjacent measured anchor: **Google/DeepMind cut data-center cooling energy ~40%** with the same process-optimization pattern (Google/DeepMind, 2016 — company-reported, measured).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the batch/run/line × parameter set. **Decision:** setpoints/recipe parameters (within safety envelopes); which drift to investigate. **Label:** yield/quality/energy per run — automatic from MES/LIMS, abundant and objective. **Horizon:** per-batch/shift tuning; continuous for APC-integrated control.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Every batch/run is a decision instance (dozens–thousands per site per day); parameter spaces of hundreds of variables. Value concentrates in the highest-margin lines and chronic-variability steps identified by loss trees.
7. **Data exhaust** — Historian time-series for all process variables, batch records, LIMS quality results, recipe/setpoint logs, maintenance context, raw-material lot data. Systems of record: **historians, MES, LIMS, DCS/SCADA**. Data richness is extreme; the challenges are contextualization (batch boundaries, product changeovers) and confounding (parameters move together under operator policy — observational causal inference problem).
8. **Actionability** — Recommendations flow to operator guidance (advisory mode) or closed-loop setpoint control (APC integration) — the mature path is advisory→supervised-closed-loop. Process-engineering change control governs action; safety envelopes bound everything.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: first-pass yield, OEE, energy per unit, quality variance (Cpk), scrap. Continuous, automatic measurement against run history; A/B by line or campaign is often possible. Strong measurability — confounded by raw-material and product-mix shifts, so baseline discipline matters.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Historian + MES contextualization (the real project); process-engineering partnership (domain causality knowledge); change-control tolerance for experimentation. Feasibility medium: data exists, but causal validity and safety/change-control make this an engineering-heavy build; strongest where DOE (designed experiments) culture already exists.
11. **Industry variants** — **Semiconductor:** virtual metrology, run-to-run control — decades of practice, AI deepens it. **Chemicals/refining:** APC installed base; ML augments models. **Pharma batch:** golden-batch analytics, regulatory change control slows loops. **Food/CPG:** giveaway (overfill) reduction — small % on huge volume. **Batteries/gigafactories:** yield ramp as the make-or-break economics (2023–2026).
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Quality inspection (detects the symptom), PdM (equipment causes), energy optimization (a specific objective slice), digital twin (the simulation environment), scrap/rework (the accounting view). Boundary: G2 finds bad units; G3 moves the distribution so fewer bad units occur.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — APC is decades-old in process industries; ML-augmented optimization is mainstream at leaders (semis, chems) and pilot-stage at mid-market; WEF/McKinsey Lighthouse network documents ~150+ scaled exemplar factories (2019–2024) against a long tail of pilots — the canonical pilot-purgatory family.
14. **Documented results** — (a) **DeepMind/Google: −40% cooling energy, ~−15% total PUE overhead** (2016 — company-reported, measured, the pattern's most famous instance). (b) WEF/McKinsey Lighthouse cohort: double-digit yield/throughput/energy gains across sites (2019–2024 — self-reported cohort). (c) Semiconductor run-to-run/virtual-metrology literature documents sustained yield-learning acceleration (academic + industry, 2000s–2020s — domain-established). Named non-lighthouse audited cases are scarce (evidence note).
15. **Failure modes** — Correlational recommendations that fail causally (parameters co-moved under old policy); golden-batch mimicry that just rediscovers known settings; recommendations outside validated envelopes (safety/regulatory rejection); data contextualization debt (batch boundaries wrong → garbage); operator distrust in advisory mode → suggestions ignored; wins on one line failing to transfer (different equipment idiosyncrasies).
16. **Typical methodology** — Historian feature engineering → GBM/Gaussian-process yield models + SHAP driver analysis → constrained Bayesian optimization / RL for setpoints; advisory-first deployment.
17. **Key references** — DeepMind data-center cooling publications (2016); WEF/McKinsey Global Lighthouse Network reports (2019–2024); semiconductor APC/virtual-metrology literature; process-industry APC practitioner material.

## H. IT, engineering & security

12 use cases. Full dossiers: **cybersecurity threat detection & response**, **incident prediction & triage (AIOps)**, **code generation & review assistance**. Compact table: root-cause analysis, alert correlation & noise reduction, capacity planning, test generation, release-risk prediction, log anomaly detection, phishing detection, vulnerability prioritization, identity/access anomaly detection.

### H1. Cybersecurity threat detection & response — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Threat detection & response; SOC analytics; SIEM/XDR detection; intrusion detection; behavioral threat analytics; managed detection & response (MDR).
2. **Problem statement** — Attackers hide in oceans of security telemetry; SOC teams drown in alerts that are mostly false positives while real intrusions dwell undetected for months. The business must find true attacks in the noise fast enough to contain them before material damage.
3. **Who has it** — Every enterprise; intensity peaks in finance, healthcare, critical infrastructure, tech, and government. Lives in the **SOC under the CISO**; owned by CISO / VP security operations.
4. **Economic pool** — Average breach cost **$4.44M globally, $10.22M in the US** (IBM Cost of a Data Breach, 2025 — survey-based measurement); mean breach lifecycle **241 days** to identify+contain (IBM 2025). Organizations with extensive security AI/automation show **~$1.9M lower average breach costs** (IBM 2025 — associational, not causal). SOC noise context: ~46–53% of alerts are false positives; up to 40% of alerts never investigated; a DBIR-cited pattern that most breaches had alerts that were ignored (survey compilations, 2024–2026 — survey-based).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the alert/entity-under-suspicion (host, identity, session) — increasingly the *incident* (correlated cluster). **Decision:** triage priority, investigate, contain (isolate host, disable account), or dismiss. **Label:** confirmed-incident dispositions from case history — decent for triage; ground truth for *missed* attacks unknowable (same epistemic hole as AML). **Horizon:** minutes–hours; continuous 24/7.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Thousands–millions of alerts/day pre-correlation at large enterprises; analyst capacity of dozens of deep investigations/day. Triage ranking is the whole economics — value concentrates in the tiny true-positive tail and in containment speed.
7. **Data exhaust** — Endpoint telemetry (EDR), network flows, identity/auth logs, cloud control-plane logs, email, threat intel, alert-disposition history. Systems of record: **SIEM (Splunk/Sentinel), EDR/XDR (CrowdStrike-class), SOAR case managers**. Exhaust volume is extreme; disposition labels are decent; adversarial drift constant.
8. **Actionability** — Ranked/enriched incidents → SOAR playbooks: host isolation, credential disable, token revocation, block rules — increasingly auto-executed for high-confidence classes. Containment actions are reversible and fast, making semi-automation tractable.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: MTTD/MTTR, dwell time, false-positive rate, alert-closure throughput, breach frequency/cost (rare-event). IBM's dataset provides industry benchmarks; internal before/after on triage metrics is straightforward. True prevention is counterfactual — measured via red-team/purple-team exercises.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Telemetry coverage (EDR deployment); SIEM/SOAR plumbing; disposition-history hygiene; 24/7 process. Feasibility high for triage/correlation augmentation (mature vendor capability); the buy-over-build gravity is strong — detection content is a product, not a project, below the largest scales.
11. **Industry variants** — **Finance:** fraud-security convergence, regulatory reporting clocks. **Healthcare:** ransomware + patient-safety stakes, legacy device estates. **Cloud-native:** control-plane/identity attacks dominate. **OT/industrial:** safety-critical, passive-monitoring-only constraints. **Government/defense:** nation-state adversaries, classification boundaries.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Phishing detection (email vector), identity/access anomaly (UEBA), vulnerability prioritization (pre-breach hygiene), log anomaly detection (the generic engine), AIOps (same telemetry pattern, different adversary: entropy vs attacker). Boundary vs AIOps: security fights an adaptive adversary; ops fights complexity.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Universal at the tool level (SIEM/EDR ship ML detection); SOC-augmentation AI mainstream and accelerating (gen-AI investigation copilots 2023–2026). Security is among the highest AI-adoption domains in IT (Deloitte: IT/cyber top function at ~46% gen-AI adoption, 2024).
14. **Documented results** — (a) IBM 2025: AI/automation-heavy security programs associate with **$1.9M lower breach cost** and shorter lifecycles (survey association). (b) Breach lifecycle fell to 241 days, a 9-year low, attributed partly to AI-powered detection (IBM 2025 — survey inference). (c) Vendor-reported triage compression (e.g., Elastic: alert volumes >1,000/day → 8 actionable, −75% false positives — vendor case, not audited). (d) 60% of orgs using SOC AI report ≥25% investigation-time reduction (Gurucul survey, 2025 — self-report).
15. **Failure modes** — Alert-fatigue economics unchanged by adding another detector (noise sources unfixed); over-trusted auto-containment causing outages (false-positive isolation of production hosts); adversarial adaptation to known detection logic; disposition labels encoding analyst shortcuts; model coverage gaps in unlogged corners (shadow IT — IBM 2025 flags shadow-AI breach premium +$670k); measuring closure throughput while dwell time on novel attacks grows.
16. **Typical methodology** — Streaming detection rules + supervised triage ranking on disposition history + UEBA anomaly models + LLM investigation summarization/copilot → SOAR playbook execution.
17. **Key references** — IBM Cost of a Data Breach (2025); Verizon DBIR (2024); SOC-fatigue survey compilations (IDC/FireEye; Osterman 2025); Gurucul AI-SOC survey (2025); MITRE ATT&CK (framework grounding).

### H2. Incident prediction & triage (AIOps) — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — AIOps; IT incident prediction/triage; event correlation & noise reduction; anomaly-based monitoring; predictive IT operations; MTTR reduction analytics.
2. **Problem statement** — Complex IT estates emit floods of alerts across fragmented monitoring tools; operations teams chase symptoms, outages surprise everyone, and diagnosis consumes the war room. The business wants incidents predicted or caught at onset, deduplicated into one actionable signal, and diagnosed fast.
3. **Who has it** — Any organization running significant digital infrastructure — banks, telcos, retailers/e-commerce, SaaS providers, airlines, public sector. Lives in **IT operations / SRE / NOC**; owned by VP infrastructure/operations or head of SRE.
4. **Economic pool** — IT downtime has long been benchmarked at **~$5,600/minute (~$336k/hour)** (Gartner-attributed estimate, mid-2010s — dated but still the canonical anchor; newer syntheses cluster $140k–300k+/hour, higher for digital-revenue leaders). Add the labor pool of NOC/SRE toil and the revenue/reputation cost of customer-facing outages (major outages routinely cost $10M+ per incident at large digital businesses — industry estimate; not source-verified).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the alert-cluster/incident (and the service/CI behind it). **Decision:** suppress/merge, priority, routing to owning team, suspected cause. **Label:** incident tickets with resolution codes and postmortems — abundant, moderately noisy. **Horizon:** real-time correlation; minutes–hours triage; prediction lead times of minutes–days for capacity-type failures.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Millions of events/day → thousands of alerts → dozens–hundreds of incidents; the compression ratio *is* the product. Value concentrates in Sev-1 prevention/shortening and in deduplicating the long tail of noise.
7. **Data exhaust** — Metrics, logs, traces (observability stack), alert streams, topology/CMDB, change/deploy events, incident tickets + postmortems. Systems of record: **observability platforms (Datadog/Grafana/Splunk), ITSM (ServiceNow), CI/CD systems**. Exhaust is huge; topology/CMDB accuracy is the classic weak dependency; change events are the highest-signal feature (most incidents follow changes).
8. **Actionability** — Correlated incident → auto-ticket with probable cause and owning team; runbook automation for known patterns (restart, failover, rollback); paging suppression. Action path mature via ITSM/SOAR-style automation; auto-remediation adopted incrementally by risk class.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: MTTR/MTTD, alert volume per operator, incident count by severity, change-failure rate, availability/SLOs. Hard, already-tracked metrics; before/after on alert compression and MTTR is standard. Good measurability, though incident mix shifts confound naive comparisons.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Consolidated observability data; ticket/postmortem history; CMDB/topology hygiene (or graph inference in lieu); SRE process ownership. Feasibility high for correlation/noise reduction (mature vendor category: BigPanda, Moogsoft-lineage, ServiceNow AIOps); prediction proper is harder and oversold — most realized value is correlation + triage + RCA acceleration.
11. **Industry variants** — **Banks/telcos:** NOC-scale legacy estates — the historical core market. **Cloud-native/SaaS:** SLO-driven SRE, deploy-correlated incidents. **Retail:** seasonal peak readiness. **Telecom network ops** proper is family L (network optimization) — adjacent but distinct (network elements vs IT services).
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Root-cause analysis and alert correlation (sub-problems, listed compactly below), log anomaly detection (engine), capacity planning (preventive sibling), security detection (H1 twin), release-risk prediction (change-side prevention). Boundary vs H1: same telemetry shape, no adversary.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Mainstream in large enterprises: ITSM/observability vendors ship AIOps natively; IT is the fastest-growing AI-adoption function (McKinsey 2025: IT function AI use jumped 27%→36% in six months). Gen-AI incident summarization/copilots diffusing rapidly (2024–2026).
14. **Documented results** — (a) Survey: 60% of AI-adopting ops/SOC teams report ≥25% investigation-time reduction; 21% report >50% (Gurucul 2025 — self-report, security-adjacent). (b) Vendor-reported alert-compression ratios of 90%+ are routine (BigPanda-class case studies — vendor-reported). (c) AT&T: AI handles >95% of service diagnostics without human intervention; average network downtime −50% over ~24 months (AT&T Analyst Day-era reporting, 2024 — company-reported, secondary). Independent audited AIOps ROI studies are scarce (evidence note).
15. **Failure modes** — Garbage topology → confident wrong root causes; alert-noise reduction that suppresses the one novel signal that mattered; automation trust destroyed by one bad auto-remediation; tool sprawl adding AIOps as yet another pane of glass; MTTR gains claimed while incident *prevention* (the bigger prize: change quality, capacity) is untouched; prediction marketing outrunning correlation reality.
16. **Typical methodology** — Streaming event correlation (clustering/graph) + supervised triage on ticket history + log-anomaly models + LLM incident summarization/RCA drafting → ITSM/runbook automation.
17. **Key references** — Gartner AIOps market guides (2019–2025); Google SRE literature (SLO/postmortem grounding); Gartner downtime-cost benchmark (mid-2010s, dated); vendor evidence base (BigPanda/ServiceNow — flagged as vendor); DORA/Accelerate research for change-failure context.

### H3. Code generation & review assistance — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — AI coding assistants; code generation; pair-programming copilots; code review automation; AI-assisted software engineering; (agentic coding at the frontier).
2. **Problem statement** — Software engineering capacity is expensive and perpetually the bottleneck; developers spend large fractions of time on boilerplate, comprehension of unfamiliar code, tests, and review latency. The business wants more shipped software per engineer-hour without degrading quality or security.
3. **Who has it** — Every organization that writes software — tech, banks (largest private dev workforces), retailers, industrials, government. Lives in **engineering under the CTO/VP Eng**; increasingly platform-engineering-owned.
4. **Economic pool** — Global developer labor is a multi-hundred-billion-dollar annual pool (~30M developers worldwide — industry estimate; not source-verified). McKinsey (2023) puts software engineering among the four functions carrying ~75% of gen-AI value, sized at **$580–1,200B annually** (projection). The best-controlled measurement: **GitHub Copilot RCT — 55.8% faster completion** of a standardized task (GitHub/Microsoft experiment, 2022–2023 — RCT on one task type; generalization debated).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the code change (completion, PR, review comment, test). **Decision:** what code to write/suggest; whether a change is safe to merge. **Label:** suggestion acceptance, PR merge outcomes, defect/incident linkage (delayed, noisy). **Horizon:** seconds (completion) to days (PR cycle), continuous.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Per-keystroke to per-PR: the highest-frequency knowledge-work assistance loop in the enterprise. Value concentrates in large codebases with strong conventions (context for the model) and in review/test latency on the critical path.
7. **Data exhaust** — Repos + full history, PRs/reviews, CI results, issue trackers, incident links, telemetry on suggestion acceptance. Systems of record: **git hosting (GitHub/GitLab), CI/CD, issue trackers**. Exhaust is extraordinary — version control is the best-labeled behavioral dataset in any function.
8. **Actionability** — Suggestions land directly in the IDE/PR; the human merges — built-in human-in-the-loop. Agentic variants (issue→PR automation) extend actuation; CI is the guardrail. Adoption friction is low (developers self-adopt).
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: cycle time, PR throughput, review latency, change-failure rate, DORA metrics; acceptance-rate telemetry native. Measurement is active and contested: RCT (55%) vs field studies showing smaller or context-dependent effects — rigorous enterprise-scale causal measurement remains genuinely hard (task heterogeneity, Hawthorne effects). A 2025 METR RCT on experienced open-source developers even measured *slower* completion with AI on familiar codebases (METR, 2025 — RCT, narrow population), underscoring context-dependence.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — IDE/platform rollout, IP/security policy (code exfiltration concerns), CI/test maturity (the safety net that lets speed become throughput). Highest-feasibility gen-AI use case: buy, turn on, measure. The hard part is attributing business impact beyond activity metrics.
11. **Industry variants** — **Regulated (banking/med-device):** provenance, license-compliance, audit requirements. **Legacy modernization (COBOL/mainframe):** translation-assist niche with outsized value claims (measured evidence thin). **Embedded/safety-critical:** certification limits usage. **Tech:** agentic frontier adoption.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Test generation, code review automation, release-risk prediction (compact below), documentation generation, incident RCA (H2 overlap when code-aware). Boundary: this dossier = producing/reviewing code; release-risk = predicting change outcomes.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — The fastest tool-level adoption of any enterprise gen-AI use case: software engineering is a top-5 gen-AI function in McKinsey 2025; GitHub reported millions of Copilot users and majority-of-Fortune-500 penetration (GitHub company reporting, 2023–2024 — vendor-reported). Enterprise-wide *measured* productivity programs are far rarer than seat deployment.
14. **Documented results** — (a) **GitHub RCT: −55.8% task time** (2022–2023 — measured, single-task RCT). (b) Field studies at large firms report 10–30% PR-throughput-style gains for active users (Microsoft/Accenture-collaborated studies, 2023–2024 — quasi-experimental, self-selected usage). (c) METR 2025 RCT: −19% (slower) for experts on familiar repos (measured, narrow population). (d) Acceptance-rate telemetry ~30% of suggestions accepted (GitHub-reported). The spread across (a)–(c) is the honest headline: effect size depends heavily on task, codebase familiarity, and developer experience.
15. **Failure modes** — Activity metrics (suggestions accepted) masquerading as productivity; quality/security regressions from confidently wrong code (studies show mixed vulnerability effects); license/IP contamination anxieties stalling rollout; skill formation of juniors (unresolved); measuring individual speed while system throughput is constrained elsewhere (review, deploy, requirements); cost per seat vs realized value never reconciled.
16. **Typical methodology** — LLM code models with repo-context retrieval (IDE completion, chat, PR review bots, test generation); agentic issue→PR pipelines gated by CI.
17. **Key references** — GitHub Copilot RCT (2022–2023); Brynjolfsson-adjacent field studies (2023–2024); METR developer RCT (2025); DORA/Accelerate metrics framework; McKinsey gen-AI economic potential — software engineering chapter (2023).

## I. HR / people

8 use cases. Full dossiers: **attrition prediction**, **recruiting funnel optimization**. Compact table: workforce planning, resume/job matching, skills inference, scheduling optimization, engagement-survey mining, internal mobility recommendation.

### I1. Employee attrition prediction — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Attrition prediction; employee churn; turnover risk; flight-risk modeling; retention analytics; regrettable-attrition prediction.
2. **Problem statement** — Valuable employees quit with little warning; each departure costs recruiting, ramp-up, lost knowledge, and team disruption. The business wants to know which employees (or segments/roles) are at elevated risk of leaving, and which controllable factors drive it, in time to intervene.
3. **Who has it** — Any large employer; most acute in high-turnover industries (retail, logistics, contact centers, healthcare nursing) and in scarce-skill segments (tech, specialized clinical). Lives in **people analytics under the CHRO**; owned by CHRO or VP people analytics; line managers act.
4. **Economic pool** — Replacing an employee costs **50–200% of annual salary** (Gallup/SHRM, 2019-era figures, still cited); US voluntary turnover costs **~$1T/year** (Gallup, 2019 — modeled estimate). High-turnover operations (e.g., contact centers with 30–60% annual attrition — industry range; not source-verified) carry attrition as a top-3 controllable cost.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the employee (or role × site segment — segment-level framing avoids much of the ethical hazard). **Decision:** manager check-in, compensation/plan adjustment, workload/rotation change, or systemic policy fix. **Label:** voluntary exit within horizon (regrettable vs non-regrettable distinction matters). **Horizon:** 3–12 months; scored quarterly.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Whole workforce re-scored quarterly; interventions are low-frequency, judgment-mediated (unlike D/E/F families). Value concentrates in scarce-skill and high-training-cost roles, and in systemic fixes (a driver affecting thousands) more than individual saves.
7. **Data exhaust** — HRIS core data (tenure, comp, promotions, manager changes), engagement surveys, time/attendance patterns, performance ratings, labor-market signals; (communications metadata is technically available and ethically radioactive). Systems of record: **HRIS (Workday/SAP SuccessFactors), survey platforms, ATS**. Data exists but is fragmented and politically guarded; sample sizes are small vs consumer churn.
8. **Actionability** — Driver reports → policy interventions (comp bands, promotion velocity, manager quality programs); individual risk → manager conversations (delicate). System of action: manager workflows + HR programs — human-mediated, slow, and the weakest actionability in the catalog relative to model quality.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: voluntary turnover %, regrettable attrition, time-to-fill, ramp cost. Impact measurable at segment level (before/after on targeted populations); individual-level A/B is ethically constrained. Attribution to the model vs the intervention program is usually blurred.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Multi-year HRIS history; exit-reason coding; works-council/privacy clearance (GDPR; EU AI Act treats employment AI as high-risk); trust that scores won't leak into performance decisions. Technical feasibility high (classic tabular churn); **organizational/ethical feasibility is the binding constraint**.
11. **Industry variants** — **Retail/logistics hourly:** schedule quality and commute dominate drivers; high-N, statistically strong. **Tech/professional:** small-N, manager and growth-path drivers. **Healthcare nursing:** burnout-driven, patient-safety consequence. **Contact center/BPO:** attrition economics are the business model (NBER agent-assist study showed AI tooling itself reduced attrition — 2023).
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Workforce planning (aggregate flows), engagement mining (driver signal source), internal mobility (a retention treatment), scheduling optimization (a driver lever for hourly work). Boundary: attrition predicts exits; workforce planning decides headcount actions given flows.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — People analytics teams are standard in large enterprises and attrition modeling is their canonical project; HR is a lower-mid adopter overall (Deloitte 2024: ~23% gen-AI adoption in HR). Much modeling remains descriptive dashboards; closed-loop intervention programs are the minority.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Gallup $1T pool and 50–200% replacement-cost anchors (2019 — modeled). (b) NBER contact-center study: AI assist reduced agent attrition (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023 — measured, as a side effect of tooling, not of attrition modeling per se). (c) Named, quantified attrition-model outcome studies are rare in public — companies don't publish them (evidence gap; vendor claims exist but are flagged as unverified).
15. **Failure modes** — Scores leaking into promotion/termination decisions (trust collapse, legal exposure); proxy discrimination (drivers correlated with protected classes); manager "interventions" that accelerate exits; small-N overfitting; predicting without any retention budget/program; works-council/regulatory stop after build (EU AI Act high-risk class); measuring saves that were never real leavers.
16. **Typical methodology** — Tabular GBM/survival analysis + SHAP driver analysis, reported at segment level; engagement-text NLP as feature input.
17. **Key references** — Gallup turnover research (2019); SHRM retention materials; Brynjolfsson et al. NBER 31161 (2023) attrition side-finding; EU AI Act employment-AI provisions (2024); people-analytics practitioner literature.

### I2. Recruiting funnel optimization — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Recruiting/talent-acquisition optimization; candidate screening & ranking; sourcing optimization; interview scheduling automation; time-to-fill reduction; (resume/job matching is the core sub-capability).
2. **Problem statement** — Hiring funnels leak: too many unqualified applicants to screen, good candidates lost to slow process, recruiter hours consumed by scheduling and screening, and quality-of-hire barely measured. The business wants the funnel faster and better-calibrated at every stage without introducing bias.
3. **Who has it** — Every scaled employer; most acute in high-volume hourly hiring (retail, logistics, healthcare support, contact centers) and scarce-skill technical hiring. Lives in **talent acquisition under the CHRO**; owned by head of TA.
4. **Economic pool** — Pool = recruiter labor + vacancy cost (each unfilled-day of a productive role costs its daily margin contribution) + mis-hire cost (commonly cited at ~30% of first-year salary per bad hire — US DOL-attributed figure; not source-verified). Time-to-fill runs ~40+ days on average across industries (SHRM-era benchmarks; not re-verified). High-volume hiring is a genuine operations problem: thousands of applications/day at large retailers.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the application/candidate × requisition. **Decision:** advance/reject/route at each stage; scheduling; offer prioritization. **Label:** stage progression, offer-accept, and (ideally) on-the-job performance/retention — the quality label is delayed and rarely joined back (the field's core measurement failure). **Horizon:** hours–weeks per stage; continuous flow.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Thousands–millions of applications/year; every one gets screened somehow. Value concentrates in high-volume roles (screening automation) and executive/scarce roles (sourcing quality, where per-hire stakes are highest).
7. **Data exhaust** — ATS histories (applications, stages, outcomes, times), resumes/profiles, job descriptions, interview feedback, assessment scores, post-hire HRIS performance/retention (join rarely made). Systems of record: **ATS (Workday/Greenhouse/iCIMS), assessment platforms, HRIS**. Exhaust is rich but labels encode historical human bias — the central hazard.
8. **Actionability** — Ranking → recruiter queues; knock-out automation; automated scheduling/chatbot pre-screen (mature, low-risk); sourcing outreach lists. Action path through ATS workflow is direct; the sensitive part is *rejection* automation.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: time-to-fill, cost-per-hire, funnel conversion by stage, offer-accept rate, 90-day/1-year retention of hires, quality-of-hire (where measured). Stage metrics are easy; quality-of-hire requires the ATS↔HRIS join and patience — few close this loop, so most programs prove speed, not quality.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — ATS data hygiene; adverse-impact testing regime (EEOC/UGESP in the US; NYC Local Law 144 requires bias audits of automated hiring tools, 2023; EU AI Act high-risk class); legal review. Scheduling/chat automation: very feasible, deployed widely. Screening/ranking: technically easy, **compliance-gated**.
11. **Industry variants** — **High-volume hourly:** chatbot screening + instant scheduling (measurable, mainstream — retail/QSR). **Tech/professional:** sourcing and matching quality, small-N. **Healthcare:** credential verification (document AI). **Staffing industry:** matching is the entire business model. **Public sector:** merit rules constrain automation.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Resume/job matching (the model core — compact row below), skills inference (the representation layer), internal mobility (same matching aimed inward), workforce planning (demand side of hiring). Boundary: this dossier is the funnel process; matching is its inference engine.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Screening chatbots/scheduling automation deployed at scale in high-volume hiring (Paradox-class vendors — vendor-reported scale); LLM resume screening spreading fast 2024–2026. HR overall lower-mid adoption (Deloitte 2024 ~23%). The Amazon scrapped-tool case (2018, Reuters-reported) remains the canonical bias cautionary tale.
14. **Documented results** — (a) High-volume hiring automation vendors report time-to-fill reductions from weeks to days for hourly roles (vendor-reported, e.g., QSR case studies — not independently audited; flagged). (b) Amazon's abandoned resume model (2018 — documented failure, bias against women's-college signals; Reuters). (c) NYC LL144 audit filings (2023+) create a public record of adverse-impact ratios — an emerging evidence source. Rigorous quality-of-hire improvement studies: essentially absent publicly (evidence gap).
15. **Failure modes** — Bias absorption from historical labels (the defining risk — Amazon case); optimizing recruiter throughput while candidate experience degrades; knock-out rules discarding non-traditional strong candidates; quality-of-hire never measured, so "optimization" means speed only; compliance stop-work after deployment (LL144/EU AI Act); candidates gaming AI screens with AI-written applications (the 2024–2026 arms race).
16. **Typical methodology** — LLM/embedding resume-job matching + stage-conversion GBMs + scheduling/chat automation; adverse-impact testing wrapped around all of it.
17. **Key references** — Reuters on Amazon's scrapped tool (2018); NYC Local Law 144 (2023) and audit literature; EU AI Act employment provisions (2024); SHRM benchmarks; high-volume hiring vendor case literature (flagged vendor).

## J. Legal & document-heavy work

6 use cases. Full dossiers: **contract review & extraction**, **document classification & routing**. Compact table: e-discovery, obligation tracking, regulatory-change monitoring, document summarization into structured records.

### J1. Contract review & extraction — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Contract review; contract analysis/intelligence; clause extraction; contract abstraction; due-diligence review; redline/playbook review; CLM intelligence.
2. **Problem statement** — Organizations sign and hold thousands of contracts whose terms — obligations, rights, risks, prices, renewal dates — are locked in unstructured prose that no one can query; reviewing new contracts is slow, expensive, and inconsistent. The business wants contract contents extracted, risks flagged against its playbook, and review time collapsed.
3. **Who has it** — Every enterprise (procurement + sales contracts); intensity peaks in legal departments, financial services, real estate, healthcare, M&A-active acquirers, and law firms. Lives in **legal/contracting operations**; owned by GC or head of legal ops (procurement/sales ops co-own their sides).
4. **Economic pool** — Poor contract management costs companies up to **~9% of annual revenue** in value leakage (World Commerce & Contracting/IACCM research, mid-2010s onward — survey-based estimate). Review-labor anchor: **JPMorgan COIN automated ~360,000 lawyer/loan-officer hours per year** of commercial-loan-agreement review (JPMorgan annual-report coverage, 2017 — company-reported). Outside counsel review runs $200–1,000+/hour (market rates).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the contract / clause. **Decision:** extract fields; accept/negotiate/escalate deviations; route for approval. **Label:** attorney-validated extractions and playbook deviations — generated as a by-product of review workflows (good labels where CLM discipline exists). **Horizon:** per contract (hours–days); portfolio extraction as batch programs.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Hundreds–thousands of new contracts/month at large enterprises + backlogs of 10k–1M+ legacy contracts for extraction programs (M&A diligence: thousands of documents in weeks). Value concentrates in high-volume standardized paper (NDAs, MSAs, leases) and high-stakes diligence.
7. **Data exhaust** — Contract repositories (PDFs/Word), CLM metadata, negotiation redline history, playbooks/clause libraries, matter-management records. Systems of record: **CLM (Icertis/Ironclad/DocuSign CLM), document management (iManage), deal rooms**. The chronic reality: contracts scattered across shared drives — repository consolidation is often step one.
8. **Actionability** — Extracted terms populate CLM fields (renewal alerts, obligation calendars — J-family obligation tracking consumes them); deviation flags drive redlines and approval routing; diligence summaries drive deal decisions. Action path exists wherever CLM/legal-ops workflow exists.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: review turnaround time, cost per contract, extraction accuracy vs attorney baseline, leakage events (missed renewals/obligations). Benchmarkable directly: the LawGeex study design (AI vs 20 lawyers on NDAs: **AI 94% accuracy vs 85% human average; 26 seconds vs 92 minutes**, 2018 — controlled comparison) shows how; production programs measure time/cost per document.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Document corpus assembly; playbook codification (the real intellectual work); attorney review workflow for validation. Feasibility high and rising steeply — LLMs made clause extraction near-commodity (2023–2026); the binding constraints are repository chaos and professional-responsibility review requirements.
11. **Industry variants** — **Financial services:** LIBOR-transition and regulatory repapering programs proved mass extraction at scale. **Real estate:** lease abstraction (rent, options, CAM). **Healthcare:** payer-provider contracts vs reimbursement analysis. **Procurement:** price/term leakage (F-family overlap). **M&A:** time-boxed diligence — the premium use.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Obligation tracking (downstream consumer), contract-clause risk review (E-family compliance lens — same engine), e-discovery (litigation posture, different scale/rules), document summarization (generic sibling). Boundary: review/extraction structures the document; obligation tracking operates the calendar it produces.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Legal was a late adopter now moving fast: legal/risk/compliance gen-AI adoption ~21% of orgs (Deloitte, 2024) but legal-AI tool adoption inside legal teams jumped post-2023 (industry surveys); CLM vendor market consolidated and AI review is now a standard feature. Law-firm adoption of gen-AI research/review tools is broad but supervised.
14. **Documented results** — (a) **LawGeex NDA study (2018): AI 94% vs lawyers 85% mean accuracy; 26s vs 92min** (controlled study). (b) **JPMorgan COIN: ~360k hours/yr automated** (2017 — company-reported). (c) Benchmark compilations: 60–90% review-time reductions on standardized contracts; e.g., a multinational's average review 92→12 minutes (case compilations, 2024–2026 — vendor-adjacent, not audited). (d) WorldCC ~9% revenue leakage pool (survey estimate).
15. **Failure modes** — Extraction accuracy fine on standard clauses, brittle on bespoke language (the tail is where risk lives); hallucinated confidence in generated summaries (professional-responsibility incidents — sanctioned fabricated citations in filings, 2023+, are the cultural scar); playbook never codified so "review" lacks ground truth; repository chaos starving the program; attorney adoption resistance (review economics threaten billing models at firms); privilege/confidentiality handling errors.
16. **Typical methodology** — LLM extraction/classification against clause taxonomies + playbook-deviation comparison + human attorney validation loop (RAG over repository for Q&A).
17. **Key references** — LawGeex comparative study (2018); WorldCC/IACCM leakage research; JPMorgan COIN coverage (2017); CLM market analyses; ABA/professional-responsibility guidance on AI use (2023–2025).

### J2. Document classification & routing — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Document classification; intelligent document processing (IDP); mailroom automation; document triage/routing; capture & indexing; unstructured-to-structured intake.
2. **Problem statement** — Organizations receive continuous streams of heterogeneous documents — forms, correspondence, claims, applications, faxes (healthcare's shame), attachments — that must be identified, indexed, and routed to the right process before any work can happen. Manual intake is slow, error-prone, and scales linearly with volume.
3. **Who has it** — Insurance (claims/underwriting intake), banking (loan ops, KYC docs), healthcare (referrals, faxes, medical records), government (benefits, permits), logistics (bills of lading, customs), legal. Lives in **operations/shared services**; owned by COO-line operations leaders.
4. **Economic pool** — Intake/indexing labor across back offices is a large fraction of the ~$200B+ global BPO document-processing market (industry estimate; not source-verified); the invoice sub-case alone shows the unit economics ($10.18→$2.36 per document — Ardent-class benchmarks, D1). Error costs compound downstream (misrouted claims/referrals delay whole processes; healthcare referral leakage is a named revenue problem).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the inbound document/page/attachment. **Decision:** document type, key fields, destination queue/system. **Label:** operator-confirmed type/fields — generated automatically by the correction workflow (excellent continuous labels). **Horizon:** seconds–hours at arrival, continuous.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Tens of thousands–millions of documents/month at document-heavy institutions. Value concentrates in the top document types by volume and in eliminating the sort/index step entirely (straight-through to the process).
7. **Data exhaust** — Document images/PDFs/emails + historical type/field/routing decisions from capture systems and process queues. Systems of record: **capture/IDP platforms (ABBYY/Kofax-lineage, cloud Document AI), ECM (OnBase/FileNet), process systems (claims, LOS)**. Deep labeled history nearly always exists.
8. **Actionability** — Classified+extracted documents auto-index into ECM and instantiate/attach to the right process (claim, loan file, referral) — pure straight-through processing; exceptions queue with reasons. No adoption barrier: the action is the process plumbing.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: straight-through rate, cost per document, intake cycle time, misroute/rework rate, backlog age. Hard operating metrics with clean before/after — among the most provable automation categories.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Volume-ranked document taxonomy; capture infrastructure; integration to destination systems; exception workflow. Very high feasibility — vision-LLMs collapsed the per-type template engineering that historically limited coverage; long-tail document types are now tractable.
11. **Industry variants** — **Insurance:** FNOL packets, medical records for claims (family L). **Banking:** loan files, trade-finance documents (E-family AML overlap). **Healthcare:** the fax problem — referrals/orders; HIPAA constraints. **Government:** benefits applications at census scale (L public sector). **Logistics:** customs/BoL, multilingual.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Invoice processing (D1 — the most industrialized instance), contract extraction (J1 — deeper single-type analysis), ticket triage (B1 — the born-digital sibling), summarization-to-structured-records (J-compact). Boundary: J2 identifies and routes anything; J1 deeply understands one genre.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — IDP is a mature, large software category predating gen-AI (capture/OCR lineage decades old); LLM upgrade cycle in full swing 2023–2026. One of the most widely *productionized* AI categories by document volume, though rarely headline-grabbing.
14. **Documented results** — (a) The D1 benchmark chain (cost/invoice 4–5× delta; touchless rates 49–80% — Ardent-class, 2023–2026) is this use case's best-quantified instance (measured benchmarks). (b) JPMorgan COIN (2017) again as the document-automation anchor (company-reported). (c) IDP vendor case studies claim 60–90% intake-labor reduction at high straight-through rates (vendor-reported; not independently audited — flagged). Named audited public results outside invoices are thin (evidence note).
15. **Failure modes** — Long-tail document types eroding straight-through economics (the 80/20 tail); extraction confidence miscalibrated → silent field errors propagating into downstream systems (worse than a rejection); exception queues understaffed (automation theater); upstream digitization skipped (faxes scanned at terrible quality); taxonomy drift as business forms change; per-page vendor pricing eating the savings.
16. **Typical methodology** — Vision-LLM classification + field extraction with confidence thresholds → straight-through routing + human-verify exception loop (the correction data continuously retrains).
17. **Key references** — Ardent Partners/APQC AP benchmarks (2023–2024) as the quantified instance; IDP market analyses (Everest/HFS); cloud Document-AI service documentation; healthcare-fax problem literature.

## K. Executive / cross-functional

6 use cases. Full dossiers: **KPI anomaly detection & alerting**, **driver analysis ("why did the metric move")**. Compact table: scenario planning, board/ops reporting automation, M&A target screening, strategy research synthesis.

### K1. KPI anomaly detection & alerting — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Metric/KPI anomaly detection; business monitoring; metric observability; automated business alerting; "Datadog for business metrics."
2. **Problem statement** — Business metrics break silently — a payment integration fails in one country, a pricing error halves margin on a category, a funnel step collapses after a release — and are discovered days or weeks later in a monthly review, after the damage compounds. The business wants material metric movements detected and routed to an owner within hours.
3. **Who has it** — Every data-generating business; most acute in multi-dimensional digital businesses (e-commerce, marketplaces, fintech, telco) where problems hide in slice intersections (country × platform × payment-method). Lives in **data/analytics teams serving the C-suite**; owned by CDO/VP analytics; consumed by every function head.
4. **Economic pool** — The pool is incident-shaped: each undetected business incident costs its duration × run-rate impact (a broken checkout flow in one market can silently cost $100k+/day at scale — illustrative arithmetic; magnitude firm-specific). No consolidated analyst sizing exists for this category (evidence gap — stated qualitatively); its value is documented incident-by-incident, analogous to IT downtime (H2's $5,600/min anchor is the structural cousin).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the metric × dimension slice × time window. **Decision:** alert or not; whom to route to; severity. **Label:** confirmed incidents/root causes from responder feedback — accumulates only if the alert workflow captures dispositions (design it in). **Horizon:** hourly–daily detection, continuous.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Thousands–millions of metric-slice combinations evaluated continuously; human attention budget of a handful of alerts/day per owner — precision is the entire game (the SOC alert-fatigue problem transplanted to business data).
7. **Data exhaust** — The metrics warehouse itself (the semantic layer), dimensional hierarchies, calendar/promo context, alert-response dispositions. Systems of record: **data warehouse/lakehouse (Snowflake/Databricks/BigQuery), BI semantic layers, incident tools (Slack/Jira)**. Prerequisite exhaust = trustworthy metrics definitions — which is exactly what immature data teams lack.
8. **Actionability** — Alert → routed notification to the metric owner with the offending slice + suspected timing/change correlation → owner investigates/fixes (pricing rollback, integration fix, campaign stop). Actionability depends wholly on ownership mapping — an alert without an owner is noise.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: detection lead time vs previous discovery (often weeks → hours), alert precision (% actioned), incident-duration reduction, recovered-loss estimates per catch. Provable by incident log comparison before/after; each caught incident is a self-documenting value story.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Semantic-layer maturity (agreed metric definitions), dimensional completeness, seasonality/promo calendars for baseline models, ownership registry. Technically straightforward (time-series anomaly detection is commodity); **organizationally gated by data-platform maturity** — feasibility tracks warehouse quality. Vendor category exists (Anodot-class business monitoring).
11. **Industry variants** — **E-commerce/marketplaces:** funnel/payment slices — highest natural fit. **Fintech/payments:** authorization-rate monitoring borders on ops-critical (H2 overlap). **Retail:** store/category sales anomalies. **CPG/manufacturing:** slower cadences, monthly data limits detection speed. **Telco:** revenue-assurance lineage (a pre-AI ancestor of this use case).
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Driver analysis (K2 — the "why" after the "what"), AIOps (H2 — same math on IT telemetry), fraud detection (adversarial anomalies), revenue assurance (telecom-specific ancestor). Boundary: K1 detects the movement; K2 attributes it.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Widely aspirational, moderately deployed: BI vendors ship anomaly features (Power BI/Looker-class), a specialist vendor niche exists, but disciplined business-monitoring programs with routed ownership remain a minority practice (survey evidence thin — prevalence claim is qualitative). Rising with lakehouse/semantic-layer maturity.
14. **Documented results** — Public quantified case studies are scarce; documented value is mostly vendor-reported catches (e.g., pricing/checkout incidents caught in hours — Anodot-class case studies; not independently audited). This is an **evidence-thin, mechanism-strong** use case: flagged as such in Synthesis 2 — its economics are incident arithmetic, not survey-sized pools.
15. **Failure modes** — Alert fatigue from naive thresholds across thousands of slices (precision collapse); no owner routing (alerts into a channel nobody reads); metric definitions disputed so alerts litigate definitions instead of incidents; seasonality/promos generating false alarms until calendars are modeled; detection without disposition capture, so the system never learns; treating it as a data-science project rather than an operational program with an on-call analog.
16. **Typical methodology** — Hierarchical time-series baselines + multi-dimensional contribution/outlier scanning + routing rules; LLM-generated alert narratives; disposition feedback loop.
17. **Key references** — Business-monitoring vendor literature (Anodot-class — vendor); telecom revenue-assurance practice (ancestor discipline); time-series anomaly-detection research; semantic-layer/metrics-layer practitioner writing (2022–2025).

### K2. Driver analysis ("why did the metric move") — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Driver analysis; metric decomposition; contribution analysis; root-cause analytics (business); variance analysis (finance vocabulary); "why" analytics; diagnostic analytics.
2. **Problem statement** — When a KPI moves — revenue down 4%, churn up, margin compressed — leadership needs to know *why*, decomposed into concrete, actionable drivers, within days; instead, analyst teams burn weeks on ad-hoc slicing, and different teams return contradictory answers. The business wants fast, consistent, quantified attribution of metric movements.
3. **Who has it** — Every enterprise with executive reporting; acute in FP&A (variance analysis every close), growth teams (funnel diagnostics), retail merchandising, telco base management. Lives in **analytics/FP&A**; owned by CFO (variance), CDO/analytics leaders (general).
4. **Economic pool** — The pool is decision speed and analyst labor: ad-hoc diagnostic requests consume a large share of enterprise analyst capacity (commonly the plurality of BI team workload — practitioner consensus; not survey-verified), and slow/wrong diagnosis delays corrective action on the underlying P&L movement (value = the K1 incident pool's second half). No analyst-firm sizing exists (stated qualitatively).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the metric movement (period-over-period delta of KPI X). **Decision:** which drivers explain it and what corrective action to take. **Label:** validated decompositions (do the parts sum? does the flagged driver survive scrutiny?) — soft labels; correctness is auditability + analyst confirmation. **Horizon:** per reporting cycle (daily–monthly) and on demand.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Every close, every business review, every anomaly from K1 spawns one; a large enterprise runs hundreds of diagnostic cycles/month across functions. Value concentrates in the recurring, templatable decompositions (mix vs rate vs volume) that consume the most analyst hours.
7. **Data exhaust** — The same warehouse/semantic layer as K1, plus driver trees (finance), experiment logs, change calendars (pricing, promo, release). Systems of record: **warehouse/BI, FP&A tools, experimentation platforms**. Exhaust sufficiency mirrors K1: it's a data-model-maturity problem, not a data-existence problem.
8. **Actionability** — Output is a quantified driver report ("−4% revenue = −2.5pp Germany payment-failure + −1.2pp category mix + …") feeding directly into corrective decisions and the narrative for K-suite/board reporting; wired into K1 alerts it becomes detect→diagnose→act. Actionability is high but human-mediated (decisions, not automated actions).
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: time-to-diagnosis (weeks→hours), analyst hours per request, decomposition auditability (parts sum to whole), corrective-action lead time. Measurable as an operations improvement on the analytics function itself; P&L attribution is indirect (via faster correction).
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Semantic layer + dimensional discipline (same as K1); driver-tree definitions (finance's existing variance methodology is the template); change calendars for candidate causes. Highly feasible with current tooling (contribution algorithms + LLM narrative are commodity); correctness discipline (avoiding spurious attribution) is the craft. This use case is the natural habitat of the tabular methodology's *explanation* stage (model → SHAP → report) applied to business aggregates.
11. **Industry variants** — **FP&A:** rate/volume/mix variance — formalized methodology exists. **Retail:** comp-sales decomposition (traffic × conversion × basket). **Telco:** ARPU/base waterfalls. **SaaS:** ARR bridge (new/expansion/contraction/churn) — already a standard artifact, ripe for automation. **CPG:** share decomposition vs category.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — K1 (detection upstream), churn/pricing driver analyses (A-family per-model SHAP is micro-scale driver analysis), MMM (C1 — causal attribution of one driver class), reporting automation (K-compact — the narrative wrapper). Boundary: K2 attributes observed movements; MMM estimates causal effects of controllable spend.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Universally performed manually; automated/AI-assisted versions emerging via BI "key influencers"/contribution features and gen-BI copilots (2023–2026). Gen-AI "chat with your data" adoption is broad at the tool level; *trusted, auditable* automated driver analysis remains scarce (hallucination + metric-trust issues). Prevalence claim qualitative — survey data thin.
14. **Documented results** — Public quantified outcomes are essentially absent (internal analytics improvements are rarely published); vendor claims of analyst-hour reductions exist (unverified). Marked openly: **mechanism-strong, evidence-thin** — its case rests on the ubiquity of the manual practice it accelerates, not on published deltas.
15. **Failure modes** — Spurious attribution (correlated dimensions credited causally); decompositions that don't reconcile to the total (credibility death); LLM narratives confidently wrong about arithmetic (the gen-BI trust problem); metric-definition disputes re-litigated per analysis; automation of the easy decomposition while the hard causal questions (why did Germany's payment failures rise?) still need humans — scope honesty required.
16. **Typical methodology** — Algorithmic contribution decomposition (mix/rate/volume, multi-dim outlier contribution) + change-point correlation with event calendars + LLM-drafted narrative with numeric grounding checks.
17. **Key references** — FP&A variance-analysis methodology (practitioner standard); BI vendor key-influencer/contribution documentation; gen-BI copilot evaluations (2024–2026 practitioner writing); SaaS ARR-bridge reporting conventions.

## L. Industry-specific families

25 use cases across 8 industries. Full dossiers (healthcare, the deepest pool): **claims denials management**, **prior authorization**, **clinical documentation**. Compact tables per industry for the rest.

### L1. Healthcare — claims denials management & prevention — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Denials management; denial prediction & prevention; revenue-cycle denials analytics; claim-scrubbing intelligence; appeal prioritization.
2. **Problem statement** — Payers initially deny a substantial share of provider claims — often for predictable, preventable reasons (eligibility, coding, authorization, documentation) — forcing expensive rework and appeals, delaying cash, and writing off legitimate revenue. Providers want denials predicted and prevented before submission, and denied claims triaged for recovery.
3. **Who has it** — Hospitals/health systems, physician groups, billing companies (US-centric; analogs wherever claims-based reimbursement exists). Lives in **revenue cycle management (RCM)**; owned by VP revenue cycle / CFO.
4. **Economic pool** — Average initial denial rate ~**12%** of hospital claims (1H 2022; up from 9% in 2016 — Change Healthcare Denials Index via CAQH CORE, 2023); Medicare Advantage 15.7%, commercial 13.9% (AHA-cited, 2022); ACA marketplace in-network denials 19% (KFF, 2024). Rework costs **$25–118 per denied claim** ($63.76 commercial, $47.77 MA — HFMA). Hospitals spent **$19.7B in 2022** fighting denials (AHA, 2024); claims adjudication costs providers ~**$25.7B/year** (industry synthesis of CAQH-type data).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the claim (pre-submission) / the denial (post). **Decision:** fix-before-submit flags; appeal vs write-off vs auto-appeal; root-cause programs by denial category. **Label:** payer adjudication outcomes (denial codes, appeal results) — complete, coded, automatic. **Horizon:** pre-submission (days), appeal windows (30–180 days); continuous flow.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — A mid-size health system submits tens of thousands of claims/month; every claim is a prediction opportunity, every denial a triage decision. Value concentrates in high-dollar inpatient claims and in systematic root causes (one payer-rule change can drive thousands of denials).
7. **Data exhaust** — Claims (837) and remittance (835) transactions with standardized denial codes (CARC/RARC), payer rules history, prior-auth records, clinical documentation, appeal outcomes. Systems of record: **EHR revenue-cycle modules (Epic/Cerner), clearinghouses, RCM platforms**. Exhaust is exceptional: standardized transaction formats + coded outcomes — among the best-labeled problems in healthcare.
8. **Actionability** — Pre-submission edits/flags in the billing workflow; appeal work-queues ranked by recoverable value × win probability; LLM-drafted appeal letters with clinical citations; root-cause reports driving upstream fixes (registration, coding, auth processes). Fully wired through existing RCM workflow.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: initial denial rate, overturn/appeal win rate, cost to collect, days in A/R, write-offs. Hard financial metrics tracked monthly by every provider; before/after and payer-segment comparisons standard. Excellent measurability.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — 835/837 history (universal), payer-rule tracking, RCM workflow integration, appeal-letter clinical grounding (LLM + chart access). High feasibility — a mature and fast-growing RCM-AI vendor market; the labeled-data situation makes this healthcare's most tractable AI problem, in contrast to clinical prediction.
11. **Industry variants** — (Within healthcare) hospital vs physician-group vs post-acute denial mixes differ; payer-side mirror-image use case (auto-adjudication/payment integrity) sits across the table — the same AI arms race from the other side, raising escalation dynamics (payer AI denying, provider AI appealing — documented 2023–2025 controversy including litigation over payer algorithmic denials).
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Prior authorization (L2 — the largest single denial root cause), medical coding automation (upstream accuracy), payment-integrity/fraud (payer side, E-family), collections (patient-responsibility AR, D2 variant). Boundary: denials management reacts to and prevents payer rejections; prior auth is the specific pre-approval workflow.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — RCM automation is among the most funded healthcare-AI segments; denial prediction/appeal drafting broadly deployed at large systems and via RCM outsourcers (industry surveys; specific penetration not source-verified). Payer-side auto-adjudication is universal.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Pool/benchmark figures above (Change/CAQH 2023, AHA 2024, HFMA, KFF 2024 — measured industry data). (b) Vendor/system-reported: denial-rate reductions of 20–40% and multi-million-dollar recovery via AI work-queues + LLM appeals (RCM vendor case studies, 2023–2026 — not independently audited; flagged). (c) The measured *problem* data here is exceptionally strong; measured *solution* deltas are mostly self-reported (noted).
15. **Failure modes** — Whack-a-mole with payer rule changes (adversarial-ish drift); fixing appeals while leaving root causes (registration/auth errors) unfixed; appeal automation flooding payers → payer counter-automation (escalation without net gain); write-off thresholds hiding recoverable value; clinical-documentation gaps that no billing-side model can fix; compliance risk if appeal letters overstate clinical facts (LLM hallucination in a regulated document).
16. **Typical methodology** — Tabular denial-risk GBM on claim/payer features + denial-code root-cause analytics + LLM appeal-letter generation grounded in chart/policy citations.
17. **Key references** — CAQH CORE claims issue brief (2023); AHA denials market scan (2024); KFF marketplace denials analysis (2024); HFMA rework-cost research; CAQH Index (admin-transaction costs).

### L2. Healthcare — prior authorization automation — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Prior authorization (PA); pre-authorization/pre-certification; utilization-management automation (payer side); auth determination & submission automation (provider side); "gold-carding" analytics.
2. **Problem statement** — Payers require pre-approval for many services and drugs; the process is manual, fax/portal-based, and slow on both sides — delaying care, consuming enormous clinical and administrative time, and generating denials when skipped or botched. Both sides want determinations made fast, consistently, and with documentation assembled automatically.
3. **Who has it** — Providers (all), payers (UM departments), pharmacy/PBMs; US-centric with analogs in managed-care systems. Lives in **patient access/revenue cycle (provider)** and **utilization management (payer)**; owned by VP patient access / CMO-UM respectively.
4. **Economic pool** — US PA administrative spend exceeds **$13B/year** (CAQH-cited, 2023); physicians report ~**2 business days/week (~13–16 hours)** of physician+staff time on PA, ~29 requests/physician/week, >80% describing burden as high (AMA PA surveys, 2017–2023 — survey-measured; hour figures widely cited from AMA waves). Per-transaction cost: ~$40–50 payer side, $20–30 provider side for manual PAs (JAMA-adjacent admin-cost analyses). Care-delay costs (abandonment, adverse events) sit on top — AMA surveys report majority of physicians seeing care abandonment due to PA (survey-measured).
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the authorization request (patient × service/drug × payer policy). **Decision:** provider side — is auth required, assemble evidence, submit; payer side — approve/pend/deny against medical policy. **Label:** determination outcomes + appeal reversals — complete and coded. **Horizon:** hours–days (statutory clocks tightening: CMS interoperability rule requires 72h urgent/7d standard by 2026).
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Hundreds of millions of PA transactions/year across the US system (CAQH Index scale); a large health system processes thousands/week. Value concentrates in high-volume imaging/specialty-drug categories and in the auto-approvable majority (payer side: most requests are ultimately approved — automating the obvious approvals is the win).
7. **Data exhaust** — PA request/outcome history, payer medical policies (documents), clinical data (EHR), X12 278 transactions, appeal outcomes. Systems of record: **EHR, payer UM systems, clearinghouses; FHIR APIs arriving under CMS-0057 (2024 rule, 2026–2027 deadlines)**. Historically fax-shaped exhaust; the regulatory API mandate is re-platforming the whole use case.
8. **Actionability** — Provider: auto-determination of auth requirements, evidence-package assembly from the chart (LLM), electronic submission, status tracking. Payer: auto-approval rules/models for clear cases, clinical-review routing for the rest, gold-carding (exempting high-approval-rate providers). Actions run through EHR work queues and UM platforms — both sides have native workflow.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: turnaround time, auto-approval rate, PA-related denial rate, staff hours per auth, care-delay/abandonment measures, appeal overturn rate (CMS now requires payer reporting of PA metrics — public measurability arriving). Strong and improving measurability.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Chart access + policy digitization (LLM-readable medical policies); FHIR/278 connectivity; payer-side model governance (regulatory scrutiny of algorithmic denials is intense — 2023–2025 litigation and CMS guidance that MA determinations can't rely solely on algorithms). Feasibility high on the provider side (assembly/submission automation); payer-side full automation is **politically/legally constrained by design** — approve-only automation is the safe pattern.
11. **Industry variants** — Medical vs pharmacy PA (PBM ecosystems differ); Medicare Advantage under specific CMS rules; Medicaid state variation; imaging/oncology/specialty-drug categories each have benefit-manager sub-industries.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Denials management (L1 — auth failures are a top denial cause), clinical documentation (L3 — the evidence source), care-gap identification (utilization's clinical mirror), payment integrity (payer). Boundary: PA is pre-service permission; denials management is post-claim recovery.
13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Payer-side auto-adjudication of clear approvals is mainstream; provider-side automation vendors scaled rapidly 2022–2026; the CMS interoperability mandate (2024 rule) is forcing adoption industry-wide on a regulatory clock — prevalence is rising by construction.
14. **Documented results** — (a) Pool figures: >$13B admin spend (CAQH 2023), AMA burden surveys (2017–2023) — measured. (b) CMS rule projects ~$15B ten-year savings from ePA (CMS regulatory impact analysis, 2024 — projection). (c) Vendor/system-reported: 50–80% reductions in auth turnaround and substantial FTE redeployment (2023–2026 — self-reported; flagged). (d) Payer algorithmic-denial controversies (UnitedHealth/nH Predicate litigation reporting, 2023 — cautionary documentation).
15. **Failure modes** — Payer-side over-automation of denials (legal/regulatory blowback — the sector's defining cautionary tale); provider evidence packages that game rather than inform (arms-race dynamics); policy-document drift (payer policies change monthly); integration debt (fax/portal long tail persisting past mandates); automating requests that shouldn't exist (gold-carding/policy simplification beats faster paperwork — the deepest fix is process removal, not process AI).
16. **Typical methodology** — Requirement-determination rules + LLM evidence extraction/assembly from EHR + policy-matching (RAG over medical policies) + X12/FHIR submission automation; payer side: approve-only decision models + review routing.
17. **Key references** — CAQH Index PA costs (2023); AMA prior-authorization physician surveys (2017–2023); CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization final rule CMS-0057-F (2024); reporting on MA algorithmic-denial litigation (2023–2024).

### L3. Healthcare — clinical documentation (ambient AI scribes) — FULL DOSSIER

1. **Name + aliases** — Ambient clinical documentation; AI medical scribes; ambient listening; note generation; clinical documentation improvement (CDI — the adjacent coding-quality discipline).
2. **Problem statement** — Clinicians spend hours per day writing visit notes and doing EHR clerical work — after-hours "pajama time" — driving burnout, reducing patient-facing capacity, and degrading documentation quality. The business wants the note produced from the conversation itself, with the clinician editing rather than authoring.
3. **Who has it** — Health systems, medical groups, telehealth providers; every specialty with encounter documentation (primary care and ED highest volume). Lives in **clinical operations/informatics**; owned by CMIO/CMO with CFO interest (throughput) and CHRO interest (burnout/retention).
4. **Economic pool** — Physicians report ~**7.3 hours/week on administrative tasks** within a 57.8-hour week (2024 physician survey) and time-motion studies have long shown ~2 hours of EHR/desk work per hour of direct care (Sinsky et al., Annals of Internal Medicine, 2016 — measured; widely replicated). Pool = clinician time (the most expensive labor in the building), burnout-driven turnover (physician replacement costs commonly cited at $500k–1M+ — industry estimates; not source-verified), and visit-capacity upside.
5. **Decision framing** — **Entity:** the clinical encounter. **Decision:** what the note (and orders/codes suggestions) should say — clinician signs. **Label:** clinician edits to drafts (rich, continuous), documentation-quality audits, coding outcomes. **Horizon:** per encounter, real time or same-day.
6. **Decision frequency and scale** — Every ambulatory encounter (a large system: millions/year). Value concentrates in high-encounter-volume specialties and among clinicians with worst documentation burden (adoption is heterogeneous by style).
7. **Data exhaust** — Encounter audio (with consent), EHR structured context, historical notes, edit deltas on drafts, coding/billing outcomes. Systems of record: **EHR (Epic — with native ambient integrations), ambient-AI platforms (Nuance DAX/Abridge-class)**. The audio exhaust didn't exist until the use case created it — a case where the AI generates its own data channel.
8. **Actionability** — Draft note lands in the EHR for signature; downstream suggestions (codes, orders, follow-ups) route through existing EHR workflow. Action = clinician sign-off; adoption is voluntary and satisfaction-driven — which has been unusually strong for this category.
9. **Measurability** — KPIs: documentation time per note, after-hours EHR time ("pajama time" — measurable from EHR audit logs, a gift for evaluation), note quality audits, burnout scores, visit volume. EHR audit-log measurement makes this one of the most *instrumentable* gen-AI use cases; health systems have published evaluations.
10. **Prerequisites & feasibility** — Consent workflows; EHR integration; specialty-specific validation; medico-legal review of documentation standards. High feasibility — the breakout healthcare gen-AI use case of 2023–2026, with large-scale deployments (Kaiser Permanente's rollout among the largest — deployment documented; specific outcome figures not source-verified here).
11. **Industry variants** — Specialty variation (procedural vs cognitive notes); ED (team, interrupted encounters — harder); nursing documentation (the larger labor pool, next frontier); veterinary/dental analogs; non-US languages/coding regimes.
12. **Adjacent/overlapping use cases** — Medical coding automation (CDI/coding from the note), denials/PA evidence assembly (L1/L2 consume documentation), patient-message drafting (in-basket burden — sibling use case with published RCT-ish evaluations), summarization-to-structured-records (J-compact generic). 13. **Prevalence & adoption** — Fastest-scaling clinical AI category: from pilots (2023) to tens of thousands of clinicians at major systems (2024–2026), with published health-system evaluations (JAMA/NEJM-adjacent, 2024–2025) showing time savings and burnout improvements — unusual publication discipline for an AI category (specific effect sizes vary by study; typical findings: significant reductions in after-hours EHR time and documentation burden, 20–50% ranges in early evaluations — study-reported ranges; heterogeneous).
14. **Documented results** — (a) Published health-system evaluations (2024–2025): reduced documentation time and after-hours EHR time, improved clinician-experience scores (peer-reviewed/system-published; effect sizes heterogeneous — study-measured). (b) Sinsky et al. 2016 time-motion baseline (measured). (c) Kaiser Permanente-scale deployments (documented rollouts; outcome figures not re-verified here — stated qualitatively). (d) Vendor-reported per-note time savings of minutes/note (vendor; flagged).
15. **Failure modes** — Hallucinated/omitted clinical facts surviving cursory sign-off (patient-safety and legal exposure — edit-vigilance decay is the real risk); note bloat (fluent but low-signal notes degrading the record); billing-compliance drift if drafts upcode; consent/privacy friction; ROI framed as visit-volume increase that clinicians experience as speed-up pressure (burnout benefit destroyed); specialty edge cases (accents, interruptions, multi-party encounters).
16. **Typical methodology** — Ambient ASR + LLM note generation constrained to encounter evidence + EHR-context grounding + clinician edit loop (edit data as continuous evaluation).
17. **Key references** — Sinsky et al., Annals of Internal Medicine time-motion study (2016); AMA administrative-burden surveys; health-system ambient-AI evaluations (2024–2025); Nuance DAX/Abridge deployment reporting; ONC/CMS documentation-burden reduction initiatives.

A3 recsys (identity + experimentation infra) · F1/F2 forecasting & inventory (master-data + planner adoption) · A2 pricing (elasticity needs price variation; B2B needs sales compliance) · E1 fraud (buy for most; real-time infra if building) · B2 resolution automation (KB curation + guardrails + brand risk) · F3 routing (buy; driver adoption) · D3 credit risk (modeling easy; governance heavy) · L2 prior auth (integration + policy digitization; regulatory tailwind) · L3 ambient documentation (consent + EHR integration; vendor-mature) · H1/H2 SOC/AIOps augmentation (vendor-mature; disposition hygiene) · J1 contract review (repository assembly + playbook codification) · G2 visual inspection (camera/lighting engineering; defect libraries) · L10 claims triage (document AI + workflow) · I6 scheduling (WFM vendor-mature; labor-relations care) · C2 content generation (brand/claims constraints + review workflow).

### Tier F3 — Hard: data acquisition, causal validity, label scarcity, or governance dominate

G1 PdM (sensors + scarce failure labels + heterogeneous fleets) · G3 yield optimization (contextualization + observational causality + change control) · C1 MMM (causal identification; expertise-dependent) · E2 AML beyond triage (regulatory validation cycles) · A6 NBA at full scope (orchestration + uplift + arbitration = program, not project) · K1/K2 at scale (semantic-layer maturity prerequisite) · I1 attrition (ethics/works councils; small N) · I2 screening/ranking (bias audits, EU AI Act/LL144) · G8 digital twins (model fidelity economics) · F6 supplier risk prediction (rare events, weak labels) · L5 readmission-style clinical prediction (intervention capacity + equity validation) · D10/K3 scenario modeling (unfalsifiable without discipline) · L22 public-sector case prioritization (highest governance stakes — Robodebt/Toeslagenaffaire lessons) · H8 release-risk (needs DORA-grade engineering culture first).

**The recurring pattern:** feasibility is set less by modeling difficulty than by (1) whether operations already generate labels as a by-product, (2) whether a system of action already exists to consume decisions, and (3) how regulated/politically sensitive the decision is. The tabular-with-natural-labels + native-action-path quadrant (B/D/L1-type back-office flows) is systematically the most attackable — and systematically less hyped than the hard quadrant.

## Synthesis 6 — Prevalence reality-check

What is actually deployed at scale vs piloted vs talked about, against survey evidence. Context anchors: 88% of organizations use AI in ≥1 function but only ~⅓ scaling and 7% fully scaled (McKinsey State of AI 2025); 71% regularly use gen AI in ≥1 function (McKinsey 2025); ~48% of AI projects reach production, averaging 8 months prototype→production (Gartner 2024); only 25% of orgs have moved ≥40% of AI experiments into production (Deloitte State of AI 2026); ~30% of gen-AI pilots reach full production (Deloitte 2025); function-level gen-AI adoption: IT/cyber 46%, marketing/sales/service 41%, product/R&D 41%, finance 25%, HR 23%, legal/risk 21% (Deloitte 2024).

### Deployed at scale (production-normal in their industries)

- **E1 transaction fraud** — universal in payments for decades; the reference production-ML domain.
- **D3 credit scoring** — universal, regulator-institutionalized (champion/challenger is standard practice).
- **A3 recommendations/personalization** — at-scale wherever digital commerce/media is the business; platform features downstream.
- **F1/F2 forecasting & replenishment** — planning-system-native across retail/CPG/manufacturing (quality varies; presence doesn't).
- **F3 routing / L17 dispatch** — parcel/field-service standard (ORION-era proof).
- **B1/B2/B3 service triage/automation/assist** — the gen-AI era's flagship deployments; service ops top-3 adopting function every McKinsey wave 2023–2025.
- **H3 coding assistants** — fastest seat-level diffusion in enterprise software history (majority-of-F500 penetration, GitHub-reported 2023–2024); *measured impact programs* much rarer than seats.
- **D1/J2 document processing** — decades of capture installed base; LLM refresh underway.
- **E5/E8 KYC & sanctions screening** — mandated, vendor-saturated.
- **G2 machine-vision inspection** — decades in electronics; deep-learning generation mainstream in auto.
- **L16 telecom network automation, L18 load forecasting** — operator/utility core practice.
- **C2 content generation (tool level)** — highest gen-AI usage anywhere (marketing #1–2 function, McKinsey 2024–2025); *systematic content ops* far thinner than tool usage.

### Widely piloted / unevenly scaled (the pilot-purgatory belt)

- **G1 predictive maintenance** — the canonical pilot-to-scale gap: one asset class works, fleet-wide stalls (industrial-AI survey pattern; Gartner/Deloitte production-rate stats above are the quantitative backdrop).
- **A1 churn programs** — models common; closed-loop intervention programs with holdouts much rarer.
- **A6 NBA** — decisioning suites owned by many telcos/banks; full orchestration realized by few.
- **E2 AML ML** — triage overlays spreading; core-monitoring replacement slow (governance).
- **G3 yield optimization** — lighthouse factories vs the long tail (WEF/McKinsey Lighthouse ~150+ exemplars against thousands of plants).
- **A2 B2B pricing AI** — active frontier (McKinsey 2024–2025 positioning); sales-adoption friction gates scale.
- **L2 prior auth automation** — regulatory clock (CMS-0057, 2026–2027) is converting pilots to mandates.
- **L3 ambient documentation** — scaling unusually fast for healthcare (tens of thousands of clinicians, 2024–2026) — likely to graduate to "at scale" within a year or two.
- **I2 recruiting automation** — scheduling/chat scaled in high-volume hiring; ranking/screening deployment throttled by bias-audit regimes.
- **H2 AIOps** — correlation/noise-reduction deployed broadly; "predictive ops" mostly aspirational.
- **C1 MMM** — renaissance in progress post-cookie; rigorous practice still concentrated in large advertisers.
- **K4 reporting automation / J6 summarization / K6 research synthesis** — spreading fast informally; institutionalized versions early.

### Mostly talked about (deployment rhetoric exceeds production reality)

- **G8 digital twins** — "twin" usually means dashboard/simulation pocket; enterprise-wide twins are rare.
- **D10/K3 AI scenario planning** — conference-stage prevalence, spreadsheet reality.
- **A5 CLV-driven management** — computed often, *decisioned on* rarely.
- **I5 skills-graph transformation** — vendor-narrative-heavy; validated skills data scarce.
- **F12 autonomous S&OP / "self-driving supply chain"** — vendor language well ahead of practice.
- **K1/K2 autonomous business monitoring & diagnosis** — real vendor niche, but the "AI CFO/analyst" framing far exceeds deployments; gated on semantic-layer maturity.
- **Agentic everything (2025–2026)** — 62% of orgs experimenting with agents, 23% scaling any, ≤10% scaled in any single function (McKinsey 2025); Gartner predicts >40% of agentic-AI projects canceled by end-2027 (Gartner 2025 — projection). The gap between agent discourse and agent production is today's widest rhetoric-reality spread.
- **Fully autonomous decision loops in regulated domains** (credit, claims denial, benefits, hiring) — legally constrained by design; human-in-loop remains the deployed reality (and cautionary tales — Robodebt (2023 royal commission), Dutch Toeslagenaffaire (2021), MA algorithmic-denial litigation (2023) — keep it that way).

## Synthesis 7 — Failure-pattern synthesis

The recurring reasons enterprise AI use cases fail, collected once. Backdrop rates: ≥30% of gen-AI projects predicted abandoned after PoC by end-2025 — causes: poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs, unclear business value (Gartner, July 2024); ~95% of GenAI pilots with no measurable P&L impact in ~6 months — core barrier framed as the "learning gap": systems that don't retain feedback or fit workflows (MIT Project NANDA, 2025 — preliminary, small-sample, 6-month window; methodology criticized on sampling and measurement-window grounds); ~80% of AI projects failing to achieve objectives per RAND-attributed analyses (2024, secondary-cited); historical anchors: "85% of AI projects deliver erroneous outcomes" (Gartner prediction, 2018) and "87% never reach production" (2019 survey lore) — both forecasts/survey figures, not measurements.

| # | Failure pattern | Mechanism | Most susceptible use cases |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **No action path** — prediction lands nowhere | Score produced, no owner/budget/workflow consumes it; "insight" ends as a dashboard | A1 churn, I1 attrition, A5 CLV, K1/K2 executive analytics, F6 supplier risk, L5 readmission (no intervention capacity) |
| 2 | **Label leakage & label politics** — the model learns the answer or the bias | Post-outcome fields in features; labels encoding past human bias; self-fulfilling routing | A1/A4 (leakage classics), I2/I4 (bias absorption — Amazon 2018), E3/L22 (investigation-biased labels), A7 (stage-gamed CRM) |
| 3 | **Measurement never designed** — impact unprovable, program dies at budget review | No holdout/AB; before/after confounded; activity metrics (scores served, content produced) reported as value | C2 content gen, B2 deflection theater, A4 lead scoring, D2 collections, most vendor-claimed categories; the NANDA 95% is substantially this pattern |
| 4 | **Data foundation debt** — the model is easy, the join is hard | Identity/master-data/semantic-layer gaps starve the use case; inventory/CMDB/repository records don't match reality | A3/A6 (identity), F2 (phantom inventory), H2 (CMDB), K1/K2 (metric definitions), J1 (repository chaos), G3 (contextualization) |
| 5 | **Causal confusion** — correlational answers to causal questions | Observational elasticities/attributions confounded; optimization on spurious drivers | C1 MMM, A2 pricing (flat-price history), A9 promo, G3 yield, K2 driver analysis, MMM-shaped anything |
| 6 | **Adversarial & regime drift** — the world updates against the model | Fraudsters adapt in days; payers/patterns/macro shift; yesterday's taxonomy | E1–E4, L1 (payer rules), H1/H10 (attackers), D3 (macro vintages), B1 (taxonomy drift), F1 (demand shocks) |
| 7 | **Alert-fatigue economics** — precision too low for the attention budget | High-volume flags overwhelm scarce human responders; trust collapses; alerts ignored (74%-of-breaches-had-ignored-alerts pattern, DBIR-cited 2024) | E2 AML (>90–95% FP baseline), H1 SOC, G1 PdM alarms, K1 metric alerts, D7 JE anomalies, L19 grid anomalies |
| 8 | **Human-adoption failure** — the people the model serves route around it | Reps/planners/drivers/adjusters/clinicians override or ignore; incentives and trust unaddressed; surveillance framing | A2 B2B pricing, F1 planner overrides, F3 driver compliance, B3 agent assist, L3 sign-off vigilance, I-family generally |
| 9 | **Governance/compliance stall or blowback** — legal reality arrives late | Fair-lending/bias/privacy/works-council/professional-responsibility constraints discovered after build; or automation harms trigger scandal | D3/E6 (fairness), I1/I2 (EU AI Act, LL144), L2/L21/L22 (algorithmic-denial litigation, Robodebt, Toeslagenaffaire), B2 (hallucination liability — Air Canada 2024), J1 (fabricated citations) |
| 10 | **Unit-economics inversion** — the solution costs more than the problem | Sensoring/licensing/inference/review costs exceed the pool; long-tail exceptions eat the savings; headcount "savings" never redeployed | G1 (sensor cost on non-critical assets), J2/D1 (long-tail documents), B2 (hard-40% still sets staffing floor), H3 (seat cost vs realized value), C2 (review bottleneck relocation) |
| 11 | **Pilot-shaped success** — works in the pilot's conditions only | One asset class/line/site/segment; heroic data prep unreproducible; scaling assumptions untested (the Gartner PoC-abandonment causes live here) | G1/G3 (asset/line heterogeneity), A6 NBA, L-family clinical prediction, G8 twins, agentic projects broadly (Gartner 2025: >40% cancellation prediction) |
| 12 | **Optimizing the symptom** — the use case automates around a fixable root cause | Faster appeals instead of fewer denials; collections on billing errors; triage of contacts a product fix would eliminate; PA acceleration where PA removal (gold-carding) is the answer | L1/L2, D2 (dispute root causes), B1/B2 (contact-reason elimination), D5 exceptions, F7 leakage (vs contract discipline) |

**The compressed lesson across all twelve:** enterprise AI use cases fail overwhelmingly at the *problem-and-plumbing* layer — labels, joins, actions, incentives, measurement, governance — and only rarely at the model layer. Which is exactly why this compendium catalogs problems, their data exhaust, their action paths, and their measurability rather than methods: the four fields that predict success (7 data exhaust, 8 actionability, 9 measurability, 10 prerequisites) are the ones that vary; the methodology (16) barely does — a handful of method families, led by the tabular pipeline, serve all 124 problems.

*End of compendium.*
