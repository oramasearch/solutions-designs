## Executive orientation

**What a methodology is.** Per the governing vocabulary, a *methodology* is a reusable arrangement of AI methods — a pipeline or graph of components (extract → prepare → feature-engineer → train → explain → evaluate → report/serve → monitor) described independently of any single business use case or deployment. A *use case* is the problem (churn, fraud, demand); a *solution* is a methodology applied correctly for real conditions and wired to the business action. This compendium catalogs only the middle layer.

**The reuse thesis.** The three layers are deliberately asymmetric: many use cases ≈ many solutions ≫ few methodologies. This document enumerates 35 methodologies; the evidence catalogs collected alongside it enumerate hundreds of use cases (Databricks alone publishes 93 solution accelerators). The compression comes from component reuse: virtually every methodology below shares extraction, feature/representation engineering, evaluation, serving, and monitoring stages with its neighbors, and many differ only in one or two stages (e.g., learning-to-rank is tabular supervised learning with a group-aware loss and evaluation; uplift modeling is tabular supervised learning with a treatment column and a causal estimand). The component reuse matrix (S1) makes this explicit. For a platform, this means the leverage is in building the ~10 shared components extremely well, not in building per-use-case stacks.

**The prevalence headline.** The single most common enterprise ML methodology is the tabular supervised learning pipeline anchored by gradient-boosted trees (M1). The claim decomposes into two parts, both examined in detail in S3. (1) *Tabular data dominates enterprise ML workloads* — not by bytes (IDC estimates ~90% of data generated by organizations in 2022 was unstructured; Gartner puts 80–90% of *new* enterprise data as unstructured) but by decision workloads and spend: IDC's 2022 analysis found only ~40% of data-technology spend goes to unstructured-data projects versus 60% to structured, and the ML literature (Grinsztajn et al. 2022; Shwartz-Ziv & Armon 2022; Borisov et al. 2022; van Breugel & van der Schaar 2024) consistently characterizes structured tables as "the dominant working data format across machine learning practice." (2) *GBMs dominate tabular ML* — Kaggle's practitioner surveys have scikit-learn used by >80% of respondents with XGBoost the 4th most-used framework (Kaggle State of ML 2021, n≈25k), and in the M5 forecasting competition every one of the top-5 solutions used LightGBM (Makridakis et al. 2022). LLM-era methodologies (RAG at 51% of enterprise GenAI deployments per Menlo Ventures 2024) are the fastest-growing layer but sit beside, not instead of, the tabular core.

**How to read a dossier.** Every methodology entry completes the same 18 fields from the research prompt. Quantitative claims carry an inline source + year; where a number could not be grounded it is stated qualitatively or marked "(estimate; not source-verified)". Qualitative/technical content (pipelines, algorithms, pitfalls) is grounded in canonical papers and official library documentation cited by name and year.

# Part I — Methodology dossiers

## Family A — Tabular supervised learning

The anchor family. Everything else in this compendium either reuses its components or exists because some modality/problem shape breaks one of its assumptions.

<a id="m1"></a>
### M1. Gradient-boosted tabular supervised learning — *the anchor methodology*

1. **Name + aliases** — Tabular supervised learning with gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDT/GBM); "the XGBoost pipeline"; "classical ML"; "predictive modeling"; "propensity modeling" (when the target is a customer action); "scoring model". The canonical arrangement: **extract tabular data → feature engineering → train XGBoost → SHAP analysis → report.**
2. **One-sentence definition** — Learn a function from a fixed-width table of point-in-time features to a labeled outcome using an ensemble of gradient-boosted decision trees, explain the trained model's behavior with Shapley-value attributions, and deliver predictions plus drivers as a report or score feed.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Binary/multiclass classification, regression, and (with the right objective) ranking, quantile regression, count/Tweedie regression, and censored survival objectives. It is the default for any "predict a labeled outcome per entity" problem.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular (rows = entities or entity-periods, columns = features). Event streams, text, and even images enter *after* being aggregated or embedded into tabular columns — which is precisely why this methodology's feature-engineering stage is the reuse hub of the whole catalog.
5. **Component pipeline** —
   - *Extraction*: pull entity-level records and event history from warehouse/lake tables (SQL); define the population, the observation (snapshot) date, and the label window.
   - *Labeling*: compute the target per entity from future-window outcomes (e.g., "churned within 90 days of snapshot") — a design decision, not a lookup.
   - *Cleaning/validation*: type coercion, deduplication, missing-value accounting, schema and distribution checks (assertions on ranges, null rates, cardinalities).
   - *Feature engineering*: build point-in-time features from data strictly before the snapshot date (aggregations, recency/frequency/monetary, ratios, deltas, categorical encodings).
   - *Training*: gradient-boosted trees with early stopping on a validation split.
   - *Tuning*: hyperparameter search (learning rate, depth/leaves, subsampling, regularization) via random/Bayesian search with cross-validated or temporally held-out scoring.
   - *Explanation*: TreeSHAP global importance, dependence plots, and per-prediction local attributions.
   - *Evaluation*: discrimination (AUC/PR-AUC), calibration, lift/gains by decile, comparison against a naive and a current-champion baseline.
   - *Reporting/serving*: a driver report for stakeholders plus either batch score tables written back to the warehouse or a low-latency scoring service.
   - *Monitoring/retraining*: input drift, score drift, delayed-label performance tracking, scheduled or triggered retrains.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Training: **XGBoost** (Chen & Guestrin 2016) — the reference implementation, strongest ecosystem; **LightGBM** (Ke et al. 2017) — leaf-wise growth, typically fastest on large data, native categorical handling, dominant in competitions (all top-5 M5 solutions; Makridakis et al. 2022); **CatBoost** (Prokhorenkova et al. 2018) — ordered target-statistics encoding, best default behavior on high-cardinality categoricals, less tuning-sensitive. Prefer LightGBM for scale/speed, CatBoost for categorical-heavy data with minimal tuning, XGBoost for ecosystem maturity and exotic objectives. Fallbacks/complements: random forests (robust, low-tuning baseline), regularized logistic regression (M2) as an interpretable baseline that should always be run, tabular neural nets and TabPFN-class models (M4) when their conditions hold. On medium-sized tabular benchmarks, tuned tree ensembles remain state-of-the-art over deep learning (Grinsztajn et al. 2022, 45-dataset benchmark; Shwartz-Ziv & Armon 2022). Tuning: Optuna/Hyperopt-style Bayesian search; early stopping on a time-respecting validation set. Explanation: TreeSHAP (Lundberg, Erion & Lee 2020) for exact polynomial-time Shapley values on trees.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Windowed aggregations over event history (counts, sums, means, min/max over 7/30/90/365-day windows); recency features (days since last X); frequency and monetary aggregates (RFM); trend/delta features (30-day vs 90-day ratio); tenure and lifecycle-stage features; categorical encodings (native categorical support in LightGBM/CatBoost; target encoding **computed out-of-fold** to avoid leakage; one-hot only for low cardinality); count/frequency encoding; interaction ratios (spend per visit); missingness indicators (missingness is often informative); winsorization for heavy tails (trees are scale-invariant, so no normalization needed). The cardinal rule: every feature must be computable *as of the snapshot date* from data available at that time.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — This methodology's failure modes are the canonical ones for the whole catalog (collected in S5):
   - *Target leakage*: features that encode the outcome (e.g., "account_closed_reason" as a churn feature; post-outcome fields updated in place in the source system). Safeguard: feature audit against the label definition; suspiciously high AUC (> ~0.95 on a behavioral problem) triggers investigation; per-feature "would this exist at scoring time?" review.
   - *Temporal leakage*: features computed over windows overlapping the label window, or training rows sampled after evaluation rows. Safeguard: explicit snapshot-date architecture; point-in-time joins (feature-store style "as-of" joins); temporal train/validation/test splits.
   - *Train/serve skew*: training features built in SQL, serving features built in application code, silently diverging. Safeguard: single feature-definition source (feature store or shared transformation code); log-and-compare serving features against training distributions.
   - *Label-definition traps*: ambiguous churn definitions (voluntary vs involuntary), censoring (entities whose label window hasn't elapsed), label delay. Safeguard: written label spec; exclude censored entities or move to survival framing (M8).
   - *Survivorship bias*: building the training population only from currently active entities. Safeguard: reconstruct historical populations as-of past snapshot dates.
   - *Entity-resolution traps*: duplicate customers splitting one entity's history across rows (deflating features, double-counting labels). Safeguard: run entity resolution (M23) upstream; grouped splits by resolved entity.
   - *Class imbalance mishandling*: naive accuracy on 1% positives. Safeguard: PR-AUC, class weights or focal-style objectives, threshold selection on business costs — and recalibrate if training used resampling.
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Temporal out-of-time (OOT) test set as the primary gate (train on snapshots ≤ T, test on snapshot T+1); k-fold CV only within the training period, grouped by entity where entities repeat; metrics: ROC-AUC and PR-AUC for discrimination, Brier score + reliability curves for calibration, lift/gain charts and precision@k for the operational decision; always compare against (a) trivial baselines (base rate, "predict last state") and (b) the incumbent model/heuristic; report confidence intervals via bootstrap; check subgroup performance (segments, cohorts) for stability and fairness.
10. **Explainability methods** — TreeSHAP global feature importance (mean |SHAP|), SHAP summary (beeswarm) plots, SHAP dependence plots for the top drivers, and local per-entity attributions ("why this customer scored 0.87"); permutation importance as a cross-check; partial-dependence/ALE plots for shape; counterfactual "what would change the score" analyses for actioning. What stakeholders actually receive: a ranked driver list with direction, segment-level driver breakdowns, and per-record reason codes — the bridge from prediction to business action, and (in regulated credit) a compliance requirement for adverse-action reasons.
11. **Data requirements** — Workable from a few thousand labeled rows (below ~1k–10k, prefer M2/M4-TabPFN); comfortable at 10⁴–10⁸ rows; needs enough label history to cover at least a few label windows plus seasonality (typically ≥12 months of history for behavioral problems); labels must be derivable from recorded outcomes (no manual annotation usually needed — a key reason for this methodology's prevalence); feature freshness must match the decision cadence.
12. **Compute profile** — CPU-only for the vast majority of workloads; training minutes-to-hours on a single multi-core machine up to ~10⁸ rows (LightGBM/XGBoost both have optional GPU acceleration for the largest cases); inference sub-millisecond per row, trivially batchable; total cost typically orders of magnitude below deep-learning methodologies — a materially underrated selection factor.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — The most mature and most adopted methodology in the catalog. Evidence: Kaggle 2017 survey — logistic regression used at work by 63.5%, decision trees 49.9%, boosted trees 23.9% (n≈16k); Kaggle 2021 — scikit-learn >80% usage, XGBoost 4th most-used framework, with "gradient boosting machines and CNNs the most popular of more complex methods" (n≈25k); M5 competition — all top-5 solutions LightGBM-based, top entries >20% better WRMSSE than the best statistical benchmark (Makridakis et al. 2022); benchmark literature — tree ensembles state-of-the-art on medium tabular data (Grinsztajn et al. 2022). Deployed at essentially every enterprise doing ML; the default in credit risk, insurance pricing (with GLM constraints, M2), churn, fraud triage, lead scoring, and demand modeling.
14. **Reusable components** — Its extraction + point-in-time feature engineering stage is reused by M5–M8 (forecasting/survival), M11 (uplift), M12 (anomaly scoring), M14–M15 (ranking features), M22 (graph features append here), M26 (bandit context vectors), M29 (the "predict" in predict-then-optimize); its GBM training stage is reused by M6 (global forecasting), M11 (uplift meta-learners), M15 (LambdaMART), M22 (GNN-embedding + XGBoost hybrids, e.g. NVIDIA's fraud blueprint 2024); its SHAP explanation stage is reused by M5–M8, M11, M15; its evaluation/monitoring stages are the template for every supervised methodology here.
15. **Representative use cases** — Churn, credit default, fraud triage scoring, lead/opportunity scoring, propensity-to-buy, claim severity, delivery-delay prediction, employee attrition, predictive maintenance (with sensor aggregates), readmission risk.
16. **Production failure modes** — Covariate and concept drift (economy shifts, product changes); feedback loops (the model's own interventions suppress the outcomes it was trained on — treated customers stop churning, biasing retrains; mitigate with holdout control groups); silent feature-pipeline breakage (a upstream table stops updating, features go stale but scores keep flowing; mitigate with data-quality assertions and null-rate alarms); label delay masking degradation (track proxy metrics until labels mature); score-distribution drift as the earliest warning signal. Standard practice: drift monitors (PSI/KS on inputs and scores), delayed-label performance dashboards, scheduled retrains with champion/challenger evaluation, and a persistent random holdout for unbiased measurement.
17. **Tooling landscape** — XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, scikit-learn (pipelines, metrics, model selection); Optuna for tuning; SHAP library; pandas/Polars/DuckDB/Spark for extraction and features; feature stores (Feast, Tecton, or warehouse-native) for point-in-time correctness; MLflow for tracking/registry; Evidently/whylogs for drift; Great Expectations/dbt tests for data quality.
18. **Key references** — Chen & Guestrin, "XGBoost: A Scalable Tree Boosting System" (KDD 2016); Ke et al., "LightGBM" (NeurIPS 2017); Prokhorenkova et al., "CatBoost: unbiased boosting with categorical features" (NeurIPS 2018); Lundberg & Lee, "A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions" (NeurIPS 2017); Lundberg, Erion et al., "From local explanations to global understanding with explainable AI for trees" (Nature MI 2020); Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux, "Why do tree-based models still outperform deep learning on tabular data?" (NeurIPS D&B 2022); Shwartz-Ziv & Armon, "Tabular data: Deep learning is not all you need" (Information Fusion 2022); Kaufman et al., "Leakage in data mining" (TKDD 2012); scikit-learn "Common pitfalls" documentation.

<a id="m2"></a>
### M2. Linear / GLM / scorecard modeling

1. **Name + aliases** — Generalized linear models; logistic/linear regression; regularized regression (ridge/lasso/elastic net); credit scorecards (WoE/points-based); GAMs/EBMs as the modern shape-constrained variant; actuarial pricing GLMs.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Fit an additive, coefficient-based model on engineered tabular features so that every prediction is a transparent weighted sum, trading peak accuracy for interpretability, auditability, stability, and regulatory acceptability.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Classification (logistic), regression (Gaussian/Gamma/Tweedie for insurance severity), counts (Poisson), rates with exposure offsets; also the inferential workhorse when coefficients themselves (not predictions) are the deliverable.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular.
5. **Component pipeline** — Extraction and labeling as M1 → heavier statistical feature preparation (binning, monotonic transforms, weight-of-evidence encoding, multicollinearity screening via VIF) → model fitting with regularization → coefficient review with domain experts (sign checks, stability across segments) → calibration → scorecard scaling (points-to-double-odds in credit) → documentation pack (model risk management) → monitoring (population stability index is native to this tradition).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Logistic/linear regression (statsmodels for inference, scikit-learn/glum for prediction); elastic net (Zou & Hastie 2005) when p is large; GAMs (Hastie & Tibshirani 1986) and Explainable Boosting Machines (Lou et al. 2013; InterpretML) when nonlinear shapes are needed but additivity must be kept; monotonicity constraints where business logic demands them. Prefer over M1 when: regulation requires reason codes and full model documentation (credit, insurance), n is small, coefficients are the product, or stability across years outweighs a few AUC points.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Supervised binning of continuous variables; weight-of-evidence (WoE) encoding with information-value screening; explicit interaction terms chosen by domain knowledge; splines for smooth nonlinearity; careful treatment of correlated features (GBMs shrug at collinearity, GLM coefficients do not); exposure offsets for rate models.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — All of M1's leakage/point-in-time discipline applies unchanged; additionally: coefficient instability under multicollinearity (safeguard: VIF screening, regularization, or orthogonalization); omitted-variable bias when coefficients are interpreted causally (safeguard: never read a predictive GLM causally — route to M10/M11); binning leakage (bins chosen on full data including test; safeguard: fit binning inside CV folds); separation in logistic regression on rare categories (safeguard: penalized likelihood).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — As M1 (OOT split, AUC/Gini, calibration) plus scorecard-specific: Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic, population stability index, characteristic stability reports; likelihood-ratio tests and information criteria when doing inference; out-of-time stability is weighted more heavily than in-sample fit in regulated settings.
10. **Explainability methods** — The model *is* the explanation: signed coefficients, odds ratios, per-feature score points; adverse-action reason codes derive directly; stakeholders receive the scorecard table itself — the gold standard for model-risk committees.
11. **Data requirements** — Works from hundreds of rows; the small-data default; needs careful feature curation more than volume.
12. **Compute profile** — Negligible; CPU, seconds to train; the cheapest methodology in the catalog.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Maximally mature (decades in production); logistic regression was the single most-used method at work in Kaggle's 2017 survey (63.5%) and Kaggle 2021 still reports linear/logistic regression as the most commonly used algorithms; ubiquitous in banking (Basel model risk frameworks), insurance pricing, epidemiology, and marketing mix baselines.
14. **Reusable components** — Shares M1's extraction/labeling/evaluation/monitoring wholesale; supplies the interpretable-baseline component every supervised methodology should run; its calibration stage is reused by M1, M8, M16; WoE/binning feeds M1 as features; it is the standard final-layer meta-learner in stacking (M31) and the outcome/propensity model inside DML (M11).
15. **Representative use cases** — Credit scorecards, insurance pricing, clinical risk scores, marketing response baselines, regulatory stress models.
16. **Production failure modes** — Population drift breaking bin boundaries (PSI monitoring is standard); silent extrapolation outside fitted ranges; stale scorecards kept alive for years past their data (governance calendars mitigate).
17. **Tooling landscape** — statsmodels, scikit-learn, glum (high-performance GLMs), interpretml (EBM), optbinning (scorecards), R's glm/mgcv heritage.
18. **Key references** — Nelder & Wedderburn, "Generalized Linear Models" (JRSS 1972); Hastie & Tibshirani, "Generalized Additive Models" (1986); Zou & Hastie, "Regularization and variable selection via the elastic net" (2005); Siddiqi, "Credit Risk Scorecards" (2006); Lou et al., "Accurate intelligible models with pairwise interactions" (KDD 2013).

### M3. AutoML for tabular data

1. **Name + aliases** — Automated machine learning; AutoML; automated model selection + hyperparameter optimization (CASH); "citizen data science" tooling; auto-ensembling.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Automate the model-family selection, hyperparameter tuning, preprocessing choice, and ensembling stages of the tabular supervised pipeline under a compute budget, so that a near-state-of-the-art model is produced from a labeled table with minimal expert intervention.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Same as M1 (classification/regression/forecasting variants); AutoML is a *meta-methodology* over M1/M2/M4's training stages.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Primarily tabular; extensions cover text/image via pretrained-embedding front-ends (e.g., AutoGluon multimodal).
5. **Component pipeline** — Extraction, labeling, and point-in-time feature discipline remain **human responsibilities** (the most important correctness fact about AutoML) → automated preprocessing search → automated model search across families (GBMs, random forests, linear, neural) → automated HPO (Bayesian optimization, successive halving/Hyperband, or fixed strong portfolios) → automated multi-layer stacking/bagging → leaderboard + holdout evaluation → export/deploy → same monitoring as M1.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — AutoGluon (Erickson et al. 2020) — portfolio + multi-layer stack ensembling, consistently top of AutoML benchmarks (AMLB; Gijsbers et al. 2024); auto-sklearn (Feurer et al. 2015) — meta-learning warm-starts + Bayesian optimization; H2O AutoML; FLAML (cost-frugal HPO); TPOT (genetic pipelines); cloud services (SageMaker Autopilot, Vertex AI, Azure AutoML). Prefer AutoGluon for raw accuracy per compute; FLAML for tight budgets; cloud AutoML for platform integration.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Automated: type inference, missing-value handling, categorical encoding, datetime expansion, text-to-embedding columns. Not automated (and the leading failure source): point-in-time correctness, label construction, entity-level aggregation design — AutoML consumes a feature table, it does not build a correct one.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Everything in M1 §8 still applies and is *easier to miss* because the tool projects competence; specific additions: internal CV that ignores time or entity grouping (safeguard: pass explicit temporal/grouped splits — all serious frameworks support this); leaderboard overfitting to the internal validation set (safeguard: untouched OOT test set); target leakage sailing through unnoticed because no human reviewed features (safeguard: SHAP review of the winning model — an implausibly dominant feature is a leak detector); resource-budget sensitivity (results vary with time budget; fix and record it).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — As M1; plus benchmark discipline: compare the AutoML output against a hand-tuned LightGBM baseline (the honest question is the delta over a competent default, which AMLB 2024 shows is often modest on easy datasets but material on hard ones).
10. **Explainability methods** — SHAP on the final ensemble (or on a distilled single model); most frameworks ship permutation importance and leaderboards; stacked ensembles are less transparent — distill to a single GBM when reason codes are required.
11. **Data requirements** — As M1; AutoML's stacking shines most at 10⁴–10⁷ rows; below that, TabPFN-class (M4) or M2.
12. **Compute profile** — CPU-heavy by design (it spends budget searching); typical budgets minutes-to-hours; cost is the search budget multiplied over reruns — bound it.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mainstream tooling, partial adoption as practice: an ICML AutoML-workshop survey (2021) found 8% of practitioners had completely adopted AutoML for model selection/HPO, 26% mostly, 35% partially, 31% not at all; Gartner-derived commentary (2024, via Kumo) reports ~75% of enterprises have *evaluated* AutoML but <20% use it as the primary ML workflow. Mature for the tuning/ensembling stages; the framing "AutoML automates the middle of the pipeline, not the ends" is the practitioner consensus.
14. **Reusable components** — Its HPO component (Optuna/SMAC/Hyperband) is reused by every trained methodology here; its ensembling component is M31; it consumes M1's extraction/feature/label components and emits into M1's serving/monitoring.
15. **Representative use cases** — Fast baselines for any M1 use case; long-tail departmental models where expert time is the constraint; model refresh automation.
16. **Production failure modes** — As M1, plus: retrain-to-retrain model-family instability (this quarter a stack, next quarter a GBM — score distributions jump; mitigate by freezing family or monitoring score PSI across retrains); silent budget-truncation quality drops.
17. **Tooling landscape** — AutoGluon, auto-sklearn, H2O AutoML, FLAML, TPOT, LightAutoML; cloud: Vertex AI, SageMaker Autopilot, Azure AutoML; benchmark: AMLB (OpenML).
18. **Key references** — Feurer et al., "Efficient and Robust Automated Machine Learning" (NeurIPS 2015); Erickson et al., "AutoGluon-Tabular" (2020); Gijsbers et al., "AMLB: an AutoML Benchmark" (JMLR 2024); Hutter, Kotthoff & Vanschoren (eds.), "Automated Machine Learning" (2019).

### M4. Tabular deep learning & tabular foundation models (TabPFN-class)

1. **Name + aliases** — Neural networks for tabular data (MLP-with-embeddings, TabNet, FT-Transformer, SAINT, RealMLP, ModernNCA); tabular foundation models / prior-fitted networks (TabPFN, TabPFN v2, TabPFN-2.5, TabICL); "in-context tabular learning".
2. **One-sentence definition** — Replace the GBM training stage of the tabular pipeline with either a purpose-built neural architecture (trained per-dataset) or a pretrained tabular foundation model that performs supervised prediction in-context with no per-dataset training at all.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Classification and regression as M1; neural variants additionally enable multi-task heads, joint learning with text/image towers, and embedding extraction for downstream reuse.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular; mixed tabular+text/image via fused towers is this family's genuine differentiator over GBMs.
5. **Component pipeline** — As M1 for extraction/labeling/features, with these substitutions: preprocessing gains normalization/quantile transforms and learned categorical embeddings (neural nets are not scale-invariant); training becomes SGD with regularization or, for TabPFN-class, a single forward pass conditioning on the training set as context; tuning is heavier for per-dataset nets, near-zero for TabPFN; the rest of the pipeline (evaluation, explanation, serving, monitoring) is unchanged from M1.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Per-dataset nets: FT-Transformer and ResNet-style baselines (Gorishniy et al. 2021), TabNet (Arik & Pfister 2021), RealMLP, ModernNCA. Foundation models: **TabPFN v2** (Hollmann et al., *Nature* 2025) — a transformer pretrained on ~100–130M synthetic datasets that "significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines including gradient-boosted decision trees" (even tuned for 4 hours) on datasets up to **10,000 samples and 500 features**; a 2025 evaluation across 300+ datasets finds TabPFN v2 the best method on ~25% of datasets vs ~9.5% for CatBoost, while also finding performance diminishes on large/high-dimensional data where trees remain optimal ("A Closer Look at TabPFN v2", 2025); TabPFN-2.5 (Prior Labs tech report, Nov 2025) extends to 50k rows / 2k features with an 87% win rate vs default XGBoost on larger datasets. Selection rule: TabPFN-class for small data (≤10–50k rows) and fast iteration; GBMs (M1) for large data, monotonic constraints, and cheap CPU serving; neural towers when tabular must fuse with text/images.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Less manual encoding (learned embeddings absorb categoricals); still requires all of M1's point-in-time aggregation design; TabPFN needs essentially no preprocessing; per-dataset nets need normalization and benefit from the same engineered features as GBMs (the "deep learning removes feature engineering" claim does not hold on tabular data — Grinsztajn et al. 2022).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — M1 §8 in full; plus: normalization statistics computed on full data (leak; fit on train only); TabPFN context-window limits silently truncating training data (safeguard: respect documented limits — ≤10k/500 for v2 per Hollmann et al. 2025); benchmark-vs-enterprise distribution gap (synthetic-prior models are strongest on clean benchmark-like tables; validate on your own OOT data before trusting).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — As M1, with one addition: always report the tuned-LightGBM delta; on small data also report across multiple random splits (variance is high at n<5k).
10. **Explainability methods** — SHAP (Kernel/Deep variants — costlier than TreeSHAP); attention maps (TabNet/FT-Transformer) are illustrative but not faithful attributions; for TabPFN, permutation importance and model-agnostic SHAP.
11. **Data requirements** — TabPFN-class: its sweet spot is *small* data (hundreds to tens of thousands of rows) — inverting the usual deep-learning rule; per-dataset nets: generally need ≥10⁵ rows to compete with GBMs.
12. **Compute profile** — GPU for training per-dataset nets and for TabPFN inference at speed (CPU-feasible for small batches); TabPFN shifts cost from training (zero) to inference (context re-processing per prediction batch); GBMs remain cheaper to serve at scale.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Per-dataset tabular DL: mature research, niche production (adopted where multimodal fusion or shared embeddings matter). Tabular foundation models: emerging — a *Nature* 2025 publication and rapid iteration (TabPFN-2.5, TabICL) but limited documented enterprise production use as of early 2026; treat as a strong candidate for small-data solutions and an active watch item.
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M1's extraction/features/labels/evaluation/monitoring; its embedding outputs feed M13 (clustering), M14 (recsys towers), M17 (retrieval); the in-context paradigm connects to M19 (prompt-based prediction); TabPFN-TS reuses it for forecasting (M7).
15. **Representative use cases** — Small-sample scientific/industrial prediction, cold-start modeling before data accumulates, multimodal underwriting (tabular + documents), embedding services.
16. **Production failure modes** — As M1; plus GPU-serving cost creep and version drift in a fast-moving model family (pin model versions; regression-test on a frozen benchmark).
17. **Tooling landscape** — PyTorch; pytorch-tabular, pytorch-frame; TabPFN (Prior Labs OSS + API); AutoGluon (integrates TabPFN-class models).
18. **Key references** — Gorishniy et al., "Revisiting Deep Learning Models for Tabular Data" (NeurIPS 2021); Borisov et al., "Deep Neural Networks and Tabular Data: A Survey" (IEEE TNNLS 2022); Hollmann et al., "Accurate predictions on small data with a tabular foundation model" (Nature 2025); Prior Labs, "TabPFN-2.5 Technical Report" (2025); van Breugel & van der Schaar, "Why tabular foundation models should be a research priority" (ICML 2024 position paper).

## Family B — Time-series forecasting

Split into three dossiers because the pipelines genuinely differ: per-series statistical fitting (M5), global supervised learning over many series (M6), and zero-shot pretrained inference (M7).

<a id="m5"></a>
### M5. Classical / local statistical forecasting (ETS, ARIMA, Prophet, intermittent demand)

1. **Name + aliases** — Statistical forecasting; local univariate forecasting; exponential smoothing (ETS/Holt-Winters); Box-Jenkins (ARIMA/SARIMA/SARIMAX); Prophet-style decomposable curve fitting; Croston-family intermittent-demand methods; Theta.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Fit one interpretable statistical model per series that decomposes level, trend, and seasonality (plus optional regressors) to extrapolate the series with prediction intervals, at negligible compute and with strong small-data behavior.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Forecasting (point + probabilistic); decomposition/seasonal adjustment; simple anomaly detection via forecast residuals.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Univariate time series (one or a moderate number of series); regular sampling assumed; exogenous regressors optional (ARIMAX, Prophet regressors).
5. **Component pipeline** — Extraction: assemble a clean regular-frequency series (gap filling, calendar alignment, outlier flagging) → transformation (log/Box-Cox, differencing) → model identification & fitting per series (automatic order selection) → residual diagnostics (autocorrelation, normality) → probabilistic forecast generation → evaluation via rolling-origin backtesting → reporting (forecast + intervals + decomposition plot) → monitoring (forecast-vs-actual tracking, re-fit on schedule).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — ETS state-space family and auto-ARIMA (Hyndman & Khandakar 2008); Theta (Assimakopoulos & Nikolopoulos 2000 — M3-competition winner); Prophet (Taylor & Letham 2017) — decomposable trend+seasonality+holidays, robust to missing data and outliers, favored for interpretability though frequently beaten by ETS/ARIMA on standard benchmarks; TBATS for multiple seasonalities; Croston/SBA/TSB for intermittent demand (many zeros); seasonal-naive as the mandatory baseline. Prefer ETS/Theta for most business series; ARIMA when autocorrelation structure or regressors matter; Croston-family for spare-parts-style sparsity.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Minimal by design: calendar effects (holidays, trading days), Box-Cox stabilization, outlier adjustment, known-future regressors (price, promotion). The methodology's appeal is precisely that it does not require the feature factory of M1/M6.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Random (shuffled) train/test splits on time series (safeguard: rolling-origin/temporal splits only); using actuals of future regressors in backtests when only forecasts would exist at decision time (use forecasted regressors); data leakage through outlier/holiday adjustment computed over the full series (fit adjustments on training window only); ignoring calendar structure (week-of-year misalignment, moving holidays); evaluating only point error while the decision needs quantiles (safety stock — evaluate pinball loss); survivorship in series selection (dead SKUs removed, biasing aggregate accuracy).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Rolling-origin (time-series) cross-validation; scale-free metrics: MASE (vs seasonal-naive), sMAPE, WRMSSE for weighted hierarchies; probabilistic: pinball/quantile loss, coverage of prediction intervals; always report the seasonal-naive and ETS baselines — in M5 (2020), only 7.5% of 5,507 teams beat the tuned exponential-smoothing benchmark ES_bu (Makridakis et al. 2022), the strongest single fact about how good simple baselines are.
10. **Explainability methods** — Decomposition plots (trend/seasonality/holiday components) — among the most stakeholder-legible explanations in all of ML; coefficient tables for regressors; interval fans for uncertainty.
11. **Data requirements** — Works from ~2–3 seasonal cycles of history per series (e.g., 2–3 years for yearly seasonality); no cross-series data needed; the small-data forecasting default.
12. **Compute profile** — CPU, milliseconds-to-seconds per series; trivially parallel across series; cheapest forecasting option; statsforecast (Nixtla) fits millions of series on a single machine.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Maximally mature (decades of M-competition evidence); still the workhorse for FP&A, capacity planning, and any setting with few series or short history; the consensus reading of M4/M5 competitions: simple statistical methods are tough baselines that most complex methods fail to beat without cross-series learning (Makridakis et al. 2020, 2022).
14. **Reusable components** — Rolling-origin evaluation is reused by M6/M7; residual-based anomaly detection feeds M12; decomposition feeds feature engineering in M6; its baselines are the mandatory yardstick for M6/M7.
15. **Representative use cases** — Revenue/budget forecasting, call-volume staffing, energy load, inventory for low-count assortments, KPI monitoring baselines.
16. **Production failure modes** — Structural breaks (regime changes) that per-series models track slowly; holiday/calendar drift; silent series-mapping breakage (SKU recodes); monitoring = forecast-value-added (FVA) tracking against naive, interval-coverage tracking, automatic re-selection on schedule.
17. **Tooling landscape** — statsforecast (Nixtla), statsmodels, R forecast/fable (Hyndman), Prophet, Darts, sktime.
18. **Key references** — Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, "Forecasting: Principles and Practice" (3rd ed., 2021); Hyndman & Khandakar, "Automatic time series forecasting: the forecast package for R" (JSS 2008); Taylor & Letham, "Forecasting at Scale" (Am. Stat. 2018); Makridakis et al., "The M4 Competition" (IJF 2020) and "M5 accuracy competition" (IJF 2022); Croston (1972).

<a id="m6"></a>
### M6. Global machine-learning & deep forecasting (GBM-on-lags, DeepAR, N-BEATS/N-HiTS, TFT; hierarchical reconciliation)

1. **Name + aliases** — Global forecasting models; cross-learning; "ML forecasting"; GBM-with-lag-features ("the M5 recipe"); probabilistic deep forecasting (DeepAR); pure deep architectures (N-BEATS/N-HiTS, PatchTST); attention-based multi-horizon models (Temporal Fusion Transformer); plus hierarchical forecast reconciliation as its correctness-completing sub-stage.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Pool many related series into one supervised training problem — either a GBM over engineered lag/window features or a deep sequence model over raw windows — to learn shared patterns across series, produce probabilistic multi-horizon forecasts, and reconcile them across an aggregation hierarchy.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Forecasting (point + probabilistic + multi-horizon), at scale (10³–10⁷ series); demand sensing with covariates; cold-start forecasting for new series via cross-learning.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Panel/hierarchical time series (many related series) + static metadata (item/store attributes) + known-future covariates (price, promotions, holidays).
5. **Component pipeline** — Extraction: long-format panel assembly with static, past-observed, and known-future covariate typing (the TFT taxonomy) → feature engineering (GBM path) or windowing/scaling (deep path) → global model training with temporal validation → quantile/distributional output heads → hierarchical reconciliation (MinT/bottom-up) so forecasts sum coherently → rolling-origin backtesting against M5 baselines → serving (batch forecast tables, typically nightly/weekly) → monitoring (FVA, bias, interval coverage) and periodic retraining.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — **LightGBM on lag/rolling features**: the M5-winning recipe — all top-5 M5 solutions used LightGBM, and top entries beat the best statistical benchmark by >20% WRMSSE (Makridakis et al. 2022); the default for retail-scale demand. **DeepAR** (Salinas et al., IJF 2020): autoregressive RNN outputting distribution parameters; Amazon-origin, strong with count distributions (negative binomial). **N-BEATS/N-HiTS** (Oreshkin et al., ICLR 2020; Challu et al. 2023): pure MLP stacks, M4-winning accuracy without covariates. **TFT** (Lim et al., IJF 2021): multi-horizon attention with variable-selection networks — the choice when covariate interpretability matters. **PatchTST/iTransformer** (2023): patch-based transformers for long horizons. Reconciliation: MinT (Wickramasuriya et al., JASA 2019). Selection: LightGBM first (accuracy-per-cost, and it exploits covariates well); DeepAR/TFT when probabilistic outputs and complex covariates dominate; N-HiTS for long horizons without covariates.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — (GBM path) lags at seasonal offsets (t-7, t-28, t-364), rolling means/stds over multiple windows, expanding-window statistics, days-since-event, price and promo features, calendar one-hots, series-identity categorical features, target scaling per series (sales normalized by series mean); careful *lag availability*: a lag-7 feature limits the recursive horizon or forces direct multi-horizon models. (Deep path) per-series normalization (critical — DeepAR's scale handling), embedding of series IDs and static attributes.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — All M5 §8 temporal discipline; plus: leakage via rolling features computed over windows crossing the forecast origin (safeguard: strict as-of computation per training sample); recursive-forecast error compounding vs direct-horizon tradeoff (choose deliberately); target normalization statistics leaking future data (fit on training window only); hierarchy incoherence (item forecasts not summing to store totals confuse planners — reconcile); new-series cold start silently receiving global mean behavior (flag and route); promotion covariates known-in-future in training but not at serve time (train/serve covariate parity audit); evaluating on the aggregate while decisions are per-series (report weighted per-series metrics, e.g. WRMSSE).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Rolling-origin backtests with multiple origins; WRMSSE/MASE vs seasonal-naive and ETS baselines (mandatory, per the M5 7.5%-beat-the-baseline finding); pinball loss and coverage for quantiles; per-segment breakdowns (fast vs slow movers, new vs mature); stability across retrain cycles.
10. **Explainability methods** — (GBM) SHAP on lag/covariate features — "what drove next month's dip"; (TFT) variable-selection weights and attention patterns (indicative, not faithful); counterfactual covariate scenarios ("forecast if no promotion") — often the actual business deliverable.
11. **Data requirements** — Needs *many related series* (the cross-learning premise; roughly ≥100s of series) or long panels; ≥2 seasonal cycles preferred; known-future covariates require an operational source at serve time.
12. **Compute profile** — LightGBM path: CPU, minutes-to-hours for millions of rows; deep path: GPU training (hours), CPU-feasible batch inference; both dominated by feature-pipeline cost at retail scale.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mainstream at scale: the standard at large retailers/marketplaces (M5 evidence; Amazon's DeepAR productized in AWS Forecast; Walmart-scale deployments in the M5 dataset itself). The GBM path is the most common production pattern (estimate; consistent with M5 results and practitioner literature, not survey-verified).
14. **Reusable components** — Reuses M1's GBM training/tuning/SHAP wholesale; its panel feature engineering is shared with M8 (discrete-time survival) and M12 (temporal anomaly features); reconciliation is a standalone reusable post-processor; probabilistic evaluation shared with M30; forecasts feed M29 (inventory/scheduling optimization) — the canonical predict-then-optimize pairing.
15. **Representative use cases** — SKU×store demand, workforce/call volume at scale, cash-flow and payments volume, energy portfolios, supply-chain lead-time forecasting.
16. **Production failure modes** — Regime breaks (COVID-style) hitting all series at once (global models adapt faster than local ones but amplify shared shocks); promotion-covariate pipeline breakage; slow bias accumulation (systematic under-forecast of growing series); feedback loops via stock-outs censoring demand (censored-demand correction is standard practice); monitoring = FVA vs naive, rolling bias, coverage, and segment drilldowns.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Nixtla stack (mlforecast, neuralforecast, hierarchicalforecast), GluonTS, PyTorch Forecasting, Darts, LightGBM; AWS Forecast (managed DeepAR-family).
18. **Key references** — Makridakis, Spiliotis & Assimakopoulos, "M5 accuracy competition: Results, findings, and conclusions" (IJF 2022); Salinas et al., "DeepAR" (IJF 2020); Oreshkin et al., "N-BEATS" (ICLR 2020); Lim et al., "Temporal Fusion Transformers" (IJF 2021); Wickramasuriya et al., "Optimal forecast reconciliation" (JASA 2019); Januschowski et al., "Criteria for classifying forecasting methods" (IJF 2020).

<a id="m7"></a>
### M7. Time-series foundation models (zero-shot forecasting)

1. **Name + aliases** — TSFMs; pretrained/zero-shot forecasters; TimeGPT (Nixtla), Chronos/Chronos-Bolt (Amazon), TimesFM (Google), Moirai (Salesforce), Lag-Llama, TabPFN-TS.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Apply a large model pretrained on massive corpora of heterogeneous time series (or synthetic priors) to forecast a new series zero-shot or with light fine-tuning, replacing per-deployment training with pretrained inference.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Forecasting (point + probabilistic), anomaly detection via forecast residuals; cold-start and long-tail series where training data is scarce.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Univariate (mostly) time series; covariate support is limited/emerging — a key current gap vs M6.
5. **Component pipeline** — Extraction and regularization as M5 → tokenize/scale series into the model's input format → zero-shot inference (optionally few-shot fine-tune) → probabilistic outputs → the *same* rolling-origin backtesting against M5/M6 baselines (non-negotiable) → serving via API or self-hosted weights → monitoring identical to M5/M6.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Chronos (Ansari et al. 2024): tokenized-value LLM-style pretraining; Chronos-Bolt: faster distilled variant; TimesFM (Das et al., ICML 2024): patched decoder; Moirai (Woo et al. 2024): masked-encoder multi-frequency; TimeGPT (Garza & Mergenthaler-Canseco 2023): closed API; TabPFN-TS (2025): tabular-foundation-model reframing that outperformed TimesFM-2.0 and Chronos-Bolt on the GIFT-Eval benchmark with an 11M-parameter model vs 500M (Hoo et al. 2025) — evidence the field is unsettled.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Essentially none (the selling point): scaling/tokenization is internal; covariates, where supported, are appended channels; calendar handling remains the user's job on irregular series.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Benchmark contamination (public series in pretraining corpora inflate zero-shot claims; safeguard: evaluate on your own private OOT data); silent domain mismatch (models pretrained on web/energy/traffic data vs your intermittent SKUs; Croston-style sparsity is a known weak spot); interval miscalibration on new domains (verify coverage empirically); API drift for closed models (pin versions); all M5 temporal-evaluation discipline unchanged.
9. **Evaluation methodology** — GIFT-Eval and fev-bench are the emerging public standards (2024–2025); in-house: rolling-origin backtest vs seasonal-naive, ETS, and a LightGBM global model — adopt only on demonstrated FVA; measure inference cost per 1k series alongside accuracy.
10. **Explainability methods** — Weak — no decomposition, no coefficients; residual analysis and post-hoc attribution only; a real adoption barrier where planners demand "why".
11. **Data requirements** — Minimal per series (that's the point): usable with dozens of observations; needs no cross-series training data of your own.
12. **Compute profile** — GPU (or API) inference; per-forecast cost far above M5/M6 at bulk scale, negligible at small scale; zero training cost.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Emerging: strong 2024–2025 benchmark results but "limited, uneven production adoption... generally viewed as maturing rather than established" (synthesis of 2025 practitioner and vendor evidence; production evidence is largely vendor case studies — TimeGPT API users, internal Amazon/Google use — without audited scale figures). Sensible posture: pilot for long-tail/cold-start series alongside an M6 champion.
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M5's series preparation and M5/M6's evaluation harness verbatim; its pretrained-inference pattern parallels M19/M35 (foundation-model adaptation); residuals feed M12.
15. **Representative use cases** — Long-tail SKU forecasting, new-product cold start, quick-turnaround forecasting without an ML team, anomaly baselines across thousands of operational metrics.
16. **Production failure modes** — Domain shift with no retraining lever (you can't fix a frozen model's blind spot except by switching); API cost/latency surprises at scale; version churn; monitoring as M6 plus per-segment FVA to catch domain-mismatch pockets.
17. **Tooling landscape** — nixtla (TimeGPT API), chronos-forecasting, timesfm, uni2ts (Moirai), GluonTS integration; GIFT-Eval leaderboard.
18. **Key references** — Ansari et al., "Chronos: Learning the Language of Time Series" (TMLR 2024); Das et al., "A decoder-only foundation model for time-series forecasting" (ICML 2024); Woo et al., "Unified Training of Universal Time Series Forecasting Transformers" (ICML 2024); Aksu et al., "GIFT-Eval" (2024); Hoo et al., "From Tables to Time" / TabPFN-TS (2025).

## Family C — Survival / time-to-event

<a id="m8"></a>
### M8. Survival analysis / time-to-event modeling

1. **Name + aliases** — Survival analysis; time-to-event modeling; reliability analysis (engineering); duration modeling (economics); churn-timing modeling; Cox proportional hazards; discrete-time hazard modeling.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Model *when* an event will occur rather than *whether* it occurs in a fixed window, treating right-censored observations (entities that haven't had the event yet) as first-class information instead of discarding or mislabeling them.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Time-to-event regression; hazard estimation; censored-data classification generalization; competing risks (multiple exclusive event types).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular with (duration, event-indicator) labels; optionally time-varying covariates in long format.
5. **Component pipeline** — Extraction: entity spells with entry date, event/censor date, censoring indicator → labeling: duration + event flag (censoring correctness is *the* core labeling task) → feature engineering as M1 (point-in-time at entry, or per-period for time-varying) → model fitting (Cox, parametric, forests, or discrete-time GBM) → evaluation with censoring-aware metrics → explanation (hazard ratios or SHAP) → reporting (survival curves, expected-time-to-event, per-period risk) → serving/monitoring as M1.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Kaplan–Meier estimator (descriptive baseline); **Cox proportional hazards** (Cox 1972) — semi-parametric, hazard-ratio interpretability, the clinical/regulatory standard; parametric AFT models (Weibull/log-normal) when extrapolation beyond observed follow-up is needed; **random survival forests** (Ishwaran et al. 2008) and gradient-boosted survival (XGBoost `survival:cox`/AFT, scikit-survival) for nonlinear accuracy; **discrete-time hazard** — expand each entity into entity-period rows and fit any M1 classifier on "event in this period?" — the pragmatic enterprise favorite because it reuses the whole M1 stack, handles time-varying covariates naturally, and outputs per-period churn probabilities; deep variants (DeepSurv, Katzman et al. 2018; DeepHit for competing risks) where scale justifies.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — As M1 plus: tenure/age-at-entry features, time-varying covariates via period expansion, period-index features (baseline hazard shape), cohort indicators; beware features that proxy the censoring mechanism.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Treating censored entities as negatives (the classic churn-modeling error — biases risk downward; safeguard: survival framing or strict window-complete labeling in M1); *informative censoring* (censoring correlated with risk, e.g. data ends because customer was acquired; safeguard: examine censoring reasons, competing-risks framing); immortal time bias (entities must survive to enter the cohort; align entry definitions); proportional-hazards violation (test Schoenfeld residuals; stratify or use time-varying effects); leakage of post-entry information into entry features (point-in-time discipline as M1).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Concordance index (Harrell's C; Uno's C for heavy censoring); time-dependent AUC and Brier score (censoring-weighted, IPCW); calibration of survival curves at business-relevant horizons; temporal OOT cohorts as M1; baseline: Kaplan–Meier by segment.
10. **Explainability methods** — Cox hazard ratios (native, stakeholder-friendly: "1.8× the churn hazard"); SHAP on discrete-time GBMs (per-period drivers); survival-curve visualizations per segment — what retention teams actually consume.
11. **Data requirements** — Needs event dates, not just event flags; enough follow-up that a reasonable fraction of entities have had the event (heavy censoring inflates variance); a few thousand events for tree-based variants.
12. **Compute profile** — CPU; discrete-time expansion multiplies rows by periods (manageable; prune post-event rows); otherwise as M1.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature in clinical research, insurance, and reliability engineering (decades); moderately adopted in customer analytics — the discrete-time reframing is common in sophisticated churn/LTV teams but many enterprises still use fixed-window M1 classification (estimate; not source-verified). Standard in credit (time-to-default, IFRS9 lifetime PD).
14. **Reusable components** — Reuses M1's entire stack via the discrete-time trick; shares point-in-time features with M1/M6; expected-time-to-event outputs feed LTV models and M29 (intervention timing/optimization); censoring-aware evaluation is its unique contribution.
15. **Representative use cases** — Churn timing, time-to-default, equipment time-to-failure, patient outcomes, subscription LTV, claim development.
16. **Production failure modes** — Cohort drift (newer cohorts behave differently — monitor by cohort); censoring-pattern shifts (a data-retention policy change looks like a hazard change); as M1 otherwise.
17. **Tooling landscape** — lifelines, scikit-survival, pycox; XGBoost/LightGBM survival objectives; R survival/flexsurv heritage.
18. **Key references** — Cox, "Regression Models and Life-Tables" (JRSS-B 1972); Ishwaran et al., "Random Survival Forests" (AoAS 2008); Katzman et al., "DeepSurv" (BMC MRM 2018); Singer & Willett, "It's About Time: Using Discrete-Time Survival Analysis" (1993); Therneau & Grambsch, "Modeling Survival Data" (2000).

## Family D — Causal inference & uplift

Three dossiers because the pipelines genuinely differ: randomization (M9), identification from observational data (M10), and per-individual treatment-effect learning for targeting (M11).

<a id="m9"></a>
### M9. Online controlled experimentation (A/B testing at scale)

1. **Name + aliases** — A/B testing; online controlled experiments (OCEs); randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for products; split testing; switchback/cluster experiments (marketplace variants); experimentation platforms.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Randomly assign units (users, sessions, geos, time-slices) to treatment and control, run the change live, and estimate its causal effect on guardrail and goal metrics with statistical error control — the only methodology in this compendium that *measures* causality by construction rather than by assumption.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Causal effect measurement; decision gating (ship/no-ship); parameter selection; incremental-value quantification for every other methodology's outputs.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Event/log streams keyed by randomization unit; tabular covariates for variance reduction and heterogeneity slicing.
5. **Component pipeline** — Hypothesis + metric specification (goal metrics, guardrails, invariants) → power analysis and minimum-detectable-effect sizing → randomization-unit choice (user vs session vs geo vs time-slice; switchbacks where units interfere) → assignment service (hashing, bucketing, exposure logging) → live run with sample-ratio-mismatch and guardrail monitoring → analysis (difference in means with CUPED variance reduction; sequential or fixed-horizon inference) → heterogeneity slicing → decision + institutional memory (experiment catalog) → meta-analysis across experiments.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Two-sample t/z tests and their regression equivalents; **CUPED** (Deng et al., WSDM 2013) — pre-experiment covariate adjustment, typically 30–50% variance reduction on retention-type metrics (Microsoft EXP practice); stratified/post-stratified estimators; sequential testing (mSPRT, always-valid p-values — Johari et al. 2017) vs group-sequential; switchback designs and cluster-randomization for interference (Bojinov et al. 2023); bandits (M26) when the goal shifts from measurement to allocation; variance-reduction ML (CUPAC/MLRATE) reusing M1 models as covariate predictors.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Pre-period metrics per unit (the CUPED covariate); exposure/trigger definitions (analyze only affected units); segment features for heterogeneity — all subject to the rule that covariates must be pre-assignment.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Sample-ratio mismatch (assignment imbalance signaling broken randomization; automated SRM tests are table stakes — Fabijan et al. 2019); peeking/optional stopping inflating false positives (sequential methods or fixed horizons); interference/network effects violating SUTVA (marketplace cannibalization; safeguard: switchbacks, cluster randomization); dilution from analyzing unexposed users (trigger-based analysis); multiple testing across metrics and slices (FDR control); novelty/primacy effects (run long enough; check time-trends); Twyman's law — outlandish wins are usually instrumentation bugs (Kohavi et al. 2020); metric gaming (pair goal metrics with guardrails).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — The methodology *is* evaluation; its own QA: A/A tests to validate the platform, SRM checks, replication of big wins, meta-analysis of historical experiments. Base rates matter: Google/Bing report only ~10–20% of experiments produce positive results, and Microsoft roughly ⅓ positive / ⅓ neutral / ⅓ negative (Kohavi & Thomke, HBR 2017) — the strongest known argument for testing everything.
10. **Explainability methods** — Effect sizes with confidence intervals per metric; segment breakdowns; stakeholders receive a scorecard: goal-metric lift, guardrail status, and a ship recommendation.
11. **Data requirements** — Traffic sufficient for power at the MDE (the binding constraint — small products can't detect small effects); an assignment mechanism; clean event instrumentation; pre-period data for CUPED.
12. **Compute profile** — Trivial analysis compute; the cost is engineering (assignment service, logging, pipelines) and calendar time (experiment duration).
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mainstream at digital-product companies: Amazon, Booking.com, Google, and Microsoft each run >10,000 experiments/year (Kohavi & Thomke, HBR 2017); Airbnb, Netflix, LinkedIn, Uber all publish platform architectures (2019 PMC review lists 13+ companies running "hundreds of concurrent experiments on millions of users"). Less adopted outside digital channels; geo-experiments and switchbacks extend it to pricing/logistics.
14. **Reusable components** — Its metric/telemetry layer is shared with all monitoring stages; CUPED covariates come from M1's feature pipelines; it is the *evaluation-of-record* for M11 (uplift targeting must be validated by experiment), M14/M15 (rec/ranking changes), M26 (bandits are its adaptive sibling), M28 (agent rollouts); experiment logs are the training data for M11 and M27 (off-policy learning).
15. **Representative use cases** — Feature launches, ranking/recommender changes, pricing tests (geo/switchback), marketing creative, ML-model champion/challenger validation.
16. **Production failure modes** — Assignment-system entropy (carryover, bucket reuse); instrumentation drift breaking metrics; experiment-culture failure modes (running underpowered tests, cherry-picking slices); platform monitoring: A/A drift, SRM alarms, metric time-series health.
17. **Tooling landscape** — In-house platforms (published: Microsoft EXP, Netflix, Airbnb ERF, Uber XP); open source: GrowthBook, Unleash, PlanOut (legacy), Eppo/Statsig/Optimizely (commercial); statsmodels/scipy for analysis.
18. **Key references** — Kohavi, Tang & Xu, "Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments" (2020); Deng et al., "Improving the Sensitivity of Online Controlled Experiments by Utilizing Pre-Experiment Data" (WSDM 2013); Kohavi & Thomke, "The Surprising Power of Online Experiments" (HBR 2017); Johari et al., "Peeking at A/B Tests" (KDD 2017); Fabijan et al., "Diagnosing Sample Ratio Mismatch" (2019); Bojinov & Gupta, "Online Experimentation" (2022).

<a id="m10"></a>
### M10. Quasi-experimental causal inference (DiD, synthetic control, IV, RDD)

1. **Name + aliases** — Observational causal inference; quasi-experiments; natural experiments; policy evaluation; difference-in-differences (DiD), event studies, synthetic control (SCM), instrumental variables (IV), regression discontinuity (RDD), interrupted time series / CausalImpact-style Bayesian structural counterfactuals.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Estimate the causal effect of an intervention when randomization is impossible, by exploiting structure in observational data — parallel trends, donor pools, instruments, or thresholds — under explicitly stated identification assumptions that must be argued and falsification-tested rather than guaranteed.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Causal effect estimation (average effects, not individual targeting — that's M11); impact measurement of launches, policies, outages, campaigns.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Panel data (units × time), aggregate time series, tabular cross-sections with assignment mechanisms.
5. **Component pipeline** — Causal question + estimand definition (ATT of what, on whom, over when) → identification-strategy selection (which natural structure exists?) → causal diagram / assumption articulation (confounders, instrument validity, threshold exogeneity) → data assembly (treated + comparison units, pre/post periods) → estimation → falsification & robustness battery (pre-trends, placebo units/dates, sensitivity analysis) → uncertainty quantification (clustered/bootstrap/permutation inference) → effect report with assumption caveats.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — **DiD/event-study** (two-way fixed effects; modern staggered-adoption estimators — Callaway & Sant'Anna 2021, Goodman-Bacon 2021 — because naive TWFE is biased under heterogeneous timing); **synthetic control** (Abadie et al. 2010) and **Bayesian structural time series / CausalImpact** (Brodersen et al. 2015) for one-or-few treated units (the geo-launch workhorse); **IV/2SLS** (encouragement designs; historical instruments) with weak-instrument diagnostics; **RDD** (Imbens & Lemieux 2008) at eligibility thresholds (credit cutoffs, ranking positions); matching/weighting (propensity scores, IPW, doubly-robust AIPW) as the confounder-adjustment baseline.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Pre-period outcome histories (the matching/donor variables); covariate balance features; running-variable construction for RDD; instrument construction; time-alignment features (event time relative to treatment).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Assuming rather than testing parallel trends (safeguard: pre-trend plots, placebo-in-time tests); contaminated donor pools in SCM (donors affected by the treatment; screen for spillovers); weak or invalid instruments (F-statistics; over-identification tests; economic argument for exclusion restriction); manipulation of the running variable at RDD thresholds (McCrary density test); staggered-adoption TWFE bias (use modern estimators); confounding by concurrent events (event-history audit around the intervention date); p-hacking across specifications (pre-register the primary spec; report the specification curve).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — There is no ground truth by definition — evaluation is falsification: placebo tests (fake treatment dates/units should show null effects), permutation inference (SCM's standard), sensitivity analysis (Rosenbaum bounds, E-values), robustness across specifications, and where possible calibration against a past RCT on a similar intervention.
10. **Explainability methods** — The identification story itself (a diagrammed argument), counterfactual-vs-actual plots (SCM/CausalImpact's synthetic trajectory chart is the most persuasive artifact in causal reporting), effect timelines from event studies.
11. **Data requirements** — Sufficient pre-period history (≥ several periods for trend validation); comparison units untouched by treatment; for IV/RDD, an actual instrument/threshold — the method menu is dictated by what natural structure exists, not by preference.
12. **Compute profile** — Trivial; CPU seconds-to-minutes; the cost is analyst expertise.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature in economics/policy (the 2021 Nobel to Card/Angrist/Imbens is for exactly this toolkit); mainstream in tech measurement teams for geo-launches and marketing incrementality (CausalImpact from Google 2015; Meta GeoLift, Uber/Airbnb publish practice); adoption concentrated in dedicated measurement/economics functions rather than general DS teams (estimate; not source-verified).
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M5/M6 forecasting components (counterfactual construction is forecasting the untreated path); shares panel-data extraction with M6; supplies the *measurement layer* for solutions built on M1/M14/M29 when experiments are infeasible; propensity/outcome sub-models come from M1/M2.
15. **Representative use cases** — Geo-level marketing incrementality, store/feature launches where randomization is impossible, pricing-policy changes, regulatory/policy impact, outage cost quantification.
16. **Production failure modes** — Mostly one-shot analyses, so the failure mode is *decision* risk: fragile identification quietly baked into ROI dashboards; standard practice is assumption-expiry (re-validate when the environment shifts) and triangulation across designs.
17. **Tooling landscape** — CausalImpact (R/Python ports), DoWhy (identification + refutation framework), statsmodels/linearmodels (panel/IV), pysynth/SparseSC, R did/synthdid/rdrobust ecosystem, Meta GeoLift.
18. **Key references** — Angrist & Pischke, "Mostly Harmless Econometrics" (2009); Abadie, Diamond & Hainmueller, "Synthetic Control Methods" (JASA 2010); Brodersen et al., "Inferring causal impact using Bayesian structural time-series models" (AoAS 2015); Callaway & Sant'Anna, "Difference-in-Differences with multiple time periods" (J. Econometrics 2021); Imbens & Lemieux, "Regression discontinuity designs" (J. Econometrics 2008); Cunningham, "Causal Inference: The Mixtape" (2021).

<a id="m11"></a>
### M11. Uplift modeling & heterogeneous treatment effects (meta-learners, DML, uplift trees)

1. **Name + aliases** — Uplift modeling; heterogeneous treatment effect (HTE) / CATE estimation; incremental-response modeling; true-lift modeling; net-lift; persuasion modeling; double/debiased machine learning (DML); off-policy evaluation (its decision-layer companion).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Learn *who changes behavior because of a treatment* — the individual-level causal effect τ(x) = E[Y|do(T=1),x] − E[Y|do(T=0),x] — rather than who is likely to act anyway, so that interventions (offers, calls, discounts) are targeted at the persuadables and withheld from sure things, lost causes, and do-not-disturbs.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Causal inference/uplift; targeting policy learning; treatment-effect ranking; policy value estimation (OPE).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular with a treatment column — ideally from a randomized experiment (M9 logs), otherwise observational with confounding adjustment.
5. **Component pipeline** — Treatment/outcome/population definition → data source: past experiment logs (gold) or observational history (requires unconfoundedness argument) → point-in-time features as M1 (pre-treatment only) → CATE estimator training → validation with uplift-specific metrics (no per-row ground truth exists) → policy construction (treat top-uplift deciles subject to budget/constraints — hands off to M29 when constrained) → *experimental validation of the policy itself* (M9) → deployment with a persistent random holdout → monitoring of incremental value.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — **Meta-learners** (Künzel et al., PNAS 2019): S-learner (single model with treatment feature; biased toward zero when effects are small), T-learner (two models; noisy differencing), **X-learner** (best with unbalanced treatment arms), **R-learner** (Nie & Wager 2021; orthogonalized loss); **Double ML** (Chernozhukov et al., Econometrics J. 2018) — cross-fitted nuisance models (any M1 learner) + orthogonal score for debiased effects with valid inference; **causal forests** (Wager & Athey, JASA 2018) — honest splitting, CIs on τ(x); **uplift trees** (Rzepakowski & Jaroszewicz 2012) — split directly on treatment-control divergence; class-transformation methods for quick baselines; **off-policy evaluation** (IPS/DR estimators — Dudík et al. 2014) to score a proposed targeting policy on logged data before launching.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Identical to M1 with one hard rule: features strictly pre-treatment (post-treatment covariates destroy the causal contrast); include effect *modifiers* domain experts nominate; propensity features in observational settings.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Training on observational data with unmeasured confounding and reading it causally (safeguard: prefer experiment logs; otherwise DML + sensitivity analysis, and say so in the report); post-treatment feature leakage; evaluating with outcome-model metrics (AUC on Y is irrelevant — a great churn predictor can be a useless uplift ranker; use uplift metrics); ignoring propensity overlap (trim/weight where positivity fails); mistaking S-learner regularization bias for "no heterogeneity"; policy feedback loops — once you target by uplift, new logs are confounded by your own policy (safeguard: persistent randomized holdout, logged propensities).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Qini curves and AUUC (area under uplift curve); uplift-by-decile bar charts against a random-targeting baseline; transformed-outcome or R-loss for model selection; **the decisive test is an M9 experiment of model-targeted vs business-as-usual targeting**, measuring incremental outcome per unit spend; OPE (doubly-robust) as the pre-launch estimate.
10. **Explainability methods** — SHAP on the CATE model ("what makes this customer persuadable"); segment-level uplift tables (the four-quadrant persuadables/sure-things/lost-causes/do-not-disturbs framing is the stakeholder lingua franca); policy statements ("treat segments A,C when budget < X").
11. **Data requirements** — The most data-hungry tabular methodology per unit of signal: effects are second-order differences, so needs large samples (typically ≥10⁴–10⁵ per arm for stable CATE; estimate consistent with practitioner literature, not survey-verified) and a real treatment-assignment record (with propensities if non-random).
12. **Compute profile** — CPU; a few× M1's cost (multiple nuisance models, cross-fitting).
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature methods, moderate adoption concentrated in experimentation-heavy firms: the open-source stack is production-grade (CausalML from Uber ~2019; EconML from Microsoft; DoWhy) and documented deployments exist at Uber, Booking, Wayfair, and banks' retention teams, but public evidence is case-study rather than survey-quantified (2026 tooling reviews characterize causal ML as "well established in advanced analytics teams... mostly anecdotal/case-study based" adoption evidence).
14. **Reusable components** — Wraps M1 entirely (nuisance models, features, SHAP); consumes M9's logs and returns to M9 for validation; hands policies to M29 for constrained allocation and to M26 when targeting must adapt online; OPE component shared with M26/M27.
15. **Representative use cases** — Retention-offer targeting, discount/coupon optimization, collections outreach, cross-sell contact policies, ad incrementality targeting.
16. **Production failure modes** — Effect drift (persuadability changes faster than propensity); eroding holdouts (business pressure to treat everyone destroys measurement; defend the holdout); creeping confounding as policies personalize; monitoring = incremental lift on the holdout, not raw outcome rates.
17. **Tooling landscape** — CausalML (Uber), EconML (Microsoft), DoWhy, scikit-uplift, grf (R causal forests).
18. **Key references** — Künzel et al., "Metalearners for estimating heterogeneous treatment effects" (PNAS 2019); Chernozhukov et al., "Double/debiased machine learning" (2018); Wager & Athey, "Estimation and Inference of Heterogeneous Treatment Effects using Random Forests" (JASA 2018); Nie & Wager, "Quasi-oracle estimation" (Biometrika 2021); Dudík, Langford & Li, "Doubly Robust Policy Evaluation" (2014); Gutierrez & Gérardy, "Causal Inference and Uplift Modelling: A Review" (PMLR 2017).

## Family E — Anomaly detection

<a id="m12"></a>
### M12. Anomaly / outlier detection (batch and streaming)

1. **Name + aliases** — Anomaly detection; outlier/novelty detection; one-class classification; unsupervised fraud screening; out-of-distribution detection; (streaming variants) online/real-time anomaly detection, changepoint detection.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Score how unusual each observation (or window) is relative to learned normal behavior — without requiring labeled anomalies — and route the top scores to investigation or automated response, in batch or on live streams.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Anomaly/outlier detection; novelty detection; changepoint detection; rare-event triage that graduates to supervised M1 once investigations produce labels.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular, event/log streams, time series (via M5/M6 residuals), graphs (M22 features), images (M24 reconstruction), text (M17 embedding distance) — genuinely modality-agnostic once a representation exists.
5. **Component pipeline** — Scoping: define the unit (transaction, entity-day, metric-window) and what "anomalous" should mean operationally → extraction + feature/representation engineering (this stage dominates outcome quality) → normal-behavior model fitting (on presumed-clean or contaminated-tolerant data) → score calibration to a review budget (alert rate = investigator capacity) → triage UI with explanations → feedback capture from investigations → (streaming path) incremental model updates, watermarking, concept-drift handling → label accumulation → periodic graduation to supervised scoring (M1) with the unsupervised layer retained for novel patterns.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — **Isolation Forest** (Liu, Ting & Zhou 2008) — the tabular default: fast, few assumptions; Local Outlier Factor (Breunig et al. 2000) for density/local structure; one-class SVM (legacy); ECOD/COPOD (2020–2022) — parameter-free empirical-CDF methods, strong ADBench performers; autoencoder reconstruction error (tabular/image/sequence); PCA-residual methods (classical process monitoring); for streams: Half-Space Trees (Tan et al. 2011), RRCF (Guha et al. 2016 — AWS Kinesis's algorithm), CUSUM/Page–Hinkley and Bayesian online changepoint (Adams & MacKay 2007) for level shifts; for time series: forecast-residual scoring on M5/M6 outputs. ADBench's 57-algorithm evaluation (Han et al., NeurIPS 2022) found *no universal winner* and that even minimal supervision beats unsupervised methods — the strongest argument for the label-feedback loop. Fraud-specific practice: velocity features and graph features (M22) feed the same scorers.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Velocity/frequency features (count of events per entity per 1m/1h/24h windows — the fraud workhorse); ratio-to-own-history features (today vs entity's trailing baseline); peer-group normalization (entity vs cohort); session/sequence statistics; graph-derived features (shared devices/addresses, community membership — PR-AUC improvements from ~30% to ~50% reported when graph features augment fraud models; industry case reports 2023–2024); embedding representations for text/image inputs.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Training-data contamination (anomalies inside the "normal" training set shrink their own scores; safeguard: robust methods, iterative cleaning, contamination-rate assumptions made explicit); threshold-on-training leakage (calibrate on held-out time); evaluation without labels (bootstrap a labeled set from investigations before claiming performance); seasonality mistaken for anomaly (deseasonalize via M5 first); alert-budget mismatch (a 0.1% alert rate on 10M events is still 10k alerts/day); entity-resolution failures splitting behavioral history (M23 upstream); feedback bias — investigators only label what was alerted (safeguard: random sampling below threshold for unbiased labels).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — With labels: PR-AUC and precision@budget (ROC-AUC misleads at extreme imbalance); alert precision from investigation outcomes; detection latency for streaming; without labels: injection studies (synthetic anomalies), stability analysis, top-k review sessions with domain experts; baseline: simple z-score/rule thresholds — often embarrassingly competitive.
10. **Explainability methods** — Per-alert feature contributions (SHAP on isolation forests via TreeSHAP-style adaptations; reconstruction-error breakdown per feature for autoencoders); "which features made this unusual" is mandatory for investigator trust; peer-comparison visualizations ("this account vs its cohort").
11. **Data requirements** — No labels needed to start (its raison d'être); needs enough history to characterize normal (weeks-to-months per entity/metric); streaming variants need bounded-memory summaries.
12. **Compute profile** — CPU, light: isolation forests train in seconds-to-minutes on millions of rows; streaming scorers run in microseconds per event; autoencoder variants need modest GPU.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature and widely deployed: fraud screening, AML transaction monitoring (regulatorily mandated in banking), IT/observability anomaly detection (Datadog/Dynatrace-class products embed it), industrial process monitoring; the unsupervised→supervised graduation pattern is standard fraud-team practice.
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M1 feature pipelines, M5/M6 residuals, M17/M24 embeddings, M22 graph features; its triage-feedback loop is the label factory for M1/M32; streaming infrastructure shared with M34; alert monitoring shared with all production monitoring stages.
15. **Representative use cases** — Payment fraud screening, AML monitoring, network intrusion, sensor/equipment fault detection, data-quality anomaly alarms, metrics observability.
16. **Production failure modes** — Alert fatigue (precision decay → investigators ignore the queue → the system is dead while dashboards look fine; monitor investigator hit-rate); adversarial drift (fraudsters probe and adapt — retrain cadence must beat adversary cadence); normal-behavior drift after product changes (retrain triggers on drift monitors); threshold rot as volumes grow.
17. **Tooling landscape** — PyOD (the reference library, 50+ algorithms), scikit-learn (IsolationForest/LOF), River (streaming), Merlion/Anomalib (time-series/vision-specific), ADBench (benchmark).
18. **Key references** — Liu, Ting & Zhou, "Isolation Forest" (ICDM 2008); Breunig et al., "LOF" (SIGMOD 2000); Chandola, Banerjee & Kumar, "Anomaly Detection: A Survey" (ACM CS 2009); Han et al., "ADBench" (NeurIPS 2022); Guha et al., "Robust Random Cut Forest" (ICML 2016); Adams & MacKay, "Bayesian Online Changepoint Detection" (2007).

## Family F — Clustering & segmentation

<a id="m13"></a>
### M13. Clustering & segmentation

1. **Name + aliases** — Unsupervised segmentation; cluster analysis; customer/behavioral segmentation; k-means/GMM/HDBSCAN pipelines; embedding-based clustering; topic-like grouping of any embedded objects.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Partition entities into coherent groups from feature or embedding similarity without labels, then *profile and name* the groups so the business can differentiate treatment by segment — where the profiling/activation step, not the algorithm, determines the value.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Clustering/segmentation; density estimation; structure discovery; cohort construction feeding downstream targeting.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular (classic segmentation); embeddings of text/image/behavior sequences (modern default); mixed-type data (requires care: Gower distance, k-prototypes, or embed-first).
5. **Component pipeline** — Objective framing (segmentation *for what decision*?) → entity-level feature assembly (RFM-style aggregates, or embeddings from M17/M24/M4) → scaling + dimensionality reduction (PCA; UMAP for density methods, with caveats) → algorithm + k selection → stability validation (bootstrap/reruns) → cluster profiling (distinguishing features per cluster, personas, sizes, value) → naming + stakeholder workshop → activation (segment tags written to CRM/CDP) → drift monitoring and scheduled refresh with segment-mapping continuity.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — **k-means/k-means++** (Lloyd 1982; Arthur & Vassilvitskii 2007) — the scalable default for globular clusters; **Gaussian mixtures** (soft assignments, elliptical shapes); **HDBSCAN** (Campello, Moulavi & Sander 2013; McInnes et al. 2017) — varying-density clusters + a noise bucket, the modern default on embeddings; agglomerative for dendrogram-driven hierarchy; k-prototypes/Gower for mixed types; spectral for graph-like similarity; latent-class models when statistical inference on segments is needed. Selection: k-means for large-n tabular simplicity; HDBSCAN for embeddings and when "not everything must belong to a cluster"; GMM when soft membership feeds downstream models.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Decision-relevant aggregates only (kitchen-sink features produce unactionable clusters); standardization (mandatory — distance-based methods); log transforms for heavy-tailed spend/usage; embeddings for unstructured inputs; deliberate feature weighting as an explicit business choice; leave out the outcome you plan to act on (else segments are just outcome bins).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Clustering noise (validate cluster *existence* — hopkins statistic, stability under resampling — before interpreting); k chosen by eyeballing an elbow (use silhouette/BIC/stability jointly, and accept business-tractability as a legitimate criterion); scale dominance by unstandardized features; UMAP-then-cluster distortions (UMAP distorts density; tune min_dist/n_neighbors, sanity-check in original space); mixed-type distance incoherence; population drift silently remapping segment IDs between refreshes (safeguard: centroid-matching continuity, versioned segment definitions); the perennial trap — segments that are statistically fine but operationally meaningless (safeguard: profiling review with the teams who must act on them).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Internal: silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, BIC (GMM), relative density (HDBSCAN); stability: adjusted Rand index across bootstrap reruns; external: downstream utility — do segments discriminate on outcomes/response the business cares about (the only metric stakeholders feel); size/coverage practicality checks.
10. **Explainability methods** — Cluster profile cards (per-cluster feature means vs population, distinguishing features by effect size); a supervised surrogate (train M1 to predict cluster membership, read its SHAP) — the neat trick that turns any clustering explainable; 2-D projection maps for orientation (labeled as illustrative).
11. **Data requirements** — Thousands of entities upward; features covering the behavior of interest; no labels; refresh cadence matched to how fast the population moves.
12. **Compute profile** — CPU; k-means scales to 10⁸ rows (MiniBatch); HDBSCAN to ~10⁶–10⁷ with approximate modes; embedding generation is the expensive part when used.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Maximally mature as technique (decades of marketing-science use); ubiquitous in CRM/CDP practice; embedding-based clustering is the growing variant (it underlies BERTopic-style topic modeling, M16, and log/ticket grouping).
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M1 feature pipelines and M4/M17/M24 embeddings; its segment outputs feed M1 (as features), M11 (heterogeneity priors), M14 (cold-start cohorts), M9 (stratification); the profile/naming stage is a reusable reporting pattern; supervised-surrogate explanation reuses M1+SHAP.
15. **Representative use cases** — Customer segmentation, store/site clustering for assortment, ticket/log grouping, SKU portfolio structure, patient phenotyping, fraud-pattern grouping (with M12).
16. **Production failure modes** — Segment drift (population moves, names stop being true); activation rot (segments computed but no downstream system consumes them — measure activation, not just refresh); refresh discontinuity breaking downstream automations keyed to segment IDs.
17. **Tooling landscape** — scikit-learn, hdbscan/fast_hdbscan, umap-learn, kmodes (k-prototypes), sentence-transformers (embeddings), BERTopic (text-specific composition).
18. **Key references** — Jain, "Data clustering: 50 years beyond K-means" (PRL 2010); Campello, Moulavi & Sander, "Density-Based Clustering Based on Hierarchical Density Estimates" (PAKDD 2013); McInnes, Healy & Astels, "hdbscan" (JOSS 2017); Arthur & Vassilvitskii, "k-means++" (SODA 2007); Kaufman & Rousseeuw, "Finding Groups in Data" (1990).

## Family G — Recommendation

<a id="m14"></a>
### M14. Recommendation & personalization (collaborative filtering → two-tower retrieval + ranking cascade)

1. **Name + aliases** — Recommender systems; collaborative filtering (CF); matrix factorization (MF); candidate-generation + ranking cascade; two-tower/dual-encoder retrieval; sequence-aware recommendation (session-based, SASRec/BERT4Rec-style); "personalization engine"; next-best-offer (its CRM cousin).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Learn user and item representations from interaction history to (a) retrieve a few hundred plausible candidates from a corpus of millions and (b) rank them with a feature-rich model calibrated to engagement/value, with exploration handled by bandits (M26) — spanning the small-scale batch-MF pipeline and the large-scale cascade as two maturity stages of one methodology.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Recommendation; top-k ranking; implicit-feedback matrix completion; sequence prediction (next item); complementary/similar-item retrieval.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — User–item interaction events (implicit feedback dominates: views, clicks, purchases); item/user metadata (tabular + text/image via embeddings); session sequences; graph structure (user–item bipartite; M22 methods apply — PinSage).
5. **Component pipeline** — Event extraction + interaction-matrix/sequence assembly (with strict event-time discipline) → negative sampling design (implicit feedback has no explicit negatives) → (stage 1: retrieval) train MF or two-tower encoders; index item vectors in ANN → (stage 2: ranking) feature-rich model (GBM or deep) over user×candidate features including retrieval scores → (stage 3: policy layer) diversity/business rules, exploration (M26), calibration → offline evaluation on temporally held-out interactions → online A/B validation (M9 — the only trustworthy evaluation) → serving (real-time feature fetch + ANN + rank, at p99 latency budgets) → monitoring (engagement, coverage, popularity bias) and continuous retraining.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Retrieval: item-kNN co-occurrence (still a strong baseline), implicit-feedback MF/ALS (Hu, Koren & Volinsky 2008), BPR (Rendle et al. 2009), **two-tower neural retrieval** (YouTube: Covington et al., RecSys 2016) with in-batch negatives, EASE (Steck 2019 — embarrassingly effective linear baseline); ANN: HNSW (Malkov & Yashunin 2018), FAISS (Johnson et al. 2019), ScaNN. Ranking: GBMs on engineered features (widespread mid-scale), deep CTR models (Wide&Deep 2016, DeepFM 2017, DLRM — Naumov et al. 2019, DCN-v2), multi-task heads (click/watch/purchase). Sequence-aware: SASRec (Kang & McAuley 2018), BERT4Rec (2019), session-based GRU4Rec (2016). Graph: PinSage (Ying et al., KDD 2018 — 3B nodes/18B edges in production, +150% hit-rate over the prior Pinterest baseline). Selection: start with EASE/ALS + GBM ranker; go two-tower + deep ranker when corpus and traffic justify; add sequence models when recency/order carries the signal (media, session commerce).
7. **Feature engineering practices** — User aggregates (M1-style windows over interactions), item popularity/recency priors, user×item cross features (category affinity), sequence features (last-n items, time gaps), retrieval-score features, context (device, time, page); embedding features from content towers for cold-start items; careful *time-travel-free* feature joins (feature state as of the impression, not as of training).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Temporal leakage in interaction splits (random splits leak future taste; safeguard: leave-last-out or timestamp splits); negative-sampling bias (in-batch negatives over-penalize popular items; correct with sampling-bias adjustment — logQ correction); train/serve skew between offline features and real-time serving state (feature-store parity); position/exposure bias — logs only contain what was shown (safeguard: propensity-weighted training/eval, exploration traffic); feedback loops — the model trains on interactions its predecessor caused, amplifying popularity (safeguard: exploration quota, popularity debiasing, holdout traffic); offline–online metric divergence (never ship on offline gains alone — validate in M9); cold-start users/items routed without content features fail silently.
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Offline: recall@k/NDCG@k on temporally held-out interactions per stage (retrieval measured on recall@few-hundred; ranking on NDCG/AUC), popularity-stratified breakdowns, coverage/diversity metrics; counterfactual/OPE estimates where propensities are logged; online: A/B on engagement + long-term value proxies + guardrails (diversity, creator equity). The offline→online gap is the field's defining epistemic problem — treat offline metrics as pre-screening only.
10. **Explainability methods** — "Because you watched X" (nearest-neighbor rationale — an interface feature more than a model property); per-recommendation feature attributions from the ranker (SHAP on GBM rankers); aggregate transparency reports (what share of exposure goes to which content classes) for policy stakeholders.
11. **Data requirements** — Interaction volume is the binding constraint: MF/kNN workable from ~10⁵ interactions; deep cascades want 10⁷+; item metadata mandatory for cold start; freshness matters (session recsys needs minutes-fresh features).
12. **Compute profile** — Retrieval training: GPU hours (two-tower) or CPU (ALS/EASE); ANN serving: memory-bound CPU at ms latency; deep rankers: GPU training, optimized CPU/GPU inference; the large-scale cascade is among the most infrastructure-heavy methodologies here — which is why the batch-MF variant persists at mid-scale.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mainstream at consumer scale with unusually well-documented value: Netflix reports ~80% of hours streamed are influenced by recommendations and estimated the system's value at ~$1B/year via churn reduction (Gomez-Uribe & Hunt, ACM TMIS 2015 — well-sourced); the oft-quoted "35% of Amazon revenue from recommendations" is a McKinsey-attributed analyst estimate from the early 2010s about web sales, widely repeated without a verifiable primary source — treat as apocryphal precision (verification 2025); PinSage/production GNN recsys documented at Pinterest, Alibaba, Uber (2018–2024). Ubiquitous in e-commerce/media; the two-tower cascade is the de facto industry architecture (Covington 2016 onward).
14. **Reusable components** — Embedding + ANN retrieval is shared verbatim with M17 (semantic search) and M23 (blocking); its ranking stage *is* M15 with engagement labels; exploration is M26; evaluation-of-record is M9; feature pipelines are M1's; sequence models share architecture with M16/M20 transformers; graph variants are M22.
15. **Representative use cases** — Product/content recommendation, next-best-action in CRM, cross-sell/upsell placement, email/push personalization, related-item widgets, feed ranking.
16. **Production failure modes** — Popularity-bias feedback loops and filter bubbles; stale-embedding drift for fast-moving catalogs; cannibalization masked by engagement metrics (measure net value in M9); cold-start decay after catalog refreshes; monitoring = engagement by cohort, coverage/diversity, embedding-drift, and guardrailed long-term value proxies.
17. **Tooling landscape** — implicit (ALS/BPR), LightFM, TorchRec/DLRM, TensorFlow Recommenders (two-tower), RecBole (research breadth), FAISS/HNSWlib/ScaNN/Milvus (ANN), Merlin/NVTabular (pipelines); managed: Amazon Personalize, Vertex AI Recommendations.
18. **Key references** — Hu, Koren & Volinsky, "Collaborative Filtering for Implicit Feedback Datasets" (ICDM 2008); Rendle et al., "BPR" (UAI 2009); Covington, Adams & Sargin, "Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations" (RecSys 2016); Ying et al., "Graph Convolutional Neural Networks for Web-Scale Recommender Systems" (KDD 2018); Gomez-Uribe & Hunt, "The Netflix Recommender System" (ACM TMIS 2015); Steck, "Embarrassingly Shallow Autoencoders" (WWW 2019); Kang & McAuley, "Self-Attentive Sequential Recommendation" (ICDM 2018).

## Family H — Learning-to-rank

<a id="m15"></a>
### M15. Learning-to-rank

1. **Name + aliases** — LTR; ranking models; search ranking; LambdaMART-style listwise GBM ranking; pointwise/pairwise/listwise learning; re-ranking (the LLM-era retrieval stage-two).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Learn to order a candidate list for a query/context so that graded-relevance metrics like NDCG improve — a supervised methodology whose unit is the *query group*, not the row — using relevance labels from editorial judgment or (more commonly) debiased click logs.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Ranking; top-k ordering; relevance modeling; re-ranking of retrieval outputs.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular query–document–context feature vectors grouped by query; text (via lexical/semantic match features and cross-encoders); click/impression logs.
5. **Component pipeline** — Query-group data assembly (query, candidates, features, labels, positions) → label acquisition: editorial grades (expensive, unbiased) or click-derived labels (cheap, position-biased) with bias correction → feature engineering (query, document, and query–document match features) → group-aware training (LambdaMART or neural) → offline evaluation (NDCG/MRR with grouped splits) → interleaving or A/B online validation → serving inside the retrieval→rank cascade at tight latency → monitoring (query-segment quality, click models) and periodic label refresh.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — **LambdaMART** (Burges 2010) — gradient-boosted trees with a listwise-informed pairwise objective; the production default for a decade+ (native in LightGBM/XGBoost `rank:ndcg`/`lambdarank`); neural LTR (TF-Ranking, allRank) — competitive mainly with raw-text features; **cross-encoder re-rankers** (monoBERT — Nogueira & Cho 2019; monoT5; LLM re-rankers) — the modern stage-two over M17 retrieval, large quality gains at high inference cost; unbiased LTR: inverse-propensity-scored objectives with position-bias estimation (Joachims et al., WSDM 2017; Wang et al. 2018). Selection: LambdaMART for feature-rich enterprise ranking; cross-encoders when text understanding dominates; IPS variants whenever training on clicks.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Query features (length, intent class, historical CTR), document features (popularity, freshness, quality scores), match features (BM25, term overlap, embedding cosine from M17, field-specific matches), context features (user, device, locale), and historical engagement aggregates per query–document pair; feature logging at impression time to prevent train/serve skew.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Position bias in click labels (clicks concentrate on top slots regardless of relevance; safeguard: propensity estimation via result randomization or intervention harvesting, IPS-weighted training); selection bias — only retrieved documents get labels (safeguard: exploration traffic, label the retrieval gap); random splits across query groups leaking query-level signal (grouped splits by query, temporal splits for logs); presentation feedback loops (the ranker trains on its own exposure choices — same safeguards as M14); NDCG computed on truncated candidate sets flattering the model (evaluate against the full judgment pool where possible).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Offline: NDCG@k, MRR, MAP on grouped/temporal splits; per-query-segment breakdowns (head vs torso vs tail queries); online: **interleaving** (team-draft) — far more sensitive than A/B for ranking deltas (Chapelle et al. 2012) — then A/B for business metrics; judgment sets refreshed on a calendar because relevance drifts.
10. **Explainability methods** — SHAP on LambdaMART features (why document A over B for this query); per-feature win/loss analysis between ranker versions; for cross-encoders, attribution is weak — rely on behavioral probes and side-by-side judgment.
11. **Data requirements** — Thousands of queries with graded judgments, or click logs at scale with propensity instrumentation; label refresh cadence tied to content drift.
12. **Compute profile** — LambdaMART: CPU, cheap; cross-encoders: GPU inference on the top-k only (that's the cascade's economic logic); latency budgets (tens of ms) shape architecture more than training cost.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature: LambdaMART-class ranking has powered commercial web/e-commerce/enterprise search since ~2010 (Yahoo! Learning-to-Rank Challenge 2010 established GBM dominance); cross-encoder re-ranking became standard alongside RAG stacks (2023 onward); every search vendor (Elastic, OpenSearch, Algolia, Vespa) ships LTR hooks.
14. **Reusable components** — Is M1's training/explanation stack with a group-aware objective; consumes M17 retrieval scores as features and serves as its re-rank stage; the same cascade slots into M14 and M18; unbiased-learning components (propensities, IPS) shared with M11/M26/M27; interleaving evaluation shared with M9.
15. **Representative use cases** — Site/product search ranking, enterprise document search, job/candidate matching, ad ranking, RAG context re-ranking, support-article ranking.
16. **Production failure modes** — Relevance drift as content/query mix shifts; judgment-set rot; feedback-loop entrenchment of historically clicked results; per-segment regressions hidden in aggregate NDCG; monitoring = query-segment dashboards, abandoned-query rates, interleaving canaries.
17. **Tooling landscape** — LightGBM/XGBoost rankers, Elasticsearch/OpenSearch LTR plugins, Vespa (native), TF-Ranking, allRank, sentence-transformers cross-encoders, Quaerite/RRE (relevance testing).
18. **Key references** — Burges, "From RankNet to LambdaRank to LambdaMART: An Overview" (MSR-TR 2010); Liu, "Learning to Rank for Information Retrieval" (2009); Joachims, Swaminathan & Schnabel, "Unbiased Learning-to-Rank with Biased Feedback" (WSDM 2017); Chapelle et al., "Large-Scale Validation of Interleaving" (2012); Nogueira & Cho, "Passage Re-ranking with BERT" (2019); Chapelle & Chang, "Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge Overview" (JMLR 2011).

## Family I — Text & language

Five dossiers: the classic supervised pipeline (M16), the embedding-retrieval substrate (M17), RAG (M18), prompt-based prediction (M19), and fine-tuning/distillation (M20). They share components heavily but their pipelines, cost profiles, and failure modes genuinely differ.

<a id="m16"></a>
### M16. Classic supervised NLP (text classification, NER, topic modeling)

1. **Name + aliases** — Traditional/classic NLP; supervised text analytics; text classification; named-entity recognition (NER) / sequence labeling; information extraction; topic modeling (its unsupervised sibling, folded here as the same pipeline minus labels).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Convert text into features or contextual embeddings and train compact supervised models (or fine-tuned small transformers) for high-throughput, low-latency, deterministic-cost text prediction — the pipeline of choice when volume is high, labels exist, and the task is stable.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Classification (single/multi-label), sequence labeling/extraction (NER, PII detection), sentiment/intent, topic discovery (unsupervised), text similarity/matching.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Text (short-form: tickets, reviews, messages; long-form via chunking); metadata side-features.
5. **Component pipeline** — Corpus extraction + cleaning (encoding, boilerplate/dedup, language ID) → labeling (annotation guidelines, inter-annotator agreement measurement, or weak supervision via M32) → representation: TF-IDF/n-grams (linear path) or pretrained-transformer encoding (BERT-class path) → training: linear/GBM on sparse features, or fine-tuned encoder (this "fine-tune a small BERT" step predates and differs from M20's LLM fine-tuning in scale and intent) → evaluation on held-out annotated data → error analysis by slice → serving at high QPS → drift monitoring (vocabulary/topic drift) + annotation refresh loop.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Linear path: TF-IDF + logistic regression/linear SVM (fastText — Joulin et al. 2017 — as the speed benchmark) — still remarkably competitive on long-document classification at a fraction of the cost; Transformer path: BERT (Devlin et al. 2019), RoBERTa, DeBERTa-v3, and distilled variants (DistilBERT — Sanh et al. 2019) fine-tuned per task — the accuracy default since 2019; NER: BiLSTM-CRF (legacy), transformer token classification, spaCy pipelines; Topic modeling: LDA (Blei, Ng & Jordan 2003) → **BERTopic** (Grootendorst 2022: embeddings → UMAP → HDBSCAN → class-TF-IDF), the modern default that literally composes M17+M13. Selection vs M19: classic wins on cost at volume, latency, determinism, and data-governance; M19 wins on zero-label start-up and task flexibility — the standard modern pattern is M19 to bootstrap labels, M16/M20-distilled to run at scale.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — n-grams/char-grams (robust to typos), domain lexicons, structural features (length, caps ratio, URLs), metadata joins; for transformers: chunking strategy for long docs, domain-adaptive pretraining when vocabulary is exotic; label-space design (taxonomy depth) is the highest-leverage "feature" decision.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Annotation inconsistency (measure κ/agreement before trusting labels; refine guidelines); train/test contamination via near-duplicate texts (dedup before splitting — templated enterprise text duplicates heavily); temporal leakage in evolving corpora (temporal splits; vocabulary of the future leaks trends); label drift as business categories change (versioned taxonomies); sampling bias — annotators see convenience samples, production sees everything (stratified sampling from live traffic); shortcut learning (models keying on boilerplate/source artifacts; probe with counterfactual edits); class imbalance at taxonomy leaves.
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Precision/recall/F1 per class (macro + weighted), confusion matrices, PR curves for triage thresholds; span-level F1 for NER (exact + partial match); slice-based evaluation (per source/language/period); calibration when scores gate automation; human-agreement ceiling as the reference; topic models: coherence (NPMI) + human-legibility review.
10. **Explainability methods** — Linear coefficients/n-gram weights (fully transparent); attention/integrated-gradients for transformers (indicative); LIME/SHAP-text for local rationales; per-class exemplar review — stakeholders receive a taxonomy report: volumes, trends, exemplars per category.
11. **Data requirements** — Linear: a few hundred labels per class; transformer fine-tune: ~1k–10k labels per task (few-shot with M19 bootstrap below that); topic modeling: 10⁴+ documents for stable structure.
12. **Compute profile** — Linear: negligible; small-transformer fine-tune: single GPU-hours; inference: CPU-feasible (distilled) or small GPU at high QPS — orders of magnitude cheaper per document than M19 at volume.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature and ubiquitous (ticket routing, content moderation, CRM enrichment run on it industry-wide); the LLM era has *shifted its role* rather than displaced it: prompt-based bootstrap + distill-to-small-model is the emergent standard economics (see M20); spaCy/Hugging Face download counts evidence massive practitioner adoption (qualitative; exact shares not survey-verified).
14. **Reusable components** — Annotation/guideline/QA components shared with M21/M24/M32; encoder embeddings feed M13/M17; its evaluation harness (per-class F1, slice analysis) is the template for M19's output checking; serving/monitoring shared with M1; distillation target for M20.
15. **Representative use cases** — Ticket/email routing and triage, review/sentiment analytics, PII detection, compliance surveillance, resume/CV parsing, voice-of-customer topic tracking (with M25 upstream).
16. **Production failure modes** — Vocabulary/topic drift (new products, slang; monitor OOV rates and score drift); taxonomy rot (categories no longer match operations); silent upstream format changes (email footers, templates); retraining on model-influenced routing outcomes (feedback loop — keep human-labeled random samples flowing).
17. **Tooling landscape** — Hugging Face transformers/datasets, spaCy, scikit-learn, fastText, BERTopic, Label Studio/Prodigy (annotation), SetFit (few-shot encoder tuning).
18. **Key references** — Devlin et al., "BERT" (NAACL 2019); Joulin et al., "Bag of Tricks for Efficient Text Classification" (2017); Blei, Ng & Jordan, "Latent Dirichlet Allocation" (JMLR 2003); Grootendorst, "BERTopic" (2022); Sanh et al., "DistilBERT" (2019); Ratner et al., "Snorkel" (VLDB 2017).

<a id="m17"></a>
### M17. Embeddings + vector retrieval (semantic search)

1. **Name + aliases** — Dense retrieval; semantic search; vector search; embedding-based retrieval (EBR); bi-encoder retrieval; hybrid (lexical+dense) search; the "vector database" methodology.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Encode objects (text chunks, products, images, entities) into a vector space where semantic similarity is geometric proximity, index the vectors for approximate nearest-neighbor search, and serve similarity queries at scale — the substrate methodology beneath RAG, semantic search, deduplication, and recommendation retrieval.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Retrieval/matching; similarity search; deduplication candidate generation; zero-shot classification (nearest labeled neighbor); clustering support (M13).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Text foremost; images/audio (CLIP-class encoders); any entity representable by an encoder; multimodal joint spaces.
5. **Component pipeline** — Corpus extraction + normalization → **chunking** (the quietly decisive design stage: size/overlap/structure-aware splitting) → embedding-model selection (benchmark on *your* retrieval task, not just MTEB rank) → batch encoding + upsert into ANN index (with metadata for filtering) → query-side encoding + hybrid combination with lexical BM25 (reciprocal-rank fusion) → optional re-ranking (M15 cross-encoders) → retrieval evaluation on a golden query set → serving (low-latency ANN + filters) → index freshness pipeline + embedding-version management → monitoring (recall canaries, latency, drift).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Encoders: Sentence-BERT lineage (Reimers & Gurevych 2019), E5/GTE/BGE open families, OpenAI/Cohere/Voyage commercial APIs; MTEB (Muennighoff et al. 2023) as the public benchmark with the caveat of overfitting-to-benchmark; late-interaction ColBERT (Khattab & Zaharia 2020) for quality between bi- and cross-encoders. ANN: **HNSW** (Malkov & Yashunin, TPAMI 2018) — the default graph index; IVF-PQ (FAISS — Johnson et al. 2019) for memory-constrained scale; DiskANN for billion-scale on disk. Hybrid: BM25 + dense with RRF — the robust default because lexical catches exact identifiers that dense misses (BEIR findings — Thakur et al. 2021: BM25 is a strong zero-shot baseline; dense models trained in-domain win, out-of-domain often lose).
7. **Feature engineering practices** — The "features" are chunking + metadata design: structure-aware chunking (headings, tables kept intact), overlap tuning, title/context prepending to chunks, metadata fields for filtered retrieval (date, source, ACL), embedding-input templating (query vs passage prefixes for asymmetric models); document hierarchy (parent-child chunks: retrieve small, return large).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Evaluating retrieval by vibes (safeguard: build a golden set of query→relevant-chunk judgments before tuning anything); embedding-version mismatch between index and queries after model upgrades (version-stamp vectors; reindex atomically); ACL/permission leakage through the index (filter at query time with security trimming — a genuine data-security class of bug unique to this methodology); stale-index drift vs source documents (freshness SLAs, CDC pipelines); chunk-boundary information loss (structure-aware chunking); ANN recall degradation at high filter selectivity (test filtered-recall explicitly); domain shift — benchmark-great encoders failing on jargon (in-domain golden-set evaluation; consider fine-tuning the encoder).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Recall@k and MRR/NDCG on the golden set (retrieval is a ranking problem — M15 metrics apply); per-query-type slices (identifiers vs conceptual vs multilingual); ANN-vs-exact recall audits (index quality separate from embedding quality); latency percentiles under production filters; A/B on downstream task success where it feeds M18.
10. **Explainability methods** — Retrieved-evidence display (the results are their own explanation); similarity-score transparency; overlap highlighting; for debugging: nearest-neighbor inspection of embedding space, UMAP maps of the corpus.
11. **Data requirements** — Corpus of any size (10³–10⁹ objects); no labels needed to start; a golden judgment set (~100+ queries) is the key data artifact to create; document freshness pipeline for living corpora.
12. **Compute profile** — Encoding: GPU batch (or API cost) proportional to corpus size — the dominant one-time cost, re-incurred on every embedding-model change; serving: memory-bound CPU (HNSW) at single-digit-ms; re-rankers add GPU inference on top-k.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mainstream and rapidly grown 2023–2025 as RAG's substrate (RAG at 51% of enterprise GenAI deployments — Menlo Ventures 2024 — implies vector retrieval at comparable penetration); mature core algorithms (HNSW 2018, FAISS 2017); the vector-database product category (Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant, Milvus, pgvector) consolidated into general databases adding vector types — evidence of infrastructure-level maturity.
14. **Reusable components** — Supplies the retrieval stage of M18 (RAG) and M14 (recsys candidate generation); blocking for M23 (entity resolution); representation for M13 (clustering) and M12 (embedding-distance anomaly); pairs with M15 re-ranking; its golden-set evaluation harness is shared with M15/M18.
15. **Representative use cases** — Enterprise knowledge search, support-article retrieval, similar-product/similar-case lookup, deduplication candidates, semantic log search, image similarity.
16. **Production failure modes** — Index/source drift; embedding-model deprecations (API vendors) forcing reindex; recall decay as corpus composition shifts; permission-model changes not propagated to filters; monitoring = golden-set recall canaries in CI, freshness lag, filtered-recall spot checks.
17. **Tooling landscape** — FAISS, HNSWlib, Qdrant, Weaviate, Milvus, pgvector/pgvectorscale, Elasticsearch/OpenSearch kNN, Vespa; sentence-transformers; MTEB/BEIR benchmarks.
18. **Key references** — Reimers & Gurevych, "Sentence-BERT" (EMNLP 2019); Malkov & Yashunin, "Efficient and robust approximate nearest neighbor search using HNSW" (TPAMI 2018); Johnson, Douze & Jégou, "Billion-scale similarity search with GPUs" (2019); Thakur et al., "BEIR" (NeurIPS 2021); Karpukhin et al., "Dense Passage Retrieval" (EMNLP 2020); Muennighoff et al., "MTEB" (2023).

<a id="m18"></a>
### M18. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

1. **Name + aliases** — RAG; grounded generation; retrieval-augmented LLMs; "chat with your documents"; enterprise knowledge assistants; agentic/iterative RAG (its multi-step variant, bridging to M28).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Ground an LLM's generation in retrieved enterprise content by injecting top-k retrieved passages into the prompt, so answers reflect current, permissioned, citable internal knowledge rather than the model's frozen parametric memory — the dominant enterprise GenAI architecture.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Grounded question answering; summarization-with-sources; knowledge-assistant generation; extraction-with-evidence.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Documents/text (wikis, policies, tickets, contracts); tables and structured data via text-to-SQL or serialized snippets; images/diagrams via multimodal models; conversation context.
5. **Component pipeline** — Corpus ingestion (connectors, parsing — M21 for scanned docs) → chunking/embedding/indexing (M17 wholesale) → query processing (rewriting, decomposition, intent routing) → retrieval (hybrid + filters + ACL trimming) → re-ranking (M15) → prompt assembly (context packing, citation scaffolding, instruction framing) → generation with grounding instructions → post-generation verification (citation validity, groundedness checks) → response with sources → feedback capture (thumbs, escalations) → evaluation harness (retrieval + generation metrics on a curated QA set) → monitoring (groundedness drift, retrieval quality, cost/latency).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Retrieval: everything in M17 (hybrid BM25+dense+RRF as the robust default) plus query rewriting/HyDE (Gao et al. 2022) and multi-hop decomposition; Re-rank: cross-encoders/LLM rerankers (M15); Generation: frontier or strong open LLMs with grounding prompts; Advanced patterns: Self-RAG (Asai et al. 2023), corrective RAG, GraphRAG (Microsoft 2024 — entity-graph summarization for corpus-global questions), agentic retrieval loops (M28); Verification: NLI-based groundedness scoring, citation-checking, LLM-as-judge with audits. Root reference: Lewis et al., "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (NeurIPS 2020).
7. **Feature engineering practices** — RAG's equivalents: chunking strategy, metadata schema, query-rewrite templates, context-window packing policy (order, dedup, token budget), prompt scaffolds mandating citation and abstention ("answer only from the provided context; say 'not found' otherwise"), and corpus curation (the highest-leverage activity — garbage corpus, garbage assistant).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Ungrounded generation despite retrieval (hallucination; safeguard: groundedness verification, citation enforcement, abstention prompting, temperature discipline); retrieval failure masquerading as generation failure (evaluate the stages *separately* — most "LLM was wrong" incidents are "retrieval returned nothing relevant"); permission leakage (ACL trimming at retrieval time — the security-critical stage); stale corpus answering with expired policy (freshness SLAs, doc-date surfacing); prompt injection via retrieved content (retrieved text is untrusted input; sanitize, instruct, and privilege-separate); eval contamination (golden QA sets leaking into few-shot prompts); context-window overpacking degrading answer quality ("lost in the middle" — Liu et al. 2023).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Two-layer: retrieval metrics (recall@k/NDCG on golden queries, M17's harness) and generation metrics (faithfulness/groundedness, answer relevance, citation precision — RAGAS-style; Es et al. 2023) scored by LLM-judges *calibrated against periodic human audits*; end-to-end task success on a curated QA set with versioned re-runs per change (any change: chunking, model, prompt); online: deflection/resolution rates, user feedback, escalation analysis; guardrail: measured hallucination rate on adversarial "not in corpus" questions.
10. **Explainability methods** — Citations to source passages (the defining explainability property — users can verify); retrieval-trace inspection for debugging; confidence via abstention behavior; stakeholders receive answers-with-sources plus corpus-coverage reports ("what the assistant can and cannot answer").
11. **Data requirements** — A curated, deduplicated, permission-mapped corpus (quality ≫ volume); a golden QA evaluation set (~100–500 questions) as the core engineering asset; feedback instrumentation; no model-training data needed (the point vs M20).
12. **Compute profile** — Dominated by LLM inference cost/latency per query (API or GPU serving); retrieval adds single-digit ms; re-rankers add GPU; corpus embedding is a recurring batch cost — overall far cheaper to *build* than M20 but with per-query costs that scale linearly with usage.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — The dominant enterprise GenAI pattern: 51% of enterprise AI deployments in production used RAG in 2024, up from 31% in 2023, vs 9% fine-tuning (Menlo Ventures, State of Generative AI in the Enterprise 2024; corroborated by an independent 600-IT-decision-maker survey); among early adopters 70–86% report RAG-style grounding layers (McKinsey/k2view figures as compiled 2025). Engineering maturity is *lower* than adoption: evaluation discipline and groundedness monitoring remain uneven (qualitative practitioner consensus).
14. **Reusable components** — Composes M17 (retrieval) + M15 (re-rank) + M19 (generation-side prompting) + M21 (document parsing) end-to-end; its evaluation harness pattern extends M17's golden sets; agentic variants hand off to M28; feedback loops feed M32 (labeling) and M20 (fine-tune data).
15. **Representative use cases** — Internal knowledge assistants (HR/IT/policy), customer-support copilots, contract/document Q&A, analyst research assistants, code-base Q&A, regulatory-compliance lookup.
16. **Production failure modes** — Corpus rot (the #1 long-run killer — stale/contradictory documents; ownership + freshness pipelines); silent retrieval degradation after reindexing; model-version behavior shifts (pin + regression-test); cost creep with usage growth; confident wrong answers eroding trust faster than "not found" ever would (bias toward abstention); prompt-injection attacks via documents; monitoring = groundedness sampling, retrieval canaries, cost/latency, feedback triage.
17. **Tooling landscape** — LlamaIndex, LangChain/LangGraph, Haystack; RAGAS/TruLens/DeepEval (evaluation); vector stores per M17; managed: Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases, Azure AI Search, Vertex AI Search, GraphRAG.
18. **Key references** — Lewis et al., "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks" (NeurIPS 2020); Gao et al., "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models: A Survey" (2023); Asai et al., "Self-RAG" (2023); Es et al., "RAGAS" (2023); Liu et al., "Lost in the Middle" (TACL 2024); Edge et al., "From Local to Global: A GraphRAG Approach" (2024); Menlo Ventures, "The State of Generative AI in the Enterprise" (2024, 2025).

<a id="m19"></a>
### M19. Prompt-based LLM classification / extraction (LLM-as-component)

1. **Name + aliases** — Prompt engineering as a methodology; zero-/few-shot prediction; in-context learning (ICL); structured-output extraction; "LLM-as-a-pipeline-step"; LLM-as-judge (its evaluation-flavored variant).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Use a pretrained instruction-following LLM, steered only by a prompt (instructions, schema, few-shot exemplars), as a drop-in prediction/extraction component that converts unstructured input into structured output with zero training data — trading per-call cost and nondeterminism for instant capability.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Classification, extraction (entities, fields, relations), normalization/standardization, summarization, transformation (format conversion), scoring/judging, synthetic-data generation (feeding M32/M33).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Text foremost; images/documents via multimodal models (overlapping M21); tabular rows serialized to text for small-n reasoning tasks.
5. **Component pipeline** — Task specification (label definitions, output JSON schema, edge-case policy — the "annotation guideline" reborn as a prompt) → prompt construction (instructions + schema + few-shot exemplars chosen deliberately) → structured-output enforcement (JSON mode/function calling/constrained decoding) → validation layer (schema check, business-rule check, confidence/abstention handling) → **evaluation on a labeled golden set** (non-negotiable — prompt changes are model changes) → batch or online serving with retry/fallback policy → drift monitoring (model-version behavior, output distribution) → economics review: at sustained volume, distill to M16/M20.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Frontier APIs (Claude, GPT, Gemini) for hard/ambiguous tasks; small open models (Llama/Qwen/Mistral-class) self-hosted for cost at volume; technique canon: few-shot ICL (Brown et al., GPT-3, NeurIPS 2020), chain-of-thought for reasoning-heavy extraction (Wei et al. 2022), self-consistency (Wang et al. 2022), constrained decoding (Outlines/JSON schema modes); LLM-as-judge with known biases — position, verbosity, self-preference (Zheng et al., MT-Bench 2023) — requiring human-calibration audits. Selection vs M16: prompt-based wins below ~10³–10⁴ calls/day or when labels don't exist yet; classic/distilled wins at sustained volume (crossover economics are task-specific; estimate, not source-verified).
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Reborn as prompt design: label definitions with counter-examples, exemplar selection (coverage of edge cases; dynamic retrieval of similar exemplars via M17), input formatting (structure preserved, noise stripped), output-schema design (flat, enumerated, nullable fields), decomposition of multi-part tasks into chained single-purpose prompts.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Unmeasured accuracy (the field's original sin; safeguard: golden-set evaluation before any production claim); prompt drift — edits without regression tests (version prompts; CI evaluation on every change); model-version drift — provider updates silently change behavior (pin versions; re-evaluate on upgrades); schema violations and hallucinated fields (constrained decoding + validators + null-when-absent instructions); few-shot leakage — golden-set items reused as exemplars (strict separation); nondeterminism (temperature 0 still isn't bit-stable across versions; design downstream for tolerance); injection via processed content (treat inputs as untrusted); per-class blind spots hidden by aggregate accuracy (slice evaluation as M16 §9); cost blowouts from token-heavy prompts at volume.
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Identical in *structure* to M16: per-class P/R/F1 on a held-out human-labeled set, slice analysis, confusion review; plus schema-validity rate, abstention calibration, and consistency (same input, repeated calls); judge-based evaluation only with periodic human-agreement audits; cost-and-latency per correct output as a first-class metric.
10. **Explainability methods** — Rationale generation (ask the model why — useful for review, not a faithful mechanism); span citations for extraction (quote-the-source discipline); confidence self-reports (poorly calibrated — prefer abstention designs); the honest framing for stakeholders: behavioral evaluation, not mechanistic explanation.
11. **Data requirements** — Zero training data (its superpower); requires a golden evaluation set (~100–500 labeled examples — cheap to build with the model's own help then human-verify); exemplar pool; volume forecasts for the economics decision.
12. **Compute profile** — No training; per-call inference cost (API tokens or GPU serving); latency 100ms–10s; the *only* methodology here whose unit economics are per-prediction-dominant — the reason distillation (M20) exists.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Massively adopted 2023–2026 as the entry point to GenAI (enterprise GenAI spend $11.5B→$37B 2024→2025, Menlo Ventures 2025; prompt-based patterns are the residual majority once RAG's 51% and fine-tuning's 9% are counted — Menlo 2024); engineering maturity catching up: evaluation-first prompt development is now the documented best practice but unevenly applied.
14. **Reusable components** — Is the generation stage of M18 and the reasoning core of M28; bootstrap labeler for M16/M32; synthetic-data generator for M20/M33; judge component for M18/M20/M28 evaluation; its golden-set + versioned-prompt-CI harness is the LLM-era mirror of M1's evaluation discipline.
15. **Representative use cases** — Ticket classification bootstrap, contract-field extraction, product-data normalization, survey coding, lead enrichment, log summarization, code review annotations.
16. **Production failure modes** — Provider deprecations/behavior shifts; cost creep with volume; quality decay on input drift with nobody re-measuring (scheduled golden-set re-runs); over-automation of judgment tasks without human escalation paths; monitoring = schema-validity, output-distribution drift, sampled human review, spend.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Provider SDKs (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google), instructor/Outlines (structured output), promptfoo/LangSmith/Braintrust (prompt evaluation & versioning), vLLM/TGI (self-hosted serving), DSPy (programmatic prompt optimization).
18. **Key references** — Brown et al., "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (NeurIPS 2020); Wei et al., "Chain-of-Thought Prompting" (NeurIPS 2022); Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench" (NeurIPS 2023); Anthropic/OpenAI prompt-engineering documentation (2023–2025); Khattab et al., "DSPy" (2023).

<a id="m20"></a>
### M20. Supervised fine-tuning, PEFT/LoRA & distillation

1. **Name + aliases** — Fine-tuning (SFT); parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT): LoRA/QLoRA, adapters; instruction tuning; continued pretraining / domain-adaptive pretraining (heavier cousin); knowledge distillation (teacher→student); preference tuning (DPO — bridging to M27).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Adapt a pretrained model's weights on curated task/domain examples — typically via low-rank adapter updates — to bake in behavior, format, style, or domain competence that prompting can't reliably achieve, or to compress a frontier model's behavior into a small cheap one (distillation).
3. **Problem class(es)** — Generation with strict format/style/tone; domain-specialized classification/extraction at volume; latency/cost-constrained deployment of LLM capability; embedding-model domain adaptation (feeding M17).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Text (instruction–response pairs, domain corpora); increasingly multimodal pairs; teacher-generated synthetic data.
5. **Component pipeline** — Decision gate (M35): confirm prompting + RAG genuinely can't meet the requirement → training-data curation (the dominant effort: collect/generate instruction–response pairs, quality-filter, dedup, decontaminate against eval sets) → base-model selection (size vs capability vs license) → PEFT training (LoRA rank/alpha, learning rate, epochs — few) → evaluation: task metrics on held-out golden set + **regression suite for capabilities that must not degrade** (catastrophic forgetting check) + safety evaluation → deployment (adapter merging or hot-swappable adapters; quantized serving) → monitoring + periodic refresh as the domain moves.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — **LoRA** (Hu et al., ICLR 2022) — low-rank weight deltas, ~100–1000× fewer trainable parameters; **QLoRA** (Dettmers et al., NeurIPS 2023) — 4-bit base + LoRA, single-GPU fine-tuning of 33–70B models; full SFT for maximal shift when compute allows; **DPO** (Rafailov et al., NeurIPS 2023) for preference-shaped behavior without an RL loop (M27 covers the RL path); distillation: teacher-labeled or teacher-generated corpora training small students (Hinton et al. 2015 lineage; the standard "frontier→8B" economics play); domain-adaptive continued pretraining for vocabulary-heavy fields (BloombergGPT 2023 as the maximal example). Base models: Llama/Qwen/Mistral-class open weights; provider fine-tuning APIs for closed models.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Reborn as data curation: instruction-format design, response-quality rubrics, difficulty/coverage balancing, dedup + near-dedup, eval-set decontamination, synthetic-data generation with teacher models and quality filtering (constitutional/rubric filtering), mixing ratios between task data and general instruction data (to fight forgetting).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Fine-tuning as a reflex when prompting would do (the most common waste; gate via M35 with measured prompt-baseline first); eval contamination via synthetic data echoing eval phrasing (decontaminate); catastrophic forgetting of general ability and safety behavior (regression + safety suites; conservative LR/epochs; mix general data); overfitting small datasets (memorization probes); teacher-error inheritance in distillation (human-audit teacher outputs); license/ToS violations in teacher-student flows; PII baked into weights (data governance pre-training — you cannot delete it after); benchmark-only evaluation missing production distribution (golden sets from real traffic).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Held-out task metrics (as M16/M19 §9) + capability-regression battery (general benchmarks pre/post) + safety/refusal evaluation + human preference review for generation quality; A/B against the prompted-frontier baseline on cost-per-correct-output — the deployment-deciding metric; distillation adds teacher-student agreement + student-vs-teacher on golden set.
10. **Explainability methods** — Behavioral only: eval reports, training-data provenance documentation (model cards), diff-testing vs base model; stakeholders receive a capability + regression + cost report.
11. **Data requirements** — SFT: ~10³–10⁵ curated pairs typically (quality dominates quantity — LIMA: Zhou et al. 2023 showed 1k excellent examples suffice for style/format); distillation: 10⁴–10⁶ teacher-generated examples; continued pretraining: 10⁸+ domain tokens; all needs rights-cleared, PII-governed data.
12. **Compute profile** — QLoRA: single high-memory GPU, hours (7–70B); full SFT: multi-GPU; serving: the payoff — small fine-tuned models on cheap GPUs replace frontier API calls; distillation shifts cost from inference (perpetual) to training (one-time).
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Techniques mature (LoRA/QLoRA are standard); adoption is the minority pattern by deliberate economics: 9% of enterprise production GenAI models are fine-tuned vs 51% RAG (Menlo Ventures 2024); Deloitte (2024) finds 20–25% of enterprises use customized/open-source LLMs in some form; open-source-model share of enterprise workloads was 13% mid-2025, down from 19% (Menlo mid-year 2025) — fine-tuning is a targeted tool, not the default path.
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M19's golden sets and synthetic-generation, M32's labeling loops; produces the served models behind M16/M18/M19/M28 at scale; embedding fine-tunes upgrade M17; evaluation/regression harness shared with M19/M28; DPO bridges to M27.
15. **Representative use cases** — Brand-voice generation, structured-report drafting, high-volume classification/extraction distilled from a frontier teacher, domain assistants with heavy jargon (legal/medical/codebase), on-prem/air-gapped capability.
16. **Production failure modes** — Domain drift outdating the tune (refresh cadence); base-model deprecations forcing re-tunes; silent regression on out-of-scope inputs (route-and-fallback designs); adapter sprawl without registry discipline; monitoring = task metrics on live samples + regression canaries.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Hugging Face PEFT/TRL, Axolotl, LLaMA-Factory, Unsloth (efficiency), vLLM/TGI/SGLang (serving), provider fine-tuning APIs, Weights & Biases/MLflow (tracking), lm-eval-harness (regression).
18. **Key references** — Hu et al., "LoRA" (ICLR 2022); Dettmers et al., "QLoRA" (NeurIPS 2023); Rafailov et al., "Direct Preference Optimization" (NeurIPS 2023); Zhou et al., "LIMA: Less Is More for Alignment" (NeurIPS 2023); Hinton, Vinyals & Dean, "Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network" (2015); Ouyang et al., "Training language models to follow instructions" (NeurIPS 2022).

## Family J — Document intelligence

<a id="m21"></a>
### M21. Document intelligence (OCR → layout → extraction)

1. **Name + aliases** — Intelligent document processing (IDP); document AI; OCR pipelines; layout-aware document understanding; key-value/table extraction; document classification-and-extraction; "capture" (legacy ECM term).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Convert unstructured or scanned documents (invoices, contracts, forms, statements) into validated structured records via a pipeline of ingestion, OCR/text-layer recovery, layout understanding, field/table extraction, validation rules, and human-in-the-loop exception review.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Extraction (fields, line items, tables); document classification/routing; splitting (multi-doc packets); matching to master data (hand-off to M23); summarization/Q&A over documents (hand-off to M18).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Documents: scanned images + born-digital PDFs (mixed quality), photos of documents, emails with attachments; layout + text + image jointly (the defining multimodality).
5. **Component pipeline** — Ingestion (mail/scan/API connectors; format normalization; page imaging) → preprocessing (deskew, denoise, orientation) → OCR or digital-text-layer extraction (with word-level bounding boxes + confidences) → document classification + packet splitting → extraction: template rules (legacy) / layout-aware models / multimodal-LLM prompting with the page image → normalization (dates, amounts, currencies, addresses) → validation: schema rules, cross-field arithmetic (line items sum to total), lookups against master data (PO/vendor match) → confidence-based routing: straight-through vs human review queue → corrections feed retraining → delivery to systems of record → monitoring (STP rate, field accuracy, exception aging).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — OCR: Tesseract (open baseline), PaddleOCR, cloud OCR (Google Document AI, Azure Document Intelligence, Amazon Textract — includes table/form parsing); layout-aware extraction: **LayoutLM family** (Xu et al., KDD 2020; LayoutLMv3 2022 — text+layout+image pretraining, the FUNSD/CORD/DocVQA benchmark line), Donut (Kim et al., ECCV 2022 — OCR-free encoder-decoder); table structure: Table Transformer (PubTables-1M, Smock et al. 2022); the 2024–2026 shift: **multimodal LLMs prompted with page images** (M19-style) now match or beat tuned layout models on many extraction tasks with zero training, moving the methodology's center of gravity from per-doc-type training to prompting + validation (practitioner consensus; benchmark evidence on DocVQA-class tasks). Selection: cloud/parser APIs + LLM extraction for heterogeneous low-volume doc types; trained layout models for stable high-volume types; rules for fixed forms.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Document-side: image quality normalization, page segmentation, reading-order recovery; extraction-side: field schemas with types/formats/nullability, anchor keywords, spatial priors (zone hints), doc-type-specific validators; the schema + validation design is the leverage point (the "feature engineering" of IDP).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — OCR-error propagation into downstream fields (safeguard: confidence thresholds per field, arithmetic cross-checks — the invoice-total check catches most digit errors); hallucinated fields from LLM extraction on absent data (null-when-absent prompting + validators + evidence bounding boxes); template drift (vendors change invoice layouts; monitor per-vendor accuracy); doc-type distribution shift; evaluation on clean samples while production sees phone photos (evaluate on production-quality samples, stratified by source); ground-truth ambiguity (annotation guidelines for messy documents); PII exposure in review queues (masking, access controls); double-processing/duplicate documents (idempotency + dedup hashing).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Field-level accuracy/F1 per field type (exact + normalized match), document-level straight-through-processing (STP) rate at a given accuracy bar, table-cell F1 (GriTS for structure), review-time per exception; business framing: STP% × volume = saved touches; benchmark sets: FUNSD, CORD, DocVQA for models — but in-house golden sets on your own doc mix decide deployment.
10. **Explainability methods** — Evidence highlighting: each extracted value linked to its bounding box on the page image (the trust-building UI standard); per-field confidence display; audit trails of corrections — reviewers receive a side-by-side document + extracted-record view.
11. **Data requirements** — Per doc-type: hundreds of annotated documents for trained extraction, near-zero for LLM-prompted extraction (plus a golden evaluation set of 50–200 docs per type); master data access for validation; retention/PII governance mandatory.
12. **Compute profile** — OCR + layout models: CPU/GPU batch, cheap per page; multimodal-LLM extraction: per-page API/GPU cost (the new cost center); volumes measured in pages/day — millions at large enterprises; human review is usually the true cost driver, which is why STP% is the KPI.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature market in transition: the IDP market is estimated at $2.3B in 2024 (Grand View Research) to $3.2B (Strategic Market Research 2024) with ~30%+ CAGR forecasts, while Infosource counts >$8B of 2024 IDP spending under a broader definition — divergent scopes, consistent growth signal; invoice/AP automation is decades-old production practice; the multimodal-LLM wave (2024–) is rapidly displacing per-template training.
14. **Reusable components** — Feeds M18 (parsed documents into RAG corpora), M23 (extracted entities into resolution), M1 (document-derived features); reuses M19 (LLM extraction), M16 (doc classification), M24 (vision preprocessing), M32 (correction-loop labeling); its human-in-the-loop review queue pattern is shared with M12's triage and M32's active learning.
15. **Representative use cases** — Invoice/AP automation, claims intake, KYC document verification, contract abstraction, loan-file processing, customs/trade documents, medical-record digitization.
16. **Production failure modes** — Vendor/layout drift degrading specific senders silently (per-sender accuracy monitoring); scanner/source quality shifts; validation-rule rot as business rules change; review-queue backlog collapse (exception aging alarms); corrections not feeding back (the improvement loop quietly dies); monitoring = STP rate, per-field accuracy on sampled audits, exception aging, per-source drilldowns.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Cloud: Azure Document Intelligence, Google Document AI, Amazon Textract; commercial IDP: ABBYY, UiPath Document Understanding, Hyperscience, Rossum; OSS: Tesseract, PaddleOCR, LayoutLM/Donut (Hugging Face), docTR, Unstructured/Docling (parsing for RAG).
18. **Key references** — Xu et al., "LayoutLM: Pre-training of Text and Layout for Document Image Understanding" (KDD 2020); Huang et al., "LayoutLMv3" (ACM MM 2022); Kim et al., "OCR-free Document Understanding Transformer (Donut)" (ECCV 2022); Smock et al., "PubTables-1M" (CVPR 2022); Mathew et al., "DocVQA" (WACV 2021); Grand View Research, "Intelligent Document Processing Market" (2024).

## Family K — Graph ML & entity resolution

<a id="m22"></a>
### M22. Graph machine learning (GNNs, link prediction, graph features)

1. **Name + aliases** — Graph ML; graph neural networks (GNNs: GraphSAGE/GCN/GAT); node/edge classification; link prediction; network analytics; graph feature engineering ("graph features for fraud"); knowledge-graph embeddings.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Model entities *and their relationships* as a graph and learn from connectivity — either by computing graph-derived features that feed tabular models, or by training message-passing networks end-to-end — capturing ring, collusion, and propagation structure that row-per-entity methods cannot see.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Node classification (risky account?), link prediction (will/should this edge exist? — recommendation, knowledge-graph completion), edge classification (fraudulent transaction?), community detection, graph-level prediction; anomaly detection on graphs.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Graph/network data constructed from relational tables: shared identifiers (device, address, phone), transactions (money movement), interactions (user–item), org/knowledge structures; usually heterogeneous (multiple node/edge types) and temporal.
5. **Component pipeline** — Graph construction from relational sources (the decisive stage: which entities, which edges, which time windows) → entity resolution first (M23 — duplicate nodes corrupt structure) → (path A: graph features) compute degree/centrality/PageRank/community/motif features per node and append to M1's feature table → (path B: GNN) neighborhood sampling + message-passing training with temporal-aware splits → evaluation with graph-appropriate splits → explanation (important neighbors/subgraphs) → serving: precomputed embeddings/features batch-refreshed, or online subgraph inference for real-time scoring → monitoring (graph drift: degree distributions, component sizes).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Classical graph algorithms: PageRank, Louvain/Leiden community detection, connected components, motif counts (production fraud staples); embeddings: node2vec (Grover & Leskovec 2016), knowledge-graph embeddings (TransE/RotatE) ; GNNs: GCN (Kipf & Welling 2017), **GraphSAGE** (Hamilton, Ying & Leskovec, NeurIPS 2017 — inductive sampling, the production template), GAT (Veličković 2018), heterogeneous variants (R-GCN, HGT), temporal GNNs (TGN — Rossi et al. 2020); the pragmatic hybrid: **GNN embeddings + XGBoost** (NVIDIA financial-fraud blueprint 2024). Selection: start with path A (graph features into M1 — cheapest, explainable, most of the lift); go GNN when multi-hop structure carries signal beyond hand-computed features and scale/team justify.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — (Path A) per-node: degree by edge type, weighted degrees, PageRank, clustering coefficient, community ID + community-level aggregates, shortest-path-to-known-bad distance, shared-attribute counts (devices/addresses per account), temporal edge velocities (new edges per window — the graph version of M12's velocity features); (Path B) node features from M1's pipelines, edge features (amount, recency), sampling fan-out tuning.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Temporal leakage through edges (an edge created *after* the label event leaks the outcome into the neighborhood; safeguard: time-sliced graph snapshots — features/messages only over edges existing at scoring time); data leakage via test-node neighbors in training aggregations (inductive splits: hold out nodes *and* their incident edges); entity-resolution errors creating false hubs (M23 quality gates); degree/popularity confounding (popular nodes score high for popularity, not risk; normalize, ablate); homophily assumption failures (GNNs underperform on heterophilous graphs; measure homophily first); oversmoothing with deep GNNs (2–3 layers is the norm); evaluation on random edge splits for link prediction (use temporal splits — random splits wildly inflate results).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Node/edge tasks: PR-AUC with temporal + inductive splits, precision@investigation-budget (fraud framing); link prediction: temporal-split ranking metrics (MRR/hits@k) against heuristic baselines (common-neighbors, Adamic-Adar — mandatory: they often win); ablation against the no-graph M1 baseline — the deployment-deciding comparison (documented lifts: fraud PR-AUC ~30%→~50% with graph features in industry case reports 2023–2024; Tencent LPS-GNN +11.9% cheating-detection precision, 2025).
10. **Explainability methods** — (Path A) SHAP on graph features — inherits M1's full explainability, a major argument for the hybrid; (Path B) GNNExplainer-class subgraph attribution (Ying et al. 2019), important-neighbor lists; investigator UI: the ego-network visualization with flagged relationships — in fraud practice, *the graph picture is the explanation*.
11. **Data requirements** — Relational sources with join keys to build edges; enough connectivity to matter (sparse graphs → path A only); labels as in M1; temporal edge timestamps for correctness.
12. **Compute profile** — Classical algorithms: CPU at billions of edges (Spark/GraphFrames, Neo4j GDS); GNN training: GPU with neighbor sampling (DGL/PyG scale to 10⁸–10⁹ edges; Tencent reports 10¹¹-edge production training, 2025); serving: precomputed embeddings are cheap, online subgraph inference is an engineering lift.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mainstream at platform scale, emerging in mid-market: documented production — Pinterest PinSage (2018, 3B nodes/18B edges, +150% hit-rate), Google Maps GNN ETA (~2020, up to 50% ETA-accuracy improvement in major cities, DeepMind), Alibaba/Uber core-product GNNs (2020s), AWS Neptune+GraphStorm fraud stacks (2023–2024), Tencent 100B-edge LPS-GNN (2025); graph *features* into tabular models are far more widely adopted than end-to-end GNNs (estimate; consistent with practitioner literature, not survey-verified).
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M23 (resolution) and M1 features; emits graph features into M1/M12 and embeddings into M14/M17; PinSage-style training reuses M14's pipelines; GraphRAG connects to M18; community detection reuses M13's validation patterns.
15. **Representative use cases** — Fraud-ring and collusion detection, AML network analysis, recommendation (M14 overlap), supply-chain risk propagation, knowledge-graph completion, IT/telecom network fault localization, insider-threat detection.
16. **Production failure modes** — Graph drift (connectivity patterns shift; monitor degree/component distributions); adversarial structure gaming (fraudsters split identities — the M23 arms race); pipeline cost creep on growing graphs (incremental graph updates); embedding staleness; monitoring = structural-stat drift + downstream model monitors from M1.
17. **Tooling landscape** — PyTorch Geometric, DGL, GraphStorm (AWS), Neo4j GDS, TigerGraph, NetworkX/igraph (analysis), Spark GraphFrames (scale-out classical), Memgraph.
18. **Key references** — Hamilton, Ying & Leskovec, "Inductive Representation Learning on Large Graphs (GraphSAGE)" (NeurIPS 2017); Kipf & Welling, "Semi-Supervised Classification with GCNs" (ICLR 2017); Ying et al., "Graph Convolutional Neural Networks for Web-Scale Recommender Systems (PinSage)" (KDD 2018); Derrow-Pinion et al., "ETA Prediction with Graph Neural Networks in Google Maps" (CIKM 2021); Ying et al., "GNNExplainer" (NeurIPS 2019); Akoglu, Tong & Koutra, "Graph-based anomaly detection: a survey" (DAMI 2015).

<a id="m23"></a>
### M23. Entity resolution / record linkage

1. **Name + aliases** — ER; record linkage; deduplication; identity resolution; merge/purge; data matching; customer data unification (CDP context); master data management's matching core; the Fellegi–Sunter tradition.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Determine which records across (or within) datasets refer to the same real-world entity — despite typos, format drift, missing fields, and no shared key — via blocking, pairwise similarity scoring, match classification, and clustering into golden entities with survivorship rules.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Matching/deduplication; classification (match/non-match on pairs); clustering (pair decisions → entity groups); a *precondition methodology* for every entity-centric analysis in this compendium.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular records with quasi-identifiers (names, addresses, emails, phones, dates of birth, tax IDs); increasingly text/embedding similarity for unstructured name fields.
5. **Component pipeline** — Source profiling + schema alignment → normalization/standardization (case, punctuation, address parsing, phonetic encodings, transliteration) → **blocking/indexing** (generate candidate pairs without O(n²): blocking keys, sorted neighborhoods, LSH/embedding ANN) → pairwise feature computation (field-level similarities: Jaro–Winkler, token overlap, geodesic distance) → match scoring: probabilistic (Fellegi–Sunter EM — unsupervised) or supervised classifier (M1 on labeled pairs) or LLM adjudication for hard pairs (M19) → threshold setting with human review band → transitive clustering into entities (with consistency constraints) → golden-record construction (survivorship rules per field) → persistent entity IDs with stability guarantees → incremental linkage for new records → quality monitoring (cluster sizes, merge/split audits).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — **Fellegi–Sunter** probabilistic linkage (JASA 1969) with EM-estimated m/u weights — the unsupervised standard, production-embodied in **Splink** (UK MoJ; widely adopted in the public sector); supervised pair classification (GBM on similarity features — M1 reuse) where labeled pairs exist; active-learning labeling (Dedupe.io pattern) to build pair labels cheaply (M32); deep/embedding matchers (DeepMatcher 2018, Ditto — Li et al., VLDB 2020: fine-tuned transformers on pair text) for dirty text-heavy records; LLM pair adjudication (2023–) as the high-accuracy/high-cost tier; blocking: standard keys → LSH → embedding-ANN blocking (M17 reuse). Commercial: Senzing (real-time ER engine), Informatica/IBM MDM-suite matchers.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Field standardization libraries (libpostal for addresses), phonetic encodings (Soundex/Metaphone), field-level similarity menus per type (names: Jaro–Winkler; addresses: token-set + geocode distance; dates: edit + plausibility), frequency-aware weighting (matching on "John Smith" is weaker evidence than on a rare name — the u-probability insight), cross-field interaction features (name match AND address match).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Blocking recall failures (true matches never become candidate pairs — invisible errors; safeguard: measure blocking recall on labeled data, use multiple redundant blocking passes); transitive-closure over-merging (A≈B, B≈C chaining into giant false clusters; safeguard: cluster-size alarms, graph-consistency checks, hub-record detection); threshold asymmetry ignored (false merges usually cost more than false splits — wrong credit history attached to a person; set thresholds on business costs, keep a human review band); training-pair sampling bias (random pairs are trivially non-matches; sample near the decision boundary); temporal identity drift (people move, change names — time-aware matching); ID instability across runs breaking downstream joins (persistent-ID discipline with versioned merge/split logs); privacy constraints on quasi-identifiers (governance; privacy-preserving linkage variants → M33).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Pairwise precision/recall/F1 on labeled pairs (stratified by difficulty); cluster-level metrics (B-cubed, cluster homogeneity); blocking recall separately; audit-sample review of merges above threshold; downstream validation — duplicate-rate reduction, entity-count sanity vs external references; ongoing merge/split audit sampling.
10. **Explainability methods** — Per-match evidence breakdown (which fields matched, with what similarity and weight — Splink's waterfall charts are the exemplar); match-decision audit trails (regulatory necessity in KYC/AML); reviewers receive side-by-side record comparisons with field-level highlighting.
11. **Data requirements** — Quasi-identifier fields with reasonable fill rates; a few thousand labeled pairs for supervised/threshold-tuning (active learning slashes this); reference data (address gazetteers) helps materially.
12. **Compute profile** — CPU; blocking makes 10⁸+-record linkage feasible on Spark/DuckDB (Splink runs on both); LLM-adjudication tier adds per-pair inference cost — reserve for the review band.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature and ubiquitous-but-invisible: census/statistical agencies have run probabilistic linkage for decades; every MDM/CDP implementation embeds it; KYC/AML sanctions screening is regulatorily mandated ER; open-source maturation (Splink, ~2020s) moved it from vendor black boxes to engineering practice. Its quality silently bounds every entity-level methodology (M1's features, M22's graphs) — the compendium's most under-appreciated dependency.
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M1 (pair classifiers), M17 (embedding blocking), M19 (LLM adjudication), M32 (active-learning labels); supplies clean entities to M1, M8, M11, M13, M14, M22; its human-review-band pattern is shared with M12/M21; survivorship/golden-record logic is a reusable data-engineering asset.
15. **Representative use cases** — Customer 360 / CDP identity stitching, KYC/AML screening, patient-record matching, supplier/vendor master dedup, fraud identity clustering (with M22), marketing suppression lists, census/statistical linkage.
16. **Production failure modes** — Source-format drift breaking standardizers (monitor per-source match rates); slow over-merge accumulation (periodic cluster audits); new-source onboarding degrading global quality (per-source quality gates); adversarial identity manipulation in fraud contexts (velocity + graph monitoring, M22); monitoring = match-rate trends, cluster-size distributions, review-band volumes, merge/split audit outcomes.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Splink (MoJ), dedupe (Python active learning), Zingg, JedAI, py_entitymatching/Magellan, Ditto (research), libpostal; commercial: Senzing, Tamr, Informatica MDM, AWS Entity Resolution.
18. **Key references** — Fellegi & Sunter, "A Theory for Record Linkage" (JASA 1969); Christen, "Data Matching" (2012); Li et al., "Deep Entity Matching with Pre-Trained Language Models (Ditto)" (VLDB 2020); Papadakis et al., "Blocking and Filtering Techniques for Entity Resolution: A Survey" (ACM CS 2020); Splink documentation (UK Ministry of Justice, 2020s).

## Family L — Computer vision

<a id="m24"></a>
### M24. Computer vision (classification, detection, segmentation; industrial inspection)

1. **Name + aliases** — Enterprise computer vision; image classification / object detection / semantic-instance segmentation; automated visual inspection (AVI) / automated optical inspection (AOI); defect detection; vision transformer pipelines; (zero-shot era) open-vocabulary detection, VLM-based inspection.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Learn visual predictors over images or video frames — usually by fine-tuning pretrained backbones on a curated, annotated image set — and deploy them against a controlled or semi-controlled imaging pipeline, where image-acquisition consistency matters as much as the model.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Classification (OK/defect, product type), detection (localize objects/defects), segmentation (pixel-precise regions), anomaly detection (unsupervised defect discovery — M12 crossover), OCR-in-the-wild (M21 crossover), video analytics (temporal extension).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Images (RGB, grayscale, IR, X-ray, microscopy), video, 3D/depth; paired metadata (line, shift, product) for slicing.
5. **Component pipeline** — Imaging design (optics, lighting, positioning — in industrial settings this stage determines feasibility) → data collection with coverage across conditions → annotation (boxes/masks/labels; guidelines + QA; the cost center) → augmentation policy → transfer-learning fine-tune of a pretrained backbone → evaluation on held-out + condition-stratified sets → error analysis and targeted re-collection (the loop that actually drives quality) → deployment: edge (on-line inspection at line rate) or batch/cloud → threshold + escalation policy (auto-reject vs human re-inspect) → monitoring (drift on image statistics, per-line performance) + retraining with hard examples.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Backbones: ResNet/EfficientNet (CNN era), ViT (Dosovitskiy et al. 2021), pretrained on ImageNet or self-supervised (DINOv2 2023); detection: YOLO family (real-time, industrial default), Faster R-CNN (two-stage accuracy), DETR-class; segmentation: Mask R-CNN (He et al. 2017), U-Net (biomedical/defect masks; Ronneberger 2015), **SAM/SAM-2** (Kirillov et al. 2023) for promptable segmentation + annotation acceleration; industrial anomaly detection: PatchCore/PaDiM-class embedding-memory methods on MVTec-AD (Bergmann et al. 2019) — train on good parts only, flag deviations (the data-efficient inspection pattern); zero-shot: CLIP (Radford et al. 2021), open-vocabulary detectors (Grounding DINO), VLM inspection prompts (M19 crossover) for low-volume flexible tasks.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Reborn as data-pipeline design: augmentation policies matched to real variation (rotation/lighting/noise — not label-destroying transforms), class-balance curation, hard-negative mining, imaging normalization (fixed exposure/white balance), tiling strategies for high-resolution inspection, synthetic defect generation (M33) where real defects are rare.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Train/serve imaging skew (the field's train/serve-skew variant: lab images vs line images; safeguard: collect training data from the production imaging rig, monitor image-statistic drift); spurious correlations/shortcuts (model keys on background, fixture, or timestamp burn-in; safeguard: saliency-map audits, background randomization); near-duplicate leakage across splits (frames from the same part/video in train and test; group splits by part/session); annotation inconsistency on boundary defects (adjudication rounds, consensus labels); class imbalance (defects are rare — PR metrics, oversampling, anomaly-mode M12); condition coverage gaps (night shift, seasonal light; stratified evaluation); label taxonomy drift as new defect types emerge.
9. **Evaluation methodology** — mAP@IoU for detection, IoU/Dice for segmentation, per-class PR for classification; operating-point analysis: false-accept vs false-reject rates at the chosen threshold — the business decision (escaped defects vs scrap/over-rejection cost); stratified by line/shift/product/condition; golden-set regression on every model change; for inspection: comparison against human-inspector agreement (which is often lower than assumed — measure it).
10. **Explainability methods** — Localization is native explanation (boxes/masks show *where*); saliency/Grad-CAM audits for classification (shortcut detection); per-defect galleries and Pareto charts (defect type × frequency) — what quality engineers receive; anomaly heatmaps for unsupervised inspection.
11. **Data requirements** — Transfer learning needs hundreds-to-thousands of labeled images per class (not millions); anomaly-mode needs only good-part images; coverage across real conditions matters more than raw count; annotation budget is the planning constraint.
12. **Compute profile** — GPU fine-tuning (hours); edge inference on embedded GPUs/NPUs at line-rate latencies (ms) — the deployment discipline; cloud batch for offline analytics; labeling cost typically exceeds compute cost.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature: machine vision is a long-established industrial market (multi-billion-dollar scale; analyst estimates in the low-teens $B for 2023–2024 — estimate; scope varies by firm, not single-source-verified) with deep penetration in electronics/automotive/pharma inspection; deep-learning-based AVI has been mainstream since ~2018; the zero-shot/VLM tier (2023–) is the emerging edge for flexible low-volume tasks.
14. **Reusable components** — Annotation + QA pipelines shared with M16/M21/M32; embeddings feed M13/M17 (visual search); anomaly-mode reuses M12; edge-serving + drift-monitoring patterns shared with M34; SAM-accelerated labeling is an M32 pattern; imaging metadata joins M1 features for root-cause analytics.
15. **Representative use cases** — Manufacturing defect inspection, shelf/planogram auditing, damage assessment (insurance), document-photo quality gating (feeding M21), safety/PPE compliance monitoring, medical imaging triage, satellite/asset monitoring.
16. **Production failure modes** — Camera/optics drift (dirty lenses, lighting decay — the #1 silent killer; image-quality monitors); process change shifting "normal" appearance (retrain triggers); new defect types unseen in training (anomaly-mode backstop, escalation sampling); threshold rot as product mix changes; monitoring = image-statistic drift, per-line score distributions, human re-inspection agreement sampling.
17. **Tooling landscape** — PyTorch/timm, Ultralytics YOLO, Detectron2, MMDetection, Anomalib (industrial anomaly), Segment Anything, Roboflow/CVAT/Label Studio (annotation), NVIDIA Triton/TensorRT + edge SDKs (deployment).
18. **Key references** — He et al., "Deep Residual Learning" (CVPR 2016); Dosovitskiy et al., "An Image is Worth 16×16 Words (ViT)" (ICLR 2021); He et al., "Mask R-CNN" (ICCV 2017); Bergmann et al., "MVTec AD — A Comprehensive Real-World Dataset for Unsupervised Anomaly Detection" (CVPR 2019); Kirillov et al., "Segment Anything" (ICCV 2023); Radford et al., "CLIP" (ICML 2021).

## Family M — Speech & audio

<a id="m25"></a>
### M25. Speech analytics (ASR → NLP pipelines)

1. **Name + aliases** — Speech-to-text analytics; conversation intelligence; call-center analytics; ASR + NLP cascade; voice-of-customer mining; contact-center AI (its productized form); audio event analytics (non-speech cousin).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Transcribe speech at scale with a pretrained ASR model (plus diarization and timestamps), then run the text-analytics stack (M16/M17/M19) over the transcripts to classify, extract, summarize, search, and score conversations — an audio front-end bolted onto the text family.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Transcription (sequence transduction); speaker diarization; classification (intent, outcome, compliance), extraction (entities, commitments), summarization, QA scoring, search over conversations; real-time agent-assist (streaming variant).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Audio (telephony 8kHz to wideband; meetings; voicemail), plus channel metadata (agent/customer legs, IVR context); output modality is text + timing — after which Family I applies.
5. **Component pipeline** — Audio capture + consent/compliance gating (recording laws, PII policy) → preprocessing (channel separation, resampling, VAD) → ASR transcription (with word timestamps + confidences) → speaker diarization / channel attribution → transcript enrichment (punctuation, redaction of PII/PCI) → text-analytics layer: M16 classifiers (intent, outcome, compliance flags), M19 summarization/extraction, M17 indexing for search → aggregation to conversation/agent/queue dashboards → human QA calibration loop → monitoring (WER drift by segment, downstream metric health).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — ASR: **Whisper** (Radford et al. 2022 — large-scale weak supervision; ~2.7% WER LibriSpeech test-clean, ~5.2% test-other for large variants, per the paper's benchmark line) and its efficient descendants (faster-whisper, distil-whisper); conformer-based commercial APIs (Google, Azure, AWS Transcribe, Deepgram, AssemblyAI); streaming ASR (RNN-T architectures) for real-time; diarization: pyannote (Bredin 2023); downstream: the full Family I menu — increasingly a single LLM pass for summary+extraction+scoring per call (M19). Selection: managed APIs for speed-to-value and telephony tuning; self-hosted Whisper-class for cost at volume and data residency.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Audio-side: channel separation (agent vs customer as separate streams — the highest-leverage preprocessing), telephony-domain adaptation, custom vocabulary/boosting for product names; text-side: turn-level features (talk-time ratio, interruptions, silence, sentiment trajectory) — the conversation-dynamics features unique to this methodology; call-metadata joins (queue, outcome codes).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — WER inequality across accents/lines/domains (aggregate WER hides pockets of 30%+ error; safeguard: stratified WER measurement on a labeled audio sample per segment); error cascade — ASR errors silently corrupt every downstream metric (safeguard: confidence-aware downstream processing, periodic human transcript audits tied to downstream metric checks); diarization swaps attributing customer words to agents (compliance false-positives; channel-separated capture avoids it entirely); hallucinated transcript segments on silence/noise (a documented Whisper failure mode; VAD gating, confidence filtering); PII/PCI leakage into transcripts and indexes (redaction before storage; DTMF masking); consent/retention violations (governance gating up front); benchmark-vs-production gap (LibriSpeech numbers don't transfer to noisy telephony — measure on your own audio).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — ASR: WER/CER on an in-domain human-transcribed sample, stratified (accent, channel, queue); diarization error rate; downstream: M16/M19 metrics computed against human-labeled calls; end-to-end: agreement between automated QA scores and human QA on a calibration sample (the operational gate); dashboard metrics validated against known events.
10. **Explainability methods** — Clickable evidence: every flag/score links to the transcript span and audio timestamp (the trust pattern for QA teams); confidence display per segment; aggregate topic/driver reports (M16-style) for VoC stakeholders.
11. **Data requirements** — Audio access with lawful basis; a human-transcribed calibration set (tens of hours, stratified); labeled calls for downstream classifiers (hundreds per class, or M19 bootstrap); custom vocabulary lists.
12. **Compute profile** — ASR is the cost center: GPU batch (Whisper-class: faster-than-real-time on modern GPUs; roughly, one GPU transcribes hundreds of audio-hours/day — estimate) or per-minute API pricing; downstream text analytics is cheap by comparison; streaming agent-assist needs dedicated low-latency serving.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature core (ASR quality crossed the utility threshold with the 2022–2023 Whisper generation; commercial speech analytics predates it by a decade); contact-center analytics is a standard enterprise product category with adoption commonly reported in the 20–40% range of contact centers deploying conversational-AI/analytics (2022–2023 analyst surveys; estimate — precise shares vary by survey and definition); post-2023, LLM summarization made per-call summaries a default feature across the category.
14. **Reusable components** — Is a front-end adapter for the entire Family I stack (M16 classification, M17 search, M18 Q&A over calls, M19 summarization); conversation-dynamics features feed M1 (churn/complaint risk models); QA-calibration loop is M32's labeling pattern; redaction components shared with M21/M33 governance.
15. **Representative use cases** — Call QA/compliance monitoring at 100% coverage, voice-of-customer topic mining, agent assist and after-call-work automation, complaint/churn-risk flagging, sales-call intelligence, meeting summarization.
16. **Production failure modes** — Telephony/codec changes degrading WER overnight (stratified WER monitors); vocabulary drift (new products); silent diarization degradation; downstream dashboards drifting from reality as ASR drifts (periodic end-to-end calibration); monitoring = WER canaries, confidence distributions, human-agreement sampling.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Whisper/faster-whisper/WhisperX, NVIDIA NeMo/Riva, pyannote (diarization), Deepgram/AssemblyAI/AWS/Azure/Google APIs; product tier: contact-center suites (Genesys, NICE, Observe.AI-class).
18. **Key references** — Radford et al., "Robust Speech Recognition via Large-Scale Weak Supervision (Whisper)" (2022); Graves, "Sequence Transduction with Recurrent Neural Networks" (2012 — RNN-T lineage); Gulati et al., "Conformer" (2020); Bredin, "pyannote.audio 2.1" (2023).

## Family N — Reinforcement learning & sequential decisioning

Two dossiers: bandits (single-step, production-proven) and offline/long-horizon RL (multi-step, harder, includes RLHF-style post-training). Agentic LLM systems get their own family (O).

<a id="m26"></a>
### M26. Multi-armed & contextual bandits

1. **Name + aliases** — Bandits; multi-armed bandits (MAB); contextual bandits (CB); Thompson sampling / UCB methods; adaptive experimentation; "explore-exploit" allocation; online policy learning (single-step).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Continuously allocate traffic among competing actions (variants, offers, layouts) to maximize cumulative reward while still exploring, optionally conditioning the choice on context features — turning the measure-then-decide loop of A/B testing into a decide-while-measuring loop for single-step decisions with fast feedback.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Sequential decisioning (single-step/myopic); adaptive allocation; personalization policy learning; budget-efficient optimization over discrete action sets.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Event streams of (context, action, reward) tuples; tabular context features (from M1 pipelines); fast, well-attributed reward signals.
5. **Component pipeline** — Action-space + reward definition (short-horizon, attributable — the design make-or-break) → context feature selection (M1 features, kept simple) → policy choice (ε-greedy / UCB / Thompson sampling; LinUCB/linear TS or tree/neural reward models for contextual) → **logging discipline: record the action-selection propensities** (the non-negotiable enabling artifact for everything downstream) → online serving with per-decision latency budgets → off-policy evaluation (IPS/DR on logged data) for candidate policies → guardrails (exploration floors, fallback to control, business constraints) → monitoring (regret proxies, reward drift, arm-share dynamics) → periodic reward-model retraining.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — MAB: Thompson sampling (Thompson 1933; empirical superiority — Chapelle & Li, NeurIPS 2011), UCB1 (Auer et al. 2002); contextual: **LinUCB** (Li et al., WWW 2010 — the Yahoo! news-recommendation landmark), linear Thompson sampling, tree/GBM reward models with bootstrapped exploration, neural bandits; policy-based: Vowpal Wabbit's contextual-bandit reductions (Agarwal et al. 2016 — the "Decision Service" production template, productized as Azure Personalizer); OPE: IPS, self-normalized IPS, doubly-robust (Dudík et al. 2014). Selection: plain MAB for context-free variant selection; LinUCB/linear TS as the robust contextual default; richer reward models only with volume.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Low-dimensional, stable context features (high-dimensional sparse contexts slow learning and complicate OPE); action features for shared-structure generalization across arms; reward shaping: proxy rewards that arrive fast but correlate with long-term value (and documented as proxies); delayed-reward attribution windows.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Missing/incorrect propensity logging (destroys off-policy evaluation and debiased retraining forever; safeguard: propensities as a schema-level requirement, logged atomically with the decision); reward-signal corruption (tracking changes silently redefine the reward; reward-pipeline monitors); short-proxy optimization harming long-term value (clickbait effect; safeguard: periodic long-horizon A/B audits of the bandit's policy — M9 stays the evaluation-of-record); non-stationarity (arm values drift; discounted/windowed estimators, exploration floors never reaching zero); delayed rewards biasing fast-feedback arms (attribution windows, importance-corrected updates); simple-regret vs cumulative-regret confusion (if the goal is *learning the best arm for later*, use an experiment, not a bandit); winner's-curse readouts of bandit dashboards (adaptive allocation biases naive means; use OPE estimators for reporting).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Offline: replay/OPE (IPS/DR) on logged propensity-stamped data before deploying a policy change; online: cumulative reward vs a persistent randomized control slice (the bandit-era holdout), regret proxies, arm-share sanity; the credibility anchor: periodic M9 experiment of bandit-policy vs business-as-usual.
10. **Explainability methods** — Arm-performance dashboards with uncertainty intervals; per-context policy summaries ("segment X mostly receives arm B because..."— via surrogate M1 model + SHAP on the policy's choices); exploration-rate transparency; stakeholders receive an allocation report + measured incremental reward.
11. **Data requirements** — Decision volume is the binding constraint: needs enough traffic per arm-context region to learn (thousands of decisions per arm minimum; contextual needs much more); rewards must arrive within an operationally short window; propensity-logged history for any offline work.
12. **Compute profile** — Trivial ML compute; the cost is the online serving path (ms-latency decision endpoint) and the logging/attribution infrastructure.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature in digital platforms: documented production lineage from Yahoo! news (2010), Microsoft's Decision Service/Azure Personalizer (2016–), Netflix artwork personalization (2017 tech-blog canon), Amazon/DoorDash/Spotify practice; adoption concentrated where decision volume is high; far less common in mid-market (estimate; not survey-verified).
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M1 features and M9's experimentation/telemetry infrastructure (a bandit is an experiment platform with an adaptive allocator); its propensity-logging + OPE components are shared with M11, M15, M27; supplies the exploration layer of M14 and the adaptive tier of M19/M28 prompt/policy selection.
15. **Representative use cases** — Creative/subject-line selection, homepage/module ranking exploration, offer/incentive selection, notification timing, search/rec exploration slices, dynamic default settings.
16. **Production failure modes** — Reward-pipeline breakage (the bandit cheerfully optimizes a broken signal); context drift; arm churn (new creatives constantly resetting learning; warm-start via action features); exploration starvation after "temporary" floors get zeroed; monitoring = reward-signal health, arm-share entropy, control-slice deltas, propensity-log completeness.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Vowpal Wabbit (the reference CB implementation), Open Bandit Pipeline (OPE), MABWiser, River (online), Azure Personalizer (managed), in-house on experimentation platforms (GrowthBook/Statsig tiers).
18. **Key references** — Li et al., "A Contextual-Bandit Approach to Personalized News Article Recommendation" (WWW 2010); Chapelle & Li, "An Empirical Evaluation of Thompson Sampling" (NeurIPS 2011); Agarwal et al., "Making Contextual Decisions with Low Technical Debt" (2016); Dudík et al., "Doubly Robust Policy Evaluation and Optimization" (Statistical Science 2014); Lattimore & Szepesvári, "Bandit Algorithms" (2020).

<a id="m27"></a>
### M27. Offline RL & long-horizon sequential decisioning (incl. RLHF-style post-training)

1. **Name + aliases** — Offline (batch) reinforcement learning; sequential policy optimization; dynamic treatment regimes; RL for operations (inventory, bidding, energy); and the post-training branch: RLHF/RLAIF, reward modeling, RL-from-verifiable-rewards (RLVR) for training LLM/agent behavior.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Learn a multi-step decision policy that optimizes cumulative long-horizon reward — from logged interaction data (offline RL), from a simulator, or from preference/verifier signals (RLHF-family) — accepting substantially higher methodological risk than bandits in exchange for optimizing sequences rather than single steps.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Sequential decisioning (multi-step); control/optimization over time (pricing ladders, inventory, bidding, energy dispatch); dynamic treatment regimes; model post-training (aligning LLMs/agents to preferences and verifiable outcomes).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Trajectories: (state, action, reward, next-state) logs; simulator rollouts; preference-pair datasets and verifier signals (post-training branch); state features from M1 pipelines.
5. **Component pipeline** — MDP formulation (state, action, reward, horizon — where most projects succeed or die) → data: logged trajectories (with behavior-policy propensities where possible) or simulator construction/validation → offline policy learning with conservatism (or simulator-based RL) → **off-policy evaluation with uncertainty bounds** (the gate: never deploy on training-objective values) → staged deployment: shadow → constrained pilot with guardrails → expanding rollout with kill-switches → online monitoring and periodic re-learning. Post-training branch: preference data collection → reward-model training → policy optimization (PPO/GRPO) or direct methods (DPO) → capability + safety regression evaluation → deployment as model weights (M20's serving).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Offline RL: Conservative Q-Learning (CQL — Kumar et al., NeurIPS 2020), Implicit Q-Learning (IQL 2021), behavior-regularized methods (TD3+BC), Decision Transformer (Chen et al. 2021 — sequence-modeling reframing); OPE: fitted Q-evaluation, DR estimators, high-confidence bounds (Thomas et al. 2015); simulator RL: PPO (Schulman et al. 2017), domain randomization for sim-to-real; dynamic treatment regimes (Murphy 2003 — the biostatistics lineage); post-training: RLHF (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al., InstructGPT, NeurIPS 2022), reward modeling, DPO (Rafailov et al. 2023), RLAIF/Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022), RLVR for reasoning/agents (2024–2025). Pragmatic hierarchy: exhaust M9/M26/M29 first; offline RL when sequences genuinely matter and rich logs exist; simulator RL when a faithful simulator exists (rare outside physics-like domains).
7. **Feature engineering practices** — State design: sufficient statistics of history (compact, Markov-ish), reward engineering with explicit anti-gaming review (reward hacking is the default outcome of sloppy rewards), action-space discretization/constraint encoding; post-training: preference-data curation (rubrics, annotator agreement), verifier design (unit tests, checkers) — the data-quality discipline of M20 applied to preferences.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Distribution shift beyond logged behavior (offline RL extrapolates into unvisited state-actions; safeguard: conservative/pessimistic objectives, behavior constraints); OPE optimism (evaluating a policy far from the logging policy has enormous variance; safeguard: high-confidence OPE, overlap diagnostics, refusal to extrapolate); reward hacking/specification gaming (policy exploits the reward's blind spots — canonical across both branches; safeguard: red-team the reward, guardrail metrics, human audit of policy behavior); simulator gap (great in sim, harmful live; staged rollout); horizon/credit-assignment misdesign; non-stationary environments invalidating learned dynamics; post-training-specific: reward-model overoptimization (Goodhart — Gao et al. 2023; KL penalties, early stopping), preference-data bias baked into behavior, safety regressions (mandatory regression suites, M20 §8).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Offline: OPE with confidence intervals against the incumbent policy's logged value; sensitivity analysis over behavior-policy uncertainty; online: guardrailed staged A/B (M9) with long-horizon value metrics and kill criteria; post-training: reward-model accuracy on held-out preferences, policy win-rates (human + calibrated judge), capability/safety regression batteries, and downstream task evaluation (M28 harnesses for agents).
10. **Explainability methods** — Policy audits: state-conditional action summaries ("in state X the policy does Y"), trajectory playbacks and counterfactual rollouts, surrogate-model + SHAP over the policy's decisions; post-training: behavioral evaluation reports and preference-alignment audits — mechanistic explanation remains research-stage.
11. **Data requirements** — The most demanding here: large trajectory logs with good state coverage (or a trustworthy simulator); reward observability over long horizons; post-training: 10⁴–10⁶ preference pairs or verifiable-task suites; annotator quality control.
12. **Compute profile** — Policy learning: GPU (modest for tabular-state problems, large for deep policies); simulators can dominate cost; RLHF-scale post-training: multi-GPU clusters (frontier-lab economics; enterprise-scale DPO on open models is single-node feasible via M20's stack).
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Split verdict. Enterprise operations offline RL: *emerging* — documented deployments (datacenter cooling — DeepMind/Google 2016–2018; recommender long-term-value RL at YouTube — Chen et al. 2019; ad bidding at Alibaba/JD) but concentrated in top-tier labs/platforms; most enterprises correctly stop at bandits (estimate; consistent with practitioner literature, not survey-verified). Post-training branch: *mainstream inside model providers* — RLHF/DPO is how every frontier assistant is built (InstructGPT 2022 onward; RLVR wave 2024–2025) — and increasingly accessible to enterprises via DPO on open models.
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M1 (state features), M26 (propensity logging + OPE — shared machinery), M9 (staged evaluation), M29 (constraint handling; RL and optimization compete for the same decisions); post-training branch consumes M20 (SFT base, serving) and M19/M28 (evaluation harnesses); its reward-modeling component is reused by M28's agent-evaluation and by LLM-judge calibration.
15. **Representative use cases** — Inventory/replenishment over lead times, bid optimization, energy dispatch/HVAC control, dynamic pricing ladders, treatment sequencing (clinical/collections), LLM/agent post-training (assistant behavior, reasoning, tool use).
16. **Production failure modes** — Policy drift as environment dynamics shift (scheduled re-learning + drift monitors on state distributions); slow-burn reward hacking discovered late (behavioral audits); guardrail erosion during rollout expansion; post-training: emergent behavior changes after provider updates (pin + regression); monitoring = guardrail metrics, state-coverage drift, OPE re-estimates on fresh logs, kill-switch drills.
17. **Tooling landscape** — d3rlpy (offline RL), Ray RLlib, Stable-Baselines3, Gymnasium (interfaces); OPE: Open Bandit Pipeline, COBS; post-training: TRL, OpenRLHF, veRL; simulators: domain-specific (AnyLogic/SimPy for ops — bridging M29).
18. **Key references** — Levine et al., "Offline Reinforcement Learning: Tutorial, Review, and Perspectives" (2020); Kumar et al., "Conservative Q-Learning" (NeurIPS 2020); Ouyang et al., "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback" (NeurIPS 2022); Christiano et al., "Deep RL from Human Preferences" (NeurIPS 2017); Rafailov et al., "DPO" (NeurIPS 2023); Gao, Schulman & Hilton, "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization" (ICML 2023); Sutton & Barto, "Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction" (2nd ed. 2018).

## Family O — Agentic

<a id="m28"></a>
### M28. Agentic LLM methodologies (tool-calling agents + evaluation harnesses)

1. **Name + aliases** — LLM agents; tool-calling / function-calling agents; agentic workflows; multi-step planning agents; orchestrated LLM systems; copilots-with-actions; MCP-style tool ecosystems; agent evaluation harnesses (the methodology's load-bearing second half).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Give an LLM a goal, a set of typed tools over enterprise systems (search, SQL, APIs, code execution), and an iterative observe–think–act loop, bounded by permissions and checkpoints, so it can complete multi-step tasks — with an evaluation harness of scenario suites treated as a co-equal deliverable because behavior is emergent and nondeterministic.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Multi-step task completion; orchestration across systems; interactive question-answering with actions; research/synthesis; code generation-and-verification; workflow automation beyond fixed pipelines.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Text (instructions, tool schemas, observations); structured tool I/O (JSON); documents/screens (multimodal agents); execution traces as the methodology's core data artifact.
5. **Component pipeline** — Task + scope definition (what the agent may and may not do) → tool design: minimal, typed, well-described tools with least-privilege credentials (tool design quality dominates model choice at the margin) → orchestration pattern selection: single agent with loop, fixed workflow with LLM steps ("workflows before agents" — the consensus design guidance), or multi-agent decomposition → context management (system prompts, memory, retrieval via M18) → guardrails: permissioning, human-approval checkpoints for irreversible actions, sandboxing, budget/step limits → **evaluation harness construction: scenario suites with programmatic success checks + trace-based judging** → staged deployment (shadow → assist-with-approval → bounded autonomy) → observability: full trace logging, cost/latency tracking → continuous evaluation on live samples + regression on every prompt/model/tool change.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Reasoning/acting patterns: ReAct (Yao et al., ICLR 2023), plan-then-execute, reflection/self-critique loops; tool use: function-calling APIs (2023–), Model Context Protocol (Anthropic 2024) as the emerging tool-interface standard; orchestration: LangGraph/agent SDKs, state-machine designs; evaluation: SWE-bench (Jimenez et al. 2024) for code agents, τ-bench (Yao et al. 2024) for tool-use with rules, GAIA, WebArena; LLM-judge scoring with human calibration (M19's discipline); model tier: frontier models for hard/open-ended agency, smaller fine-tuned models (M20) for narrow high-volume agent steps.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Reborn as context + tool engineering: tool descriptions as the "feature definitions" (precise names, parameter docs, failure semantics); context curation (what enters the window per step); memory design (scratchpads, summarized history); environment observability (make system state legible to the agent); prompt scaffolds encoding procedures and stop conditions.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Unbounded blast radius (an agent with write access can do real damage; safeguard: least-privilege tools, approval gates on irreversible actions, sandboxes, reversibility-first tool design); prompt injection via tool outputs and retrieved content (treat all tool returns as untrusted; privilege separation between instruction and data); error cascades in long chains (per-step validation, checkpointing, bounded retries); nondeterministic regressions from model/prompt updates (pin versions; full regression harness on every change); evaluation theater — demos instead of measured success rates (scenario suites with programmatic checks, statistically meaningful sample sizes); multi-agent complexity without measured benefit (start with workflows; add autonomy only where measured); cost/latency runaways (budgets, step caps); silent capability gaps discovered by users (adversarial scenario coverage).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Scenario-suite success rate (end-to-end task completion with programmatic verification where possible — the gold standard); trace-level judging (correct tool choice, faithful grounding, rule adherence — τ-bench-style pass^k for reliability across retries); cost and latency per completed task; human-review calibration of judge scores; online: assisted-mode acceptance rates, escalation rates, incident tracking; regression runs on every model/prompt/tool change (CI for agents).
10. **Explainability methods** — Full execution traces (every thought/tool-call/observation — inspectable and auditable; the agent-era audit log); per-task rationale summaries; approval-gate presentations ("the agent wants to do X because Y"); stakeholders receive success-rate dashboards + incident reviews rather than model internals.
11. **Data requirements** — No training data to start (built on pretrained models); the critical data assets are the scenario suite (dozens-to-hundreds of realistic tasks with verifiable outcomes), tool documentation, and accumulating trace logs (which later feed M20/M27 fine-tuning of agent behavior).
12. **Compute profile** — Inference-dominated: many LLM calls per task (10–100+), making per-task cost the design constraint; latency measured in seconds-to-minutes; no training compute unless fine-tuning agent models (M20/M27).
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Emerging-to-early-mainstream, fast-moving: 12% of enterprise GenAI implementations were agentic in 2024 and only 16% of implementations qualified as "true agents" (plan-act-adapt) in 2025 (Menlo Ventures 2024, 2025); a 2025 KPMG survey found 61% of organizations not yet actively exploring agentic AI; meanwhile coding agents reached genuine production traction (2024–2026) — the leading indicator domain because verification is cheap. Engineering practice (harnesses, guardrails) is consolidating but young.
14. **Reusable components** — Composes M18 (retrieval), M19 (structured LLM steps + judging), M15/M17 (search tools), M20 (fine-tuned components), M27 (RL-trained agent policies); its trace-logging/observability pattern extends M9's telemetry; scenario-harness discipline is the LLM-era mirror of M9's experiment culture; tools themselves wrap every other methodology (an agent can call M1's scorer or M29's optimizer — the integration layer of the catalog).
15. **Representative use cases** — Customer-support resolution agents, coding/engineering agents, data-analysis assistants (query→chart→narrative), back-office process automation (claims, onboarding), research/synthesis agents, IT/ops runbook execution.
16. **Production failure modes** — Model-update behavior shifts breaking workflows (pin + regress); tool/API drift (contract tests for tools); slow scope creep beyond evaluated territory (scope registries, coverage tracking); trust erosion from a single spectacular failure (incident response + guardrail postmortems); cost creep; monitoring = success-rate on sampled live tasks, escalation/approval rates, cost per task, injection-attempt detection.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Agent SDKs (Anthropic Claude Agent SDK, OpenAI Agents SDK), LangGraph, MCP tool ecosystem, E2B/sandboxing, LangSmith/Braintrust/Langfuse (tracing + evals), promptfoo/DeepEval (regression), SWE-bench/τ-bench (public benchmarks).
18. **Key references** — Yao et al., "ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models" (ICLR 2023); Schick et al., "Toolformer" (NeurIPS 2023); Jimenez et al., "SWE-bench" (ICLR 2024); Yao et al., "τ-bench" (2024); Anthropic, "Building Effective Agents" (2024); Kapoor et al., "AI Agents That Matter" (2024).

## Family P — Optimization & OR

<a id="m29"></a>
### M29. Predict-then-optimize & mathematical-optimization hybrids

1. **Name + aliases** — Prescriptive analytics; predict-then-optimize (PTO); ML+OR; mathematical programming (LP/MIP), constraint programming (CP); scheduling/routing optimization; simulation-optimization; decision-focused learning (the research-edge variant); "the decision layer".
2. **One-sentence definition** — Feed ML predictions (demand, risk, uplift, ETA) into a mathematical optimizer that chooses the best feasible action plan under hard business constraints (capacity, budget, rules) — converting per-entity predictions, which are not yet decisions, into globally consistent, constraint-respecting decisions.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Optimization/allocation (assignment, scheduling, routing, inventory, pricing, network design); constrained decisioning over M1/M5–M8/M11 outputs; what-if/scenario analysis (simulation branch).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular parameters (costs, capacities, forecasts as inputs); graph/network structure (routing); the predictions of upstream methodologies are first-class input data.
5. **Component pipeline** — Decision framing: variables, objective, constraints elicited from operations (the requirements-engineering stage that dominates success) → prediction layer: forecasts/scores with uncertainty from M1/M5–M8/M11 → model formulation (LP/MIP/CP; or heuristics/metaheuristics at extreme scale) → solving with time budgets and optimality-gap targets → solution validation with planners (feasibility in the *real* world, not just the model) → scenario/sensitivity analysis (robustness to forecast error) → deployment: planner-in-the-loop recommendations or automated execution → feedback: realized outcomes vs planned → re-solve cadence + model maintenance as constraints change.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Exact: LP/MIP via branch-and-cut (Gurobi/CPLEX commercial; HiGHS/CBC/SCIP open); CP-SAT (Google OR-Tools — the scheduling workhorse); metaheuristics (large-neighborhood search, genetic/simulated annealing) for messy large instances; routing: OR-Tools VRP, specialized solvers; simulation-optimization: discrete-event simulation (SimPy/AnyLogic) wrapped in search when dynamics defy closed-form; stochastic programming and robust optimization for decisions under forecast uncertainty; decision-focused learning / smart predict-then-optimize (Elmachtoub & Grigas, Management Science 2022) — train the predictor on decision loss rather than prediction loss (research-to-early-adopter); RL (M27) as the competing paradigm when sequential adaptivity beats re-solving.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Reborn as model formulation: decision-variable design, linearization of business rules, big-M discipline, symmetry breaking, soft-constraint penalty calibration (encoding *preferences* vs *rules* correctly), scenario generation from forecast distributions (quantiles from M6/M30 as robust inputs).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Point-forecast optimization ignoring uncertainty (deterministic plans that shatter on forecast error; safeguard: quantile/scenario inputs, robust or stochastic formulations, buffer policies); the prediction–decision interface mismatch (a model tuned for AUC may misrank exactly where the optimizer is sensitive; safeguard: evaluate predictions on decision loss, consider SPO); infeasibility from stale constraint data (constraint-data pipelines with validation; graceful relaxation with audit trails); optimizer gaming reality (exploiting model approximations — solutions that are optimal-in-model and absurd-in-practice; planner review loops); unit/scale bugs in objective coefficients (dimensional analysis, solution sanity bounds); local optima in metaheuristics presented as optimal (report gaps); double-counting when uplift (M11) feeds allocation (consistent counterfactual accounting).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Solution quality: objective value + optimality gap; decision quality: realized-outcome comparison vs incumbent planning (shadow runs, then M9-style regional experiments where feasible); robustness: performance across forecast-error scenarios; operational acceptance: plan-override rates by human planners (high overrides = the model is missing constraints); re-solve stability (plans shouldn't thrash between runs — penalize deviation).
10. **Explainability methods** — Constraint-binding analysis ("capacity at DC-3 is the bottleneck") and shadow prices/duals — genuinely decision-relevant explanations unique to this family; solution diffs vs previous plan; infeasibility explanations (IIS — irreducible infeasible sets); planners receive an annotated plan: what changed, what binds, what it costs.
11. **Data requirements** — Accurate constraint/master data (capacities, costs, rules — usually the binding data problem, worse than the ML side); upstream forecasts with uncertainty; realized-outcome capture for the feedback loop.
12. **Compute profile** — CPU solver time (seconds to hours; MIP is NP-hard — time budgets + gap tolerances are the practice); commercial solver licenses are a real cost line; re-solve cadence (nightly/hourly) drives infrastructure.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature — operations research predates ML in enterprises by decades (airline crew scheduling, refinery planning since the 1960s–80s); ubiquitous in logistics, manufacturing, workforce scheduling, and supply chain (Amazon, UPS ORION, airline RM as canonical documented deployments); the *predict-then-optimize coupling* with modern ML forecasts is the mainstream integration pattern; decision-focused learning remains emerging.
14. **Reusable components** — Consumes M5–M8 forecasts, M1/M11 scores/uplifts, M22 network structure; supplies the decision layer that turns any predictive methodology into action (the "solution" layer of the vocabulary, systematized); scenario machinery shared with M30 (uncertainty) and M33 (synthetic scenarios); its planner-in-the-loop pattern parallels M12/M21 review queues.
15. **Representative use cases** — Replenishment/inventory optimization, workforce/crew scheduling, vehicle routing, marketing-budget allocation (over M11 uplifts), price/markdown optimization (over M6 elasticity forecasts), network/facility design, promotion planning.
16. **Production failure modes** — Constraint drift (the business changes rules, the model doesn't hear; constraint-review cadence with operations); master-data rot; forecast-degradation propagating into plans (monitor upstream FVA); planner trust collapse after one absurd plan (validation guardrails, explanation); monitoring = override rates, realized-vs-planned gaps, solver-time/infeasibility alarms.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Gurobi, CPLEX, FICO Xpress (commercial); HiGHS, SCIP, CBC, OR-Tools (open); Pyomo/PuLP/CVXPY (modeling); Timefold (scheduling); SimPy/AnyLogic (simulation); Nextmv (decision-ops platform).
18. **Key references** — Elmachtoub & Grigas, "Smart 'Predict, then Optimize'" (Management Science 2022); Bertsimas & Kallus, "From Predictive to Prescriptive Analytics" (Management Science 2020); Winston, "Operations Research: Applications and Algorithms"; Google OR-Tools documentation; Holt, Modigliani, Muth & Simon lineage for production planning (1960).

## Family Q — Probabilistic

<a id="m30"></a>
### M30. Probabilistic / Bayesian modeling & uncertainty quantification

1. **Name + aliases** — Bayesian statistics/modeling; probabilistic programming (PPL); hierarchical/multilevel models; Bayesian inference; uncertainty quantification (UQ); conformal prediction (the distribution-free UQ cousin, folded here); "small-data statistics with priors".
2. **One-sentence definition** — Specify a generative probability model with explicit priors and latent structure, infer full posterior distributions over its parameters and predictions (MCMC/variational), and deliver calibrated uncertainty — the methodology of choice when data is small/hierarchical, domain knowledge is real, and the *distribution* of outcomes drives the decision.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Regression/classification with credible intervals; hierarchical estimation (many small groups: stores, SKUs, regions — partial pooling); marketing-mix modeling (MMM); A/B analysis with priors; demand/price elasticity; reliability; any decision needing P(outcome > threshold) rather than a point estimate.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular (often small-n, grouped/nested); time series (structural models — the M10 CausalImpact engine); event counts.
5. **Component pipeline** — Generative model specification (likelihood + hierarchy + priors — the domain-knowledge encoding stage) → prior predictive checks (simulate before seeing data: do priors imply sane worlds?) → inference: MCMC (NUTS) or variational approximation → convergence diagnostics (R-hat, ESS, divergences) → posterior predictive checks (does the fitted model reproduce the data's texture?) → model comparison (LOO/WAIC) → decision analysis: integrate the posterior over the business loss function → reporting: distributions, credible intervals, probabilities-of-events → updating cadence as data accrues.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Inference: Hamiltonian Monte Carlo/NUTS (Hoffman & Gelman 2014 — the modern default), ADVI/SVI for speed at scale; models: hierarchical GLMs (partial pooling — Gelman & Hill 2007), Bayesian structural time series (Scott & Varian 2014 — MMM/CausalImpact), Gaussian processes (surrogate modeling, spatial), state-space models; PPLs: Stan (the reference), PyMC, NumPyro/Pyro (JAX/torch scale), brms (R formula interface); distribution-free UQ: **conformal prediction** (Vovk et al. 1999; Angelopoulos & Bates 2023 tutorial) — wraps *any* M1/M6 model with finite-sample coverage guarantees, the lowest-cost route to honest intervals; quantile regression (native in GBMs) as the pragmatic middle.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Model-structure engineering instead: grouping/hierarchy design (what pools with what), prior elicitation from domain experts and historical meta-analyses, transformation to well-behaved scales, adstock/saturation functional forms (MMM), identifiability analysis before fitting.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Unexamined priors driving conclusions on small data (prior predictive checks; sensitivity analysis across prior choices — report it); confusing credible intervals with guarantees under model misspecification (posterior predictive checks; conformal as a model-free backstop); convergence failures silently ignored (hard gates on R-hat/divergences); label switching/non-identifiability in latent models; hierarchical leakage — using future group-level data in partial pooling for backtests (temporal discipline as M1/M5); MMM-specific: multicollinearity between channels making attribution fragile (regularizing priors, experiments to calibrate — M9 as ground truth); overfitting model complexity to one dataset (LOO comparison, held-out validation).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Calibration first-class: interval coverage on held-out data (nominal vs empirical), probabilistic scores (CRPS, log score); posterior predictive checks as the in-model gate; LOO/WAIC for model comparison; decision-quality backtests (did acting on the posterior beat acting on point estimates?); for MMM: calibration against incrementality experiments (M9/M10) — the modern standard.
10. **Explainability methods** — Parameters have meanings by construction (elasticities, channel effects, group offsets — with credible intervals); uncertainty communicated as probability statements ("72% probability the new supplier reduces defects"); hierarchical shrinkage visualizations (group estimates vs pooled); stakeholders receive distributions and decision-relevant probabilities rather than point scores — a genuinely different reporting contract.
11. **Data requirements** — Its comparative advantage is small/structured data: works from dozens of observations per group given hierarchy + priors; needs domain knowledge as a real input; conformal needs only a modest calibration set (~10²–10³).
12. **Compute profile** — CPU MCMC: seconds-to-hours (model-complexity-bound, not data-bound); NumPyro/JAX on GPU for large hierarchies; conformal: negligible.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature methods with concentrated adoption: standard in pharma/clinical statistics, actuarial work, and econometrics; MMM's post-privacy resurgence (2021–2025: Meta Robyn, Google Meridian/LightweightMMM, PyMC-Marketing) made hierarchical Bayesian modeling the default marketing-measurement methodology as user-level tracking degraded; conformal prediction moved research→early-mainstream (2021–2025) as the practical UQ wrapper; general-DS adoption remains niche relative to M1 (estimate; not source-verified).
14. **Reusable components** — Supplies the uncertainty layer for M5/M6 (probabilistic forecasts), M29 (scenario inputs), M9/M10 (Bayesian analysis engines — CausalImpact *is* a Bayesian structural model); conformal wraps M1/M4/M6 outputs; prior/posterior-checking discipline is a reusable validation pattern; hierarchical pooling logic parallels M6's cross-series learning.
15. **Representative use cases** — Marketing-mix modeling, new-product/sparse-SKU forecasting, clinical/pharma statistics, insurance reserving, elasticity estimation, small-market geo experiments, reliability engineering.
16. **Production failure modes** — Model-structure rot as the business changes (annual re-specification reviews); silent prior dominance as data windows shift; MCMC runtime creep with growing hierarchies; misread uncertainty by consumers (decision-framing templates); monitoring = coverage tracking (are 90% intervals catching 90%?), residual/PPC drift.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Stan/CmdStan, PyMC, NumPyro/Pyro, brms; PyMC-Marketing/Robyn/Meridian (MMM); MAPIE/crepes (conformal); ArviZ (diagnostics); GPyTorch/BoTorch (GPs, Bayesian optimization — which also powers M3's tuners).
18. **Key references** — Gelman et al., "Bayesian Data Analysis" (3rd ed. 2013); Gelman & Hill, "Data Analysis Using Regression and Multilevel/Hierarchical Models" (2007); Hoffman & Gelman, "The No-U-Turn Sampler" (JMLR 2014); Scott & Varian, "Predicting the Present with Bayesian Structural Time Series" (2014); Angelopoulos & Bates, "Conformal Prediction: A Gentle Introduction" (FnTML 2023); Carpenter et al., "Stan" (JSS 2017).

## Family R — Ensembling

<a id="m31"></a>
### M31. Ensembling & stacking (cross-cutting component methodology)

1. **Name + aliases** — Model ensembling; stacking/stacked generalization; blending; bagging; model averaging (incl. Bayesian model averaging); super learner; snapshot/seed ensembles; (LLM-era) model routing, self-consistency, mixture-of-agents.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Combine multiple models' predictions — by averaging, weighted blending, or training a meta-learner on out-of-fold predictions — to reduce variance and exploit error diversity, reliably buying accuracy at the cost of complexity; catalogued as a methodology because it is a *component pattern* reused inside nearly every other entry.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Any supervised problem (classification/regression/ranking/forecasting); uncertainty estimation via ensemble spread (deep ensembles); LLM output aggregation.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Inherits the base models' modality; the ensemble layer itself consumes prediction vectors.
5. **Component pipeline** — Base-model portfolio design (diversity by algorithm family, features, seeds) → **out-of-fold (OOF) prediction generation** (each base model predicts data it never trained on — the correctness heart) → meta-learner training on OOF predictions (or simple averaging/rank-averaging) → whole-ensemble evaluation on untouched holdout → serving decision: deploy the ensemble, or distill it into a single model for production simplicity → monitoring of components + combination.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Stacking (Wolpert 1992); Super Learner with oracle guarantees (van der Laan et al. 2007); bagging/random subspace (Breiman 1996); simple/weighted/rank averaging (competition workhorses); multi-layer stacks (AutoGluon's production embodiment — Erickson et al. 2020); deep ensembles for UQ (Lakshminarayanan et al., NeurIPS 2017); forecast combination — the "forecast combination puzzle": simple averages are stubbornly hard to beat (Makridakis M-competition lineage; M4/M5 top entries were combinations); LLM-era: self-consistency voting (Wang et al. 2022), model routing.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Diversity engineering: different algorithm families (GBM + linear + neural), different feature subsets/views, different objectives/seeds; meta-features (base-model confidence, disagreement) for the meta-learner; keep the meta-learner simple (regularized linear — M2 — is the default).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Stacking leakage (meta-learner trained on in-fold base predictions sees leaked target information — the classic silent inflation; safeguard: strict OOF protocol, temporal OOF for time-ordered data); holdout exhaustion (repeatedly evaluating stack variants on one holdout overfits it; nested validation or a final untouched set); diversity collapse (five tuned GBMs ensemble to one GBM's accuracy; measure error correlation); production drift asymmetry (one component degrades, the blend quietly sinks; per-component monitoring); complexity debt without measured benefit (require the ensemble to beat the best single model by a margin worth its ops cost).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Ensemble vs best-single-model delta on OOT holdout (the honest gate); error-correlation matrices of components; ablation (leave-one-model-out contribution); stability across retrains; calibration after combination (averaging can decalibrate — recalibrate).
10. **Explainability methods** — Harder by construction: component-weight reporting, SHAP on the distilled surrogate (the standard practice when reason codes are required), per-component attribution ("the text model drove this flag").
11. **Data requirements** — Enough data for OOF machinery (k-fold on the training set); nothing beyond the base models' needs.
12. **Compute profile** — Multiplies training and serving cost by the component count; distillation restores serving cost at a small accuracy tax — the common production compromise.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature and pervasive as a component: every competitive ML result of the last decade is an ensemble (Kaggle/M-competition canon); AutoGluon's default multi-layer stacking put it inside mainstream AutoML; random forests and GBMs are *themselves* ensembles — making this the most-reused pattern in the compendium even where nobody says "stacking".
14. **Reusable components** — Wraps M1/M2/M4/M6/M15/M16 base models interchangeably; OOF-prediction machinery is shared with M11 (cross-fitting in DML is the same trick for a causal reason); distillation bridges to M20; deep-ensemble UQ feeds M30's uncertainty layer; forecast combination is standard practice in M5/M6.
15. **Representative use cases** — Squeezing accuracy in high-value scoring (credit, fraud), forecast combination, competition-grade baselines to bound achievable accuracy, UQ via ensemble spread, LLM answer aggregation.
16. **Production failure modes** — Component-pipeline breakage degrading the blend silently (per-component canaries); retrain skew (components retrained on different windows); version-management sprawl (registry discipline); monitoring = per-component + ensemble metrics, disagreement drift.
17. **Tooling landscape** — scikit-learn StackingClassifier/Regressor, mlxtend, AutoGluon (industrialized stacking), vecstack; MLflow registries for component management.
18. **Key references** — Wolpert, "Stacked Generalization" (Neural Networks 1992); Breiman, "Bagging Predictors" (ML 1996); van der Laan, Polley & Hubbard, "Super Learner" (SAGMB 2007); Lakshminarayanan et al., "Simple and Scalable Predictive Uncertainty Estimation using Deep Ensembles" (NeurIPS 2017); Dietterich, "Ensemble Methods in Machine Learning" (MCS 2000).

## Family S — Label efficiency

<a id="m32"></a>
### M32. Label-efficiency methodologies (active learning, weak supervision, semi/self-supervised)

1. **Name + aliases** — Label-efficient learning; active learning (AL); weak supervision / programmatic labeling (Snorkel-style); semi-supervised learning (SSL); self-supervised pretraining (SSP); data-centric AI's labeling arm; human-in-the-loop labeling operations; LLM-assisted annotation (the 2023+ default variant).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Systematically reduce the human-labeling bottleneck by choosing *which* examples to label (active learning), generating noisy labels programmatically and denoising them (weak supervision), exploiting unlabeled data directly (semi/self-supervised), or bootstrapping labels with LLMs — a methodology about manufacturing the *training data* that every supervised entry consumes.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Meta-problem: supervised-model construction under label scarcity, for any downstream problem class (most commonly M16 text, M24 vision, M21 documents, M23 pairs).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Any; leverage is highest where unlabeled data is abundant and expert labels are expensive (documents, images, domain text, audio).
5. **Component pipeline** — Label-need audit (classes, volumes, expertise required, cost per label) → strategy selection (AL vs weak supervision vs SSL/SSP vs LLM-bootstrap — usually combined) → annotation infrastructure: guidelines, tooling, QA with inter-annotator agreement → (AL loop) train seed model → acquisition scoring (uncertainty/diversity) → human labels the selected batch → retrain, repeat → (weak-supervision loop) write labeling functions (rules, lexicons, distant supervision, LLM prompts) → label-model denoising of overlapping noisy votes → train end model on probabilistic labels → (SSP path) pretrain encoder on unlabeled corpus, fine-tune on small labeled set → convergence gate: measured label-quality and downstream-model metrics → handoff of the labeled asset to M1/M16/M24 pipelines + continuing refresh loop.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — AL: uncertainty sampling (Lewis & Gale 1994), BADGE/coreset diversity methods (2019–2020), with the sober caveat that naive uncertainty sampling often barely beats random on modern pretrained models (empirical AL literature 2021–2023 — pilot before committing); weak supervision: **Snorkel** (Ratner et al., VLDB 2017; data-programming label model), distant supervision traditions; SSL: pseudo-labeling/self-training, FixMatch (Sohn et al. 2020); SSP: SimCLR/DINOv2 (vision), masked-LM pretraining (text — the mechanism that made M16's transfer learning work); LLM-assisted: M19 prompts as labeling functions or first-pass annotators with human verification — now the default bootstrap (2023–), typically cutting cost several-fold vs from-scratch human labeling (estimate; practitioner consensus, not survey-verified).
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Guideline engineering (the annotation guideline is the label's spec — invest accordingly); labeling-function design (precision-first rules with measured coverage); acquisition-batch stratification (avoid AL tunnel vision on one region); gold-set curation (a small, pristine, human-triple-checked benchmark that everything else is measured against).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Sampling bias from AL (the labeled set is deliberately non-random — models and *evaluation* trained on it are biased; safeguard: keep a random-sample eval set, always); correlated labeling functions overweighting one signal (label-model diagnostics); LLM-labeler systematic errors replicated at scale (human audit by slice; disagreement-triggered review); guideline drift mid-project (versioned guidelines, re-adjudication); agreement theater (high κ on easy cases, chaos on hard ones — stratify agreement measurement); pseudo-label confirmation bias in SSL (confidence thresholds, calibration checks); gold-set contamination (it must never enter training).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Label quality: agreement vs the gold set (per class/slice), labeling-function precision/coverage matrices; downstream: end-model metrics vs a random-labeling-budget baseline (the honest measure of AL/weak-supervision value); learning curves (performance vs labels spent) to decide when to stop; cost accounting: labels-per-dollar and model-quality-per-dollar.
10. **Explainability methods** — Labeling-function provenance per training label (why this example is labeled X); disagreement dashboards; annotator-performance analytics; stakeholders receive a data-quality report — coverage, agreement, known blind spots — attached to every dataset release.
11. **Data requirements** — Abundant unlabeled data; access to domain experts (bounded hours — that's the point); a labeling tool and workflow; a budget model for label economics.
12. **Compute profile** — Light (loop retraining of modest models); SSP path is the exception (GPU pretraining — usually skipped by using public pretrained encoders); LLM-labeling incurs M19 per-call costs.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature components, uneven adoption: annotation operations with QA are standard in every serious NLP/CV shop; Snorkel-lineage weak supervision proved out at scale (Google Snorkel DryBell, 2019) and commercialized; self-supervised pretraining is universal *indirectly* (everyone fine-tunes pretrained encoders); LLM-assisted labeling became the de facto bootstrap after 2023; classic AL remains situationally valuable rather than default.
14. **Reusable components** — Manufactures training data for M1, M16, M20, M21, M23, M24; its human-review-loop is the same pattern as M12/M21 triage queues; LLM-bootstrap composes M19; gold-set discipline mirrors M17/M18/M19 golden-eval sets; correction loops in production (M21 §5) are this methodology running continuously.
15. **Representative use cases** — Bootstrapping ticket/document classifiers, PII/compliance labelers, defect-image datasets, entity-matching pair labels, ASR calibration transcripts, safety/moderation datasets.
16. **Production failure modes** — Label pipelines going stale as the domain drifts (scheduled re-labeling of fresh samples); annotator turnover degrading consistency (onboarding + calibration tasks); weak-supervision rule rot; the eval set aging out of representativeness (refresh with random current traffic); monitoring = agreement trends, class-distribution drift in fresh labels.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Label Studio, Prodigy, CVAT (annotation); snorkel/skweak (weak supervision); modAL/baal (AL); cleanlab (label-error detection); Argilla (LLM-era data curation); SetFit (few-shot).
18. **Key references** — Settles, "Active Learning Literature Survey" (2009); Ratner et al., "Snorkel: Rapid Training Data Creation with Weak Supervision" (VLDB 2017); Bach et al., "Snorkel DryBell" (SIGMOD 2019); Sohn et al., "FixMatch" (NeurIPS 2020); Northcutt et al., "Confident Learning" (JAIR 2021); Chen et al., "SimCLR" (ICML 2020).

## Family T — Privacy & synthetic

<a id="m33"></a>
### M33. Privacy-preserving learning & synthetic data

1. **Name + aliases** — Privacy-preserving ML (PPML); federated learning (FL); differential privacy (DP/DP-SGD); synthetic data generation (SDG); privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs); secure computation adjacent (MPC/HE — noted, rarely production-primary); anonymization's rigorous successor.
2. **One-sentence definition** — Train or share models and datasets under formal or structural privacy constraints — keeping data decentralized (federated), bounding individual influence with noise (differential privacy), or replacing real records with generated lookalikes (synthetic data) — enabling analytics/ML where raw-data movement is prohibited.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Meta-constraints applied to other methodologies' training/data-sharing stages; cross-org/cross-border model training; privacy-safe test/dev data provisioning; rare-class augmentation.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Tabular (synthetic-data mainstay), text (DP fine-tuning concerns), images (federated/medical), event streams (telemetry with local DP).
5. **Component pipeline** — Privacy-requirement formalization (threat model, regulation mapping — GDPR/HIPAA, cross-border rules; what "private enough" means, quantitatively) → technique selection (FL vs DP vs SDG vs plain governance — often the honest answer) → (FL) client orchestration, federated averaging rounds, secure aggregation, heterogeneity handling → (DP) privacy accounting (ε,δ budgets), DP-SGD training or DP statistics release → (SDG) generator fitting (copulas/GANs/diffusion/LLMs) → **utility evaluation (TSTR: train-on-synthetic-test-on-real) + privacy evaluation (membership-inference resistance, nearest-record distances)** → deployment with documented guarantees → audit + budget monitoring.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — FL: FedAvg (McMahan et al., AISTATS 2017), secure aggregation (Bonawitz et al. 2017), FedProx for heterogeneity; DP: the (ε,δ) framework (Dwork et al. 2006), DP-SGD (Abadi et al., CCS 2016), Rényi/moments accounting; SDG: CTGAN/TVAE (Xu et al., NeurIPS 2019), copula methods (SDV — Patki et al. 2016), diffusion and LLM-based tabular generators (2023–), with the essential caveat that synthetic data *without* DP provides empirical, not formal, privacy; deployed-at-scale exemplars: Google Gboard federated + DP language models (2017–2023, documented), Apple local DP telemetry (2017), US Census Bureau's DP 2020 Census release — the largest formal-DP deployment on record.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Schema/constraint specification for generators (types, ranges, referential integrity, business rules — synthetic data that violates invariants is useless); per-feature sensitivity analysis for DP budgets; client-side feature standardization in FL; rare-category handling (the privacy/utility collision point — rare values are both the most identifying and the most analytically interesting).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Privacy theater — calling non-DP synthetic data "anonymous" (memorization of outliers is real; safeguard: membership-inference and nearest-neighbor audits, DP training of generators where guarantees are claimed); utility collapse at tight ε (DP noise destroying minority-class signal; report utility-vs-ε curves, don't pick ε by vibes); FL data heterogeneity breaking convergence (non-IID-aware algorithms, per-client evaluation); evaluation leakage — tuning the generator on the holdout it's judged by; synthetic-data feedback loops (models trained on synthetic data generating future "data"; provenance tracking); fairness degradation under DP (noise hits small subgroups hardest — subgroup utility audits); regulatory over-claiming (legal review of what the math actually guarantees).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Utility: TSTR deltas vs real-data training (per task and per subgroup), statistical-fidelity suites (marginals, correlations, constraint satisfaction — SDMetrics-style); privacy: formal ε accounting where DP is used; empirical attacks (membership inference, attribute inference, singling-out) where it is not; FL: global vs per-client performance spread.
10. **Explainability methods** — Privacy-guarantee documentation (ε,δ, threat model — the artifact auditors receive); fidelity reports for synthetic datasets; per-client transparency in FL; the stakeholder deliverable is a guarantees-and-limitations statement, not a model explanation.
11. **Data requirements** — (FL) participating clients with local data and compute; (DP) larger n than non-private training for equal accuracy (the privacy tax); (SDG) enough real data to fit generators without memorizing (small datasets are the *worst* SDG candidates, opposite to intuition).
12. **Compute profile** — FL: communication-bound rounds across clients; DP-SGD: 2–10× training-cost overhead (per-example gradient clipping); SDG: modest GPU for deep generators; overall the cost is engineering + governance more than FLOPs.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Mature niches, not yet default practice: production-proven at consumer scale (Gboard, Apple, Census 2020) and in regulated data-sharing (health/finance consortia); synthetic test-data provisioning is the fastest-growing commercial slice (Mostly AI/Gretel/SDV ecosystem, 2021–2025); most enterprises still satisfy privacy via governance/access control rather than PETs (estimate; consistent with the IDC/Gartner governance literature, not survey-verified).
14. **Reusable components** — Wraps the training stages of M1/M4/M16/M20/M24 (DP-SGD is a drop-in optimizer change; FL wraps any gradient-trained model); SDG reuses M30's generative modeling and feeds M32 (augmentation) and M24/M12 (rare-event synthesis); privacy evaluation reuses M12's attack-style thinking; governance components shared with M21/M25 redaction.
15. **Representative use cases** — Cross-hospital model training, cross-border analytics under data-residency law, privacy-safe dev/test environments, fraud-consortium modeling without data pooling, telemetry collection with local DP, rare-disease/rare-fraud augmentation.
16. **Production failure modes** — Privacy-budget exhaustion over repeated releases (budget ledgers); generator drift from the real distribution (periodic re-fit + fidelity monitoring); FL client dropout/skew; downstream teams treating synthetic data as real for decisions it can't support (labeling + provenance metadata); monitoring = utility-drift on TSTR canaries, attack-resistance re-audits.
17. **Tooling landscape** — Flower, TensorFlow Federated (FL); Opacus, TensorFlow Privacy (DP-SGD); OpenDP/SmartNoise (DP releases); SDV/SDMetrics, Synthcity, CTGAN (SDG); Gretel/Mostly AI/Tonic (commercial); PySyft (research).
18. **Key references** — Dwork, McSherry, Nissim & Smith, "Calibrating Noise to Sensitivity" (TCC 2006); Abadi et al., "Deep Learning with Differential Privacy" (CCS 2016); McMahan et al., "Communication-Efficient Learning of Deep Networks from Decentralized Data" (AISTATS 2017); Xu et al., "Modeling Tabular Data using Conditional GAN" (NeurIPS 2019); Kairouz et al., "Advances and Open Problems in Federated Learning" (FnTML 2021); Stadler, Oprisanu & Troncoso, "Synthetic Data — Anonymisation Groundhog Day" (USENIX Security 2022).

## Family U — Streaming

<a id="m34"></a>
### M34. Online / streaming learning

1. **Name + aliases** — Online learning; incremental learning; streaming ML; real-time ML; concept-drift-adaptive learning; "learning one example at a time"; plus its infrastructural sibling: real-time feature computation (streaming feature pipelines).
2. **One-sentence definition** — Maintain models and features that update continuously from an event stream — learning incrementally per example or micro-batch, detecting and adapting to concept drift, and serving fresh state at millisecond latency — for decision loops where nightly batch retraining is structurally too slow.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Classification/regression under non-stationarity (fraud, abuse, intrusion); streaming anomaly detection (M12 overlap); real-time personalization state; drift detection as its own deliverable (feeding every batch methodology's monitoring).
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Event/log streams (transactions, clicks, sensor telemetry) with strict arrival-time semantics; features derived over sliding/tumbling windows.
5. **Component pipeline** — Stream ingestion (Kafka-class transport; schema contracts) → **real-time feature computation**: windowed aggregates (counts/sums/velocities per key) maintained in a low-latency store, with *event-time* semantics, watermarks, and late-data policy → online scoring at event arrival → label capture as outcomes materialize (often delayed — the central complication) → incremental model update (per-example/micro-batch) or fast scheduled retrain (the pragmatic alternative — "near-line" learning) → drift detection on inputs/outputs/errors → automated adaptation (window resets, model swaps) with human-gated fallbacks → monitoring: latency, throughput, staleness, drift, delayed-label accuracy.
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — Incremental learners: SGD-family linear models (the reliable core), **Hoeffding/Very Fast Decision Trees** (Domingos & Hulten, KDD 2000) and Adaptive Random Forests (Gomes et al. 2017); drift detectors: DDM (Gama et al. 2004), ADWIN (Bifet & Gavaldà 2007), Page–Hinkley/CUSUM; streaming anomaly: RRCF/Half-Space Trees (M12); bandits (M26) as the streaming decision layer; the honest architectural note: most production "real-time ML" is *streaming features + frequently-retrained batch models* (M1 retrained hourly/daily against a real-time feature store) rather than true per-example learning — true online learning is reserved for adversarial/high-drift domains (practitioner consensus; estimate, not survey-verified).
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Velocity/window aggregates per entity key (the same features as M12 §7, computed online); decayed counters (exponential forgetting); event-time vs processing-time discipline; feature-freshness SLAs; **online/offline feature parity** — the same definitions computed in stream and batch (the feature-store raison d'être) to kill train/serve skew.
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Event-time/processing-time confusion (late events silently corrupting windows; safeguard: watermarks, explicit late-data policy, replay tests); train/serve skew between batch-training features and streaming-serving features (parity testing, single-definition feature stores); delayed/censored labels (a transaction's fraud label arrives weeks later — naive immediate updates learn nonsense; safeguard: delayed-join label pipelines, importance-corrected updates); feedback loops amplified by speed (the model blocks what it would learn from — persistent random-allow slices, as M12/M26); poisoning/adversarial manipulation of self-updating models (update-rate limits, anomaly gates on gradients/data, human-gated adaptation); drift-detector alarm fatigue (tuned thresholds, hierarchical alarms); state corruption on replay/failover (idempotent, checkpointed stream processing).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — Prequential (test-then-train) evaluation — each example is scored before it's learned from, yielding an honest running accuracy; time-sliced metrics around known drift events (adaptation speed); delayed-label backfill evaluation (true accuracy once labels mature); shadow-mode comparison vs the batch champion before cutover; infrastructure SLOs (p99 scoring latency, feature staleness) as first-class evaluation.
10. **Explainability methods** — Same per-prediction attributions as the base model family (linear weights, tree SHAP) with the twist that explanations must be *reproducible as-of the decision time* (log feature values + model version per decision — the audit requirement in fraud); drift reports (what changed, when) as a stakeholder deliverable.
11. **Data requirements** — A real event stream with stable schemas and keys; label-capture paths with known delay distributions; volume enough to justify the infrastructure (below ~10⁴–10⁵ events/day, batch + frequent retrain is almost always the right answer — estimate).
12. **Compute profile** — CPU-bound stream processing (Flink/Kafka Streams-class) sized to throughput; models deliberately small for per-event latency; the cost center is the always-on infrastructure and its operations, not FLOPs.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — Infrastructure mature (Kafka/Flink are enterprise standards; feature stores mainstream since ~2021); *true* incremental learning adoption is narrow — concentrated in fraud/abuse/trading/adtech where drift is adversarial and fast; the streaming-features + fast-batch-retrain hybrid is the mainstream pattern (documented across fraud-platform engineering blogs; qualitative).
14. **Reusable components** — Its real-time feature computation is the serving substrate for M1, M12, M14, M26 online scoring; drift-detection components are the monitoring stage of *every* production methodology here; prequential evaluation reuses M5/M6's temporal discipline; label-delay handling shared with M8 (censoring logic); stream infrastructure shared with M9's telemetry and M25's real-time variants.
15. **Representative use cases** — Card-fraud scoring at authorization time, abuse/bot detection, real-time bidding, dynamic risk limits, sensor-based process control alerts, session-level personalization state.
16. **Production failure modes** — Upstream schema changes breaking features mid-stream (contracts + canary consumers); state-store corruption/failover gaps; drift-adaptation oscillation (over-eager resets); slow poisoning; label-pipeline lag masking degradation (staleness alarms on label joins); monitoring = end-to-end decision latency, feature staleness, prequential accuracy trends, drift-alarm audit trail.
17. **Tooling landscape** — River (the reference online-ML library), Vowpal Wabbit (online + bandits), Apache Flink/Kafka Streams/Spark Structured Streaming (compute), Feast/Tecton/Chronon (feature stores), Redis/RocksDB (state), MOA (research lineage).
18. **Key references** — Domingos & Hulten, "Mining High-Speed Data Streams" (KDD 2000); Gama et al., "A Survey on Concept Drift Adaptation" (ACM CS 2014); Bifet & Gavaldà, "Learning from Time-Changing Data with Adaptive Windowing" (SDM 2007); Gomes et al., "Adaptive Random Forests" (ML 2017); Montiel et al., "River: machine learning for streaming data in Python" (JMLR 2021); Hermann & Del Balso, "Meet Michelangelo" (Uber Eng 2017 — the feature-store lineage).

## Family V — Adaptation decisions

<a id="m35"></a>
### M35. Foundation-model adaptation decision methodology (prompt vs RAG vs fine-tune vs pretrain)

1. **Name + aliases** — FM adaptation strategy; the "prompt vs RAG vs fine-tune" decision; LLM solution-architecture selection; build-vs-adapt-vs-buy for AI capability; model-selection-and-adaptation governance.
2. **One-sentence definition** — A decision methodology (not a model pipeline): systematically choose, for a given capability need, among prompt-only (M19), retrieval-grounding (M18), fine-tuning/distillation (M20), RL-style post-training (M27), and train-from-scratch — plus the model-tier and hosting choices — by testing the cheapest sufficient rung first and escalating only on measured failure.
3. **Problem class(es)** — Methodology selection itself; capability-requirement decomposition; cost/risk/performance trade-off analysis for any generation/understanding task.
4. **Data modality(ies)** — Whatever the target task touches; the decision inputs are requirements (accuracy, latency, cost, privacy, auditability) and data inventories (corpora, examples, preferences).
5. **Component pipeline** — Requirement specification (task definition, quality bar, latency/cost envelopes, data-governance constraints, update cadence of the underlying knowledge) → golden evaluation set construction *before any architecture choice* (the methodology's core discipline) → **the escalation ladder**: (1) prompt engineering on a strong model — measure; (2) + few-shot exemplars and decomposition — measure; (3) + RAG if the failure is *knowledge* (missing/stale/private facts) — measure; (4) fine-tune if the failure is *form/behavior/latency/unit-cost* (format adherence, style, domain reasoning patterns, need for a small cheap model) — measure; (5) post-train with preferences/verifiers if the failure is *judgment/policy alignment*; (6) continued-pretraining/from-scratch only for vocabulary-alien domains or sovereignty mandates (rare — frontier-scale economics) → orthogonal choices at each rung: model tier (frontier API vs open-weights), hosting (API/VPC/on-prem), single vs routed multi-model → decision record with re-evaluation triggers (model releases, price changes, volume growth) → periodic re-run of the ladder as the frontier moves (yesterday's fine-tune motive is often today's prompt capability).
6. **Canonical algorithms and model families per stage** — The rungs are M19, M18, M20, M27 respectively (see those dossiers); the decision heuristics with evidence: knowledge problems → RAG (fresh/private/citable — and RAG dominates production at 51% vs 9% fine-tuning, Menlo Ventures 2024, because most enterprise failures are knowledge failures); behavior/format/cost problems → fine-tune/distill; both → RAG + fine-tune combined (they compose, not compete — the common misframing); "RAG vs fine-tuning" survey data: 71% of early GenAI adopters deploy RAG over fine-tuning (McKinsey-attributed compilation 2025); open-weights share of enterprise workloads 13% mid-2025, down from 19% (Menlo mid-year 2025) — the API-first default is empirically dominant.
7. **Feature engineering practices** — Requirements engineering artifacts: task rubrics, quality bars with measurable definitions, cost models (per-call × volume vs training amortization — the crossover math), data-inventory audits (what examples/corpora/preferences actually exist and are rights-cleared).
8. **Correctness pitfalls and safeguards** — Choosing architecture by fashion, not measured failure mode (fine-tuning to fix a knowledge problem bakes in stale facts — the canonical category error; RAG to fix a format problem adds latency for nothing; safeguard: diagnose failures on the golden set *by category* — knowledge vs behavior vs reasoning — before escalating); skipping the golden set ("we'll know it when we see it" — you won't); sunk-cost persistence in a fine-tune after model releases erase its edge (scheduled ladder re-runs); ignoring total cost of ownership (a cheap-per-call fine-tuned model plus its maintenance team vs a pricier API); compliance/architecture mismatches discovered late (governance constraints enter at requirement time, not deployment time); benchmark-driven model choice unvalidated on your task (your golden set outranks any leaderboard).
9. **Evaluation methodology** — One golden set, every rung: identical task metrics (M19 §9) measured per architecture candidate, plus latency and cost-per-correct-output; decision matrix scored against requirements; sensitivity to volume forecasts (the crossover point moves with usage); post-decision: quarterly re-benchmarking against the moving frontier.
10. **Explainability methods** — The decision record itself: measured evidence per rung, the failure-mode diagnosis, the chosen trade-offs — the architecture-review artifact; stakeholders receive a recommendation memo with cost curves and risk register.
11. **Data requirements** — A golden evaluation set (~100–500 examples) — mandatory; corpus inventory (for the RAG rung), example/preference inventory (for tuning rungs), volume forecasts.
12. **Compute profile** — Evaluation-scale inference across candidates (modest); the methodology *saves* compute by preventing unnecessary training.
13. **Maturity and adoption** — The decision itself is universal (every GenAI project makes it, explicitly or by default); the *disciplined* version — golden-set-first, ladder-based, re-evaluated — is emerging best practice codified in provider and practitioner guidance (2023–2025); the market's revealed preference validates the ladder's ordering: prompting and RAG dominate production, fine-tuning is the 9% minority tier, from-scratch is vanishingly rare outside model vendors (Menlo 2024; Deloitte 2024: 20–25% use customized LLMs in some form).
14. **Reusable components** — Orchestrates M18/M19/M20/M27/M28 as its options; its golden-set + failure-taxonomy machinery is shared with all of Family I; the decision-record pattern generalizes to *any* methodology selection in this compendium (the S2 selection guide is its tabular cousin).
15. **Representative use cases** — Any new LLM capability: support assistant architecture, document-processing modernization (M21 vs M19 trade), code-assistant rollout, domain-expert copilot, replacing a legacy M16 stack.
16. **Production failure modes** — Decision rot as the frontier moves (quarterly re-runs); architecture sprawl (every team choosing differently without shared evaluation — platform-level golden-set registries); vendor-dependency surprises (deprecations, price shifts — multi-vendor abstraction where risk warrants).
17. **Tooling landscape** — Evaluation platforms (promptfoo, Braintrust, LangSmith), cost calculators, model gateways/routers (LiteLLM-class), architecture-decision-record practice.
18. **Key references** — Menlo Ventures, "The State of Generative AI in the Enterprise" (2024, 2025); Deloitte, "State of Generative AI in the Enterprise" (Q4 2024); provider adaptation guides (Anthropic/OpenAI, 2023–2025); Bommasani et al., "On the Opportunities and Risks of Foundation Models" (2021); Zhao et al., "A Survey of Large Language Models" (2023).

**Evidencing (and correcting) the tabular-dominance claim.** The vocabulary asserts: *the tabular methodology dominates because tabular data constitutes the majority of business data.* The evidence supports the conclusion but demands a correction to the premise:

- **The premise as stated is false by volume.** IDC's Global DataSphere work estimates ~90% of data generated by organizations in 2022 was unstructured (IDC, "Untapped Value: Unstructured Data", 2022), with the 2024–2028 forecast putting unstructured+semistructured at ~93% of 2023 generation; Gartner-attributed figures put 80–90% of *new* enterprise data as unstructured, growing ~3× faster than structured (Research World 2025). By bytes, business data is mostly *not* tabular.
- **The premise is true by decision workload and investment.** IDC (2022) finds only ~40% of data-technology spend targets unstructured data — 60% goes to structured projects despite their ~10% volume share. The ML literature is unanimous in the same direction: "tabular data dominate the landscape of data science" (enterprise-tabular studies 2024–25); "structured tables are the dominant working data format across machine learning practice" and the medium of record in healthcare, finance, manufacturing, and compliance (van Breugel & van der Schaar 2024 position-paper line; Borisov et al. 2022; Shwartz-Ziv & Armon 2022). Consequential, regulated, operational decisions — credit, fraud, pricing, risk, maintenance — are overwhelmingly tabular prediction problems.
- **The methodology-level claim then follows from two supported facts**: (a) enterprise ML workloads are predominantly tabular (above), and (b) GBM pipelines are the strongest and most-used method on tabular data (Kaggle survey framework rankings 2021–22; Grinsztajn et al. 2022 benchmark; all top-5 M5 solutions LightGBM, Makridakis et al. 2022). Hence M1 is the most common enterprise methodology — **because tabular data is the majority of business *decision* data, not the majority of business *bytes***. The corrected phrasing matters: the byte-majority (documents, communications, media) is exactly the substrate the Family I/J methodologies are now industrializing, which is why the GenAI layer is the fastest-growing tier without displacing the tabular core.
- **One further honesty note:** production-rate statistics often cited alongside prevalence claims are of mixed quality — "87% of DS projects never reach production" (VentureBeat 2019) traces to no transparent survey and should be treated as apocryphal; the defensible figures are Gartner's ~48–54% of AI projects reaching production (~2020–21 surveys) and Rexer Analytics 2023's 32% pilot→production rate.

## S5. Correctness-discipline summary — the shared safeguards

The same small set of correctness disciplines recurs across all 35 dossiers. Collected once, they are close to a complete definition of what the vocabulary calls making a methodology "correct-by-construction for the situation":

1. **Point-in-time discipline (the master rule).** Every feature, join, adjustment, and statistic must be computable from data available at the moment the prediction/decision would have been made. Instances: M1's snapshot architecture and as-of joins; M5/M6's rolling-origin evaluation and train-window-only transforms; M8's entry-date features; M11's pre-treatment-only covariates; M14/M15's impression-time feature logging; M22's time-sliced graph snapshots; M31's temporal OOF; M34's event-time watermarks. Leakage is the same bug in every costume.
2. **Target leakage audits.** Features that encode the outcome (post-outcome fields, label proxies, target-encoded categoricals fit on full data, normalization statistics from the test period). Standard tests: per-feature "exists at scoring time?" review; implausibly-good-metric alarms; SHAP inspection of suspiciously dominant features (works even inside AutoML, M3).
3. **Split integrity: temporal + grouped + deduplicated.** Random splits are wrong whenever time, entities, or near-duplicates connect rows: temporal OOT sets (M1–M8), grouped splits by entity/query/part/session (M1, M15, M16, M24), near-duplicate removal before splitting (M16, M24), inductive node/edge holdouts (M22), leave-last-out for interactions (M14).
4. **Label-definition rigor.** Labels are designed, not found: written label specs (M1), censoring correctness (M8, M34's delayed labels), annotation guidelines + inter-annotator agreement + gold sets (M16, M21, M24, M32), reward specification with anti-gaming review (M26, M27), golden evaluation sets built *before* architecture choices (M17–M20, M28, M35).
5. **Train/serve parity.** One definition of every feature/transform across training and serving: feature stores and shared transformation code (M1, M34), imaging-rig consistency (M24), covariate availability parity (M6), embedding-version stamping (M17), prompt/model version pinning with regression CI (M19, M20, M28).
6. **Baseline supremacy checks.** No methodology ships without beating its cheap baseline honestly: base-rate/naive classifiers (M1), seasonal-naive and ETS (M5–M7 — only 7.5% of M5 teams beat ES_bu), BM25 (M17), heuristic link predictors (M22), random targeting (M11), the best single model (M31), the prompted-frontier baseline (M20), human-inspector agreement (M24).
7. **Causal ground truth via randomization; identification honesty otherwise.** Predictive metrics never justify causal claims: uplift and policy changes validate through experiments (M9 as the evaluation-of-record for M11, M14, M15, M26, M27); observational estimates carry falsification batteries and stated assumptions (M10); persistent randomized holdouts defend measurement against the deployed system's own feedback (M1 §16, M11, M12, M26, M34).
8. **Feedback-loop containment.** Deployed models corrupt their own future training data (treated customers, blocked fraud, shown recommendations, bandit allocations): exploration quotas and random slices, propensity logging as a schema requirement (M26 — enabling all off-policy work), investigation-sampling below thresholds (M12), treatment-log retention (M11).
9. **Evaluation beyond the aggregate.** Slice/segment/subgroup breakdowns (M1's cohorts, M15's query segments, M16/M19's per-class F1, M24's per-line/shift, M25's stratified WER, M33's subgroup utility under DP); calibration as a first-class property (M1, M2, M30's coverage tracking); uncertainty reported where decisions consume it (M6 quantiles, M30, conformal wrappers).
10. **Entity-resolution and data-quality precedence.** Entity errors poison everything downstream silently (M23 as the invisible bound on M1/M8/M11/M13/M14/M22); schema/distribution assertions at pipeline boundaries (Great-Expectations-style, M1 §5); master-data validation in M21/M29.
11. **Drift monitoring + retraining governance.** Input/score/performance drift monitors with delayed-label dashboards (M1's PSI/KS pattern reused everywhere), domain-specific canaries (golden-set recall for M17/M18, WER canaries for M25, image statistics for M24, structural stats for M22, coverage for M30), champion/challenger retrains, and versioned model registries; M34 owns the streaming-grade version of all of it.
12. **Human-review economics.** Wherever automation is partial, the review loop is designed, measured, and fed back: confidence-routed queues (M12, M21, M23), calibration sampling (M19's judges, M25's QA), approval gates on irreversible actions (M28) — with alert-budget matching to actual human capacity so precision decay doesn't silently kill the system.

The reuse claim, restated as a conclusion: **items 1–12 are methodology-independent.** A platform that enforces them as *infrastructure* — point-in-time joins, split-aware evaluation harnesses, propensity-stamped decision logs, drift monitors, golden-set registries, review-queue primitives — makes every methodology in this compendium safer simultaneously, which is precisely the leverage the vocabulary's "few methodologies, many solutions" asymmetry predicts.
